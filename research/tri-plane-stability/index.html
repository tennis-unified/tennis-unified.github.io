Three points, four contacts, and what the constraint costs

A coaching framework built on a three-legged stool, tested against the literature, the geometry, and the drills that carry it into a player's week.

17,300 words 10 parts 27 sources Grades A–X 30 October 2026

A three-legged stool never wobbles. A four-legged chair does. From that observation a coaching framework was built and taught: three points define a plane, the fourth creates instability unless it lies in the same plane; therefore load one leg, define the swing plane with two hands, and tuck one hand close to the body.

The observation is good. The geometry, as far as it goes, is correct. What the framework does is subtler and more interesting than it claims: it fuses three different physics questions into a single phrase, and when they are separated, they do not all point the same way.

This page is the short version of a dossier that takes the framework seriously enough to test it. It does three things the original document did not: it separates the questions, it corrects the geometry at its weakest joint, and it grades every load-bearing claim — including the ones that turn out to be coaching doctrine wearing the clothes of measurement.

The claim, as stated

Five principles, in the framework's own words:

PrincipleClaim
1. Single-fulcrum loadingOne leg must be fully loaded at a time. This creates a single clean vertical axis through that leg's ground reaction force; 50/50 loading creates two competing axes and you get stuck in the middle.
2. Wide-base compensationIf two legs are loaded, they must be wide. A narrow base is a small support polygon; gravity escapes it.
3. Two hands define a planeThe racket path is a disc, not a line. Two hands set the tilt, height and direction of that disc before the swing.
4. The proximal tuckPull one mass close to the axis. L = Iω: lower moment of inertia means higher angular velocity. The tossing arm tucks; the off-hand pulls to the chest.
5. The three-point checkAt contact, exactly three points work in one plane: the loaded leg, the tucked hand, the extended racket hand.

One phrase, three questions

"Three versus four points" is doing three jobs at once. Separated, they give three different answers — and in one respect they point in opposite directions.

"3 vs 4 points" is really about…More contact meansVerdict
Determinacy — geometry. Three non-collinear points fix a plane; a fourth overconstrains it unless coplanar.Irrelevant. What matters is where the swing plane is, not how many contacts exist.True
Sufficiency — statics. A larger support polygon resists a larger perturbation.More stable. More contact is strictly better here.False
Freedom — rotational dynamics. Every contact that transmits torque can constrain the axis.Less free. More contact means less rotational freedom.True — for a different reason

The framework's persuasive force came from moving between these three registers without announcing the move. The stool is a statement about determinacy. The wide base is a statement about sufficiency. The tuck is a statement about freedom. Only the first and third support "fewer contacts"; the second says the opposite, and it is the one coaches quote when they teach the ready position.

The constraint budget

Restated, the framework becomes sharper than it was:

The optimal configuration is the minimum constraint that still satisfies the task's stability demand — because every constraint beyond sufficiency is paid for in rotational freedom.

That one sentence absorbs all five principles, and it explains the studies that look like counter-evidence. Open stance produces the same racket speed as square, by a different route, because each architecture spends its constraints where they are cheapest. The two-handed backhand is not an exception to "fewer contacts"; it is a closed chain with a coplanar fourth point, which is a different design that buys rigidity where rigidity is useful.

The budget has a parameter. Call it λ — the stability demand of the task. Ready position, return of serve and a defensive slide are high-λ: they want a wide base, two legs, and they accept the rotational cost. A first serve and an attacking forehand are low-λ: they want one anchor and a free axis. The framework's error was to state a low-λ rule as a universal law, and then to teach it during high-λ tasks.

Modelled values, not measured ones. The model is a linear inverted-pendulum margin of stability with a stated leg length and a fixed foot; Appendix A of the dossier lists every assumption. The point is the shape of the relationship, which is robust: the required width scales with the lateral velocity you must arrest.

Three corrections

Correction 1 · geometry

Two hands do not define a plane

Two points define a line. A plane needs a third non-collinear point, and in a swing that point is the shoulder line — or the racket head. The framework's own axiom says so, which means the principle is one constraint short exactly where it is meant to be strongest.

Repair: "Chest and hands, one plate." Hands fix the line; the chest line fixes the tilt.

Correction 2 · mechanics

Overconstraint makes you rigid, not wobbly

A chair wobbles because its fourth leg is rigid and cannot close the gap. A body is compliant: the fourth contact closes, and the misfit is stored as preload — about 400 N at 20 mm of unevenness for a leg at 20 N/mm. You do not fall over. You lose rotation, and you arrive late.

Repair: "One leg owns the turn" — which teaches the right failure mode.

Correction 3 · the serve

The serve is not a vertical-axis spin

The figure-skater image belongs to groundstrokes. In the serve, Bahamonde's angular-momentum decomposition found the vertical component small and inconsistent; the dominant transfer is proximal-to-distal into the racket arm, and 75% of total angular momentum near impact sits in that arm. Teaching a server to "spin like a skater" points them at an axis the stroke does not use.

Repair: keep the tuck as a trunk-acceleration cue for groundstrokes, and drop the vertical-axis language from serve coaching entirely.

The evidence, graded

Every load-bearing claim in the dossier carries a grade, because the framework's persuasive force came largely from inferences that read like measurements. The grades are not decoration: they determine what a coach is allowed to say.

GradeMeaningCoach may say
AMeasured in tennis players, replicated or large-sample, directly on point"Research shows…"
BMeasured in tennis players, single study or narrow sample"One study found…"
CEstablished general biomechanics, imported to tennis by inference"The physics says…"
DCoaching doctrine or expert consensus, not measured"Most coaches teach…"
XAsserted in the source, contradicted by evidence or by its own geometryDo not say it; say the restatement instead

What the audit found, principle by principle:

PrincipleWhat the literature actually measuresGradeThe restatement
P1 Single fulcrum The legs matter, and matter more for better players: peak vertical GRF in the serve is 2.12 ± 0.36 BW in elite versus 1.68 ± 0.33 in beginners, correlating with impact height at r = 0.85. But which leg is never measured as 100%. Open and square stances produce equal racket speed; one study found single-leg loading reduces total available force. A legs
X 100%		 At maximum rotation demand, the majority of load sits under one leg — 70–85%, with the inside leg a light brace. During high-stability tasks, two legs and a wide base are correct.
P2 Wide base Widening reduces lateral sway, and lateral force is higher in defensive than attacking stances. But widening also raises the moment of inertia about the vertical axis: the knowledge base itself puts the redirection cost at 100–150 ms. A band
C mechanism		 Wide enough that the margin of stability stays positive at the task's lateral velocity — roughly 1.5–2× shoulder width. Beyond that, width buys stability and spends rotation.
P3 Two hands "Swing plane" has no standardised peer-reviewed definition; it is used in at least three non-equivalent senses. The two-hander is a closed chain with eight degrees of freedom against the one-hander's seven, with greater upper-extremity rigidity. C geometry
D mechanism		 The swing plane is the plane of the shoulder–hand–hand triangle. Hands fix the line; the chest fixes the tilt. Keep any fourth point either on that plane or out of the equation.
P4 The tuck Angular momentum near impact is 75.1% in the racket arm; the transfer is proximal-to-distal. But no tennis study shows that tucking the non-hitting arm raises trunk angular velocity, and the best controlled trial of tossing-arm timing found it changed the toss and the timing, not head or trunk kinematics. C During trunk acceleration, keep the off-arm close so it does not brake the turn; release before contact so the hitting arm can extend. The benefit is permissive, not generative. Attach no percentage.
P5 Three-point check As stated, the three named points are the loaded foot, the tucked hand and the racket hand. They do not generally lie in one plane — the foot is on the ground and the plane is in the air. C relabelled One plane (shoulder–hand–hand) plus one axis (the loaded leg), and the two must agree. That version is testable, and it becomes a four-item worksheet.

An unexpected finding. The 1,000-article knowledge base this project draws on contradicts itself on four of the five principles — including two opposite definitions of which leg is "outside" on the forehand, and a biomechanics article (ART-011) stating that loading predominantly on one leg "reduces the total force available for the stroke." The dossier lists all five in §7.6 and recommends a task-phase qualifier pass: five articles, one editorial sitting.

The practice layer New in v1.1

A wrong claim in a document is inert. A wrong claim inside a drill is repeated three hundred times a week, by a player who is trying to comply, under a coach watching for the criterion the drill names. Every drill is a claim in operational form — so v1.1 applies the same instrument to the fifteen drills in the source framework.

Each drill was read for four things: the mechanism it assumes, the criterion it scores, the errors it can detect, and — more revealingly — the errors it structurally cannot detect. Verdicts: Keep, Repair, Relabel, Retire.

#DrillVerdictWhy
1.1Flamingo Freeze — outside leg only, inside knee up, 2 s holdRepairTrains static single-support balance, not axis placement. The knee-up position deletes the inside foot's corrective authority, so the player passes by stiffening.
1.2Med-ball single-leg throwRepairThe best of the three mechanically. Only the dose is wrong: 100% demand, inside foot removed rather than braced.
1.3Serve front-leg lock — freeze on the front leg, back leg in airRelabelIt is a landing drill. The serve's drive question is not trained; foot-up versus foot-back differ by 1 m/s, inside noise.
2.1Tape-measure ready — feet at shoulder width + 50%Repair1.5× is the lower bound of the band, presented as the target. No upper bound, no cost term — so it teaches over-widening.
2.2Return wall sit — wide base, 10 s isometric holdRepairTen seconds of isometric squat is a quad-burn drill with a stance attached. The margin that matters is dynamic.
2.3Defensive slide and stick — slide, wide stop, 2 s holdKeepThe one drill that trains the mechanism the model names: margin of stability at the task's lateral velocity.
3.1Frame the disc — both hands on throat and tipRepairTwo hands on the racket fix the racket's line and face roll. Tilt is the relation between that line and the chest line, which the drill never references.

4.1Serve tuck and hold — grab your shirt, serve holding it to contactRepairThe tuck's window is the acceleration phase; it reverses before contact so the arm can extend. Holding to contact trains the opposite.
4.2Pin the shirt forehand — towel under the off-armRepairGood binary criterion, but only half of it. There are two errors — early drop and late hold — and the drill can only see the first.
4.3Figure-skater spins — arms out, spin, pull inRelabelCorrect physics for an upright spin, which is the groundstroke case. Not the serve case. Keep the drill, delete the transfer claim.
5.1Freeze call — partner checks three points are coplanarRetireGeometrically false, and a judgement no partner can make in real time. It produces agreement, not measurement.
5.2Video three-point audit — pause at contact, count the pointsRepairThe method is exactly right and the only drill that produces a record. The checklist is wrong: it omits the shoulder line and mislabels the fourth point.
5.3Stool test progression — wide, load, plane, tuck, contactRelabelSound pedagogy that should survive. The name teaches the wrong failure mode: you do not fall off a chair, you stop being able to turn on it.

Two keep, eight repair, three relabel, two retire. Two of fifteen survive unchanged. That is not a verdict on the coach who wrote them; it is what happens when a drill set is derived from five principles and two of the five turn out to be mis-stated.

Four faults that run through the whole set

F1

The 100% fault

Three drills demand complete single-leg loading. A player given an impossible instruction does not report failure; they find a way to feel compliant — usually by stiffening the ankle and hip and freezing the trunk. Fix: every load cue becomes a ratio, and the brace is scored, not tolerated.

F2

The hold-through-contact fault

Three drills keep the off-hand pinned through the hitting zone. That converts a rotation drill into a blocking drill, and it produces players who are stable, compact and slow. Fix: every tuck drill gets a release window, stated as a position.

F3

The unfalsifiable criterion fault

Two drills ask a human to see a plane in real time. Neither can fail in a way the player can dispute, so both produce the appearance of assessment — and teach that the check is a ritual. Fix: a criterion must be countable, or filmed.

F4

The one-sided scorecard

Every drill is scored on stability: did you fall, did the towel drop. Not one is scored on rotation cost. A drill set built to detect instability will report success for a player who has over-constrained into rigidity, because rigidity is stable. Fix: one extra column.

The last fault is the structural one, and it follows directly from correction 2. The framework's own thesis — that overconstraint is paid for in rotation — is nowhere in its assessment. The repair costs a stopwatch and a phone: every drill card gets a second score, time-to-redirect or shoulder-rotation range at contact or simply "did the follow-through finish."

Flamingo Freeze → the anchored hold

The 40-second film, audited

The framework now has a Vietnamese teaching film: forty seconds, four captioned segments, drawn from match footage. It is the first artefact in this project that a player will actually watch — which makes it the most consequential document in the set.

Finding 1 — the omission is correct, and should be made deliberate. The two principles the film leaves out are precisely the two that failed the audit: P3 is one geometric constraint short, and P5's three points are not coplanar. A film cannot show a plane it cannot define. What looks like an incomplete edit is the only defensible edit. Make it a rule: film what can be shown; do not film what the model cannot specify.

Finding 2 — one caption carries an X-grade claim into the most-copied medium. "100% lực qua 1 chân, tạo trục quay thẳng đứng." Both halves are graded X: the load is never 100%, and the axis is a kinematic inference that has never been measured — and for the serve it is specifically wrong. A document can carry a caveat in the next paragraph; a caption cannot. This is the highest-priority edit in the whole project, because the film will be re-shared without the dossier attached.

Finding 3 — the Principle 4 caption is the model caption. It states the mechanism, names the quantity, and attaches no number. That is exactly the epistemic status of P4: sound physics, unmeasured in tennis. It should be the template for every other caption.

Finding 4 — the Principle 2 caption will be over-applied. True inside the band, silent on the cost. A viewer will widen until it stops feeling wrong, which is past 2× shoulder width. One word fixes it: vừa đủ — wide enough, not widest.

A production standard, derived from the four findings

  1. One claim per caption. Two claims on one line is how a caveat gets lost.
  2. No absolute quantifiers — 100%, always, must, never — unless the grade is A.
  3. If the mechanism is grade C, say the mechanism and withhold the number.
  4. Show the failure mode in the same shot as the success. A film that only shows correct execution teaches recognition, not correction.
  5. Caption the criterion, not the feeling. "Hand released before contact" beats "feel the tuck."

Each is traceable to a restatement, executable, and does not require the player to believe something the evidence does not support. The first two change behaviour fastest, because they replace an impossible instruction with a ratio a player can actually hit.

What would falsify this

A model that cannot be wrong is not a model. Six hypotheses are stated with their tests in §12.2 of the dossier; three of them would change the conclusion outright.

If this were shown…Then
Tucking the non-hitting arm measurably raises trunk angular velocityP4 moves from C to A, and the framework's most-quoted mechanism becomes its best-supported one.
A single load split is optimal across tasksThe λ-framework collapses and the original universal rule is vindicated.
The rotation axis in a stroke does not pass near the loaded footThe reinterpretation of single-leg loading breaks, and with it the reason the constraint budget works.

Until then the honest position is the one the dossier takes: the framework survives, reframed. What emerges is a model with a name, a mechanism, a measurable cost function, quantitative predictions, and an explicit list of the experiments that would break it. That is more than the original document had, and it is more useful to a coach than a rule that cannot be questioned.
