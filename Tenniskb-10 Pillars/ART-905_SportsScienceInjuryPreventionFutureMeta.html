ART-905: Tennis Elbow — Pathophysiology, Prevention, and Rehabilitation

Executive Summary

Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis) is a degenerative condition of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendon affecting 40-50% of recreational tennis players at some point in their careers. Despite its name, only 5-10% of cases are actually caused by tennis — the condition results from repetitive microtrauma to the extensor tendon origin at the lateral epicondyle.

Understanding the Pathology

Tendon Anatomy

The extensor carpi radialis brevis originates from the lateral epicondyle of the humerus via a shared common extensor tendon. The ECRB is specifically responsible for wrist extension and radial deviation. It is particularly vulnerable because: - Its origin lies beneath the extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL), creating a relatively avascular zone - The tendon is compressed against the lateral epicondyle during wrist flexion and forearm pronation - Repetitive grip-and-release motions create cumulative microtrauma

The Degenerative Process

Tennis elbow is not primarily an inflammatory condition — the suffix "-itis" is misleading. Histological studies reveal: - Tendinosis: Collagen disorganization, increased ground substance, and neovascularization - Angiofibroblastic hyperplasia: Proliferation of fibroblasts and vascular granulation tissue - Mucoid degeneration: Accumulation of mucoid material between collagen fibers - Failed healing response: The tendon attempts to repair but lacks adequate blood supply and organizational signals

This means anti-inflammatory treatments (cortisone, NSAIDs) address symptoms but not the underlying pathology. Repeated cortisone injections actually weaken tendon structure and are associated with higher recurrence rates.

Tennis-Specific Risk Factors

Racket Factors

  • Head size: Oversized rackets (105+ sq. inches) reduce shock transmission
  • Stiffness rating: Higher stiffness (RA 70+) transmits more vibration to the arm
  • String tension: Higher tension = more shock (approximately 15-20% more force per 5 lbs tension increase)
  • String material: Polyester strings transmit more shock than natural gut or multifilament
  • Grip size: Too small or too large increases grip force requirements, stressing the extensors

Technique Factors

  • Late backhand contact: Forces the wrist to extend at impact, loading the ECRB
  • Excessive wrist snap on serves: Adds unnecessary extensor demand
  • Eastern backhand grip with open racket face: Increases wrist extension at contact
  • Arm-dominated forehand: Places higher stress on the extensor muscles

Training Factors

  • Rapid volume increase: More than 20% weekly increase in hitting volume
  • Inadequate recovery: Hitting multiple consecutive days without rest
  • Insufficient conditioning: Weak forearm muscles cannot absorb shock
  • Ball weight: Heavier balls (e.g., pressurized vs. pressureless) increase impact force

Prevention Protocols

Equipment Optimization

  • Use a flexible frame (RA 60-65)
  • Choose natural gut or multifilament strings at moderate tension (45-52 lbs)
  • Select the correct grip size (4-5 mm space between fingertips and palm heel)
  • Add a vibration dampener (modest effect, but may reduce perception of shock)

Technique Modifications

  • Contact the ball in front of the body on the backhand
  • Use a semi-Western or Western grip on the backhand to reduce wrist extension
  • Engage the kinetic chain to reduce arm dominance
  • Avoid excessive wrist action on serves and groundstrokes

Conditioning Protocol

  • Eccentric wrist extension exercises: 3 sets of 15, twice daily, using a light weight (1-3 lbs)
  • Grip strengthening: Stress ball squeezes, 3 sets of 30-second holds
  • Forearm stretches: Wrist flexor and extensor stretches, 3 x 30 seconds
  • Rotator cuff strengthening: Internal/external rotation with resistance band, 3 x 15

Rehabilitation Protocols

Phase 1: Acute Management (Weeks 1-2)

  • Relative rest from provocative activities
  • Ice application (10-15 minutes, 3-4x daily)
  • Counterforce brace (tennis elbow strap) during activity — reduces tendon load by 20-30%
  • Gentle range-of-motion exercises

Phase 2: Loading Phase (Weeks 3-6)

  • Begin eccentric wrist extension: 3 sets of 15 repetitions, daily
  • Sit with forearm resting on table, wrist hanging over edge
  • Use opposite hand to lift the weight into wrist extension
  • Slowly lower (5-second eccentric phase) using the affected arm
  • Introduce isometric wrist holds: 5 x 45-second holds at 70% maximum voluntary contraction

Phase 3: Strengthening Phase (Weeks 6-10)

  • Progress eccentric load (increase weight by 1-2 lbs as tolerated)
  • Add concentric wrist extension: 3 x 15
  • Include pronation/supination exercises with a hammer or weighted bar
  • Begin grip strengthening: 3 x 15 repetitions with progressive resistance

Phase 4: Return to Tennis (Weeks 10-16)

  • Start with basket feeding at 50% intensity, 20-30 balls per session
  • Gradually increase volume (no more than 20% per week)
  • Monitor symptoms: pain during play > 3/10 on VAS scale warrants volume reduction
  • Ensure technique modifications are in place before full-intensity play

Evidence-Based Adjunctive Treatments

Treatment Evidence Level Recommendation
Eccentric exercise program Strong First-line treatment
Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection Moderate Consider for chronic cases (>6 months)
Extracorporeal shockwave therapy Moderate Effective for refractory cases
Cortisone injection Weak (harmful long-term) Not recommended beyond single use
NSAIDs Weak (symptom only) Short-term pain relief only
Counterforce brace Moderate Useful as adjunct during activity
Acupuncture Insufficient Not supported by evidence

Practical Application: "Load the Tendon Wisely"

Tendons respond to load — they need progressive stress to adapt and strengthen. If you've never experienced tennis elbow, begin a preventive eccentric loading program: hold a 2-pound weight, rest your forearm on a table with your wrist hanging over the edge, use your other hand to lift your hand into extension, then slowly lower it over 5 seconds. Three sets of 15 daily is enough to condition the ECRB against future overload. Prevention is infinitely easier than rehabilitation.
