ART-0119: Neuromuscular Reflex Priming — Utilizing the Myotatic Reflex in Movement Preparation

Executive Summary

The myotatic reflex is not a fixed trait — it is a trainable neuromuscular pathway that can be strengthened through specific exercise modalities. Drop jumps, pogo series, and contrast pairings (alternating heavy strength work with explosive plyometrics) all increase the sensitivity of the muscle spindle, shorten the reflex latency, and amplify the reflex-driven force contribution to the first step. After 4-8 weeks of targeted training, players typically show a 15-25% improvement in first-step speed on standardized court tests. This article provides a complete training system for reflex priming — exercise selection, dosage, progression, safety protocols, and carryover assessment.

The Trainable Reflex

The conventional view is that reflexes are involuntary and therefore untrainable. This is only partially true. While the basic monosynaptic pathway cannot be shortened (the neural transmission time of 30-50 ms is a physiological constant), three aspects of the reflex are modifiable:

  1. Muscle spindle sensitivity. Repeated exposure to rapid lengthening increases the number and responsiveness of intrafusal fibers within the spindle. More sensitive spindles fire at lower thresholds and higher rates, producing a stronger reflex signal for the same stretch velocity.

  2. Synaptic efficacy. The synapse between the spindle afferent and the motor neuron in the spinal cord becomes more efficient with repeated activation. This means the reflex signal reaches the muscle faster and with greater amplitude after training.

  3. Golgi tendon organ threshold. The GTO is the reflex's brake — it inhibits the contraction when tendon force gets too high, protecting against injury. Training raises this threshold, allowing the player to produce more force before the protective inhibition kicks in. This is the most important adaptation for explosive movement: a higher GTO threshold means more usable reflex force.

Exercise Modalities for Reflex Priming

1. Drop Jumps

Purpose: Train the stretch reflex during the landing-to-rebound transition.

Execution. The player steps off a box (20-40 cm high), lands on both feet with ball-of-foot contact, and immediately rebounds vertically as high as possible. The goal is to minimize ground-contact time while maximizing rebound height.

Key variables: - Box height. Start at 20 cm, progress to 30, 40, and eventually 50 cm. Higher boxes produce faster stretches and stronger reflexes — but also higher injury risk. Never exceed 50 cm for tennis players. - Ground-contact time. Elite players achieve under 200 ms. Recreational players start at 300+ ms and should aim to reduce to below 250 ms before increasing box height. - Sets and reps. 3-4 sets of 4-6 reps, with 60-90 seconds rest between sets. Drop jumps are highly neurologically demanding — volume must be controlled.

2. Pogo Series

Purpose: Train the ankle-dominant stretch reflex in a low-amplitude, high-repetition context.

Execution. The player performs continuous vertical hops with minimal knee flexion — bouncing primarily from the ankle and calf complex. The knees stay nearly locked; the movement is driven by the Achilles tendon and calf muscles.

Progression: - Level 1: 20 contacts at low height (3-5 cm), focusing on minimal ground contact time. - Level 2: 20 contacts at moderate height (5-8 cm), with a tempo constraint (metronome at 200 BPM). - Level 3: 20 contacts with a weighted vest (2-4 kg), maintaining the same ground-contact times. - Level 4: Single-leg pogos, 10 contacts per leg, with the same tempo constraints.

Sets and reps: 3-5 sets per level, with 45-60 seconds rest between sets.

3. Contrast Pairs

Purpose: Use post-activation potentiation (PAP) to temporarily increase motor neuron excitability before explosive movements.

Execution. The player performs a heavy strength exercise (85-90% of 1 RM) followed, after 15-30 seconds rest, by an explosive movement that uses the same muscle groups. The heavy load "potentiates" the nervous system — motor neurons become more excitable, and the reflex contribution to the subsequent explosive effort is amplified.

Tennis-specific contrast pairs: - Heavy squat (85% 1 RM, 3 reps) + maximal vertical jump (3 reps). Potentiates the quadriceps and gluteals for vertical push-off. - Heavy Romanian deadlift (85% 1 RM, 3 reps) + broad jump (3 reps). Potentiates the hip extensors for horizontal push-off. - Heavy calf raise (85% 1 RM, 5 reps) + pogo series (10 contacts). Potentiates the calf-Achilles complex for ankle-dominant reflex push-off.

Sets and reps: 3-4 contrast pairs per session, with 15-30 seconds rest between the heavy load and the explosive movement. The potentiation window is narrow — longer rest periods eliminate the effect.

4. Depth Landings with Directional Push-Off

Purpose: Bridge the gap between vertical reflex training and horizontal court movement.

Execution. The player steps off a 30 cm box, lands on both feet, and immediately pushes off laterally (not vertically) toward a target 2-3 m to the side. This trains the reflex to contribute to horizontal force production, which is the specific demand of tennis first-step mechanics.

Sets and reps: 3 sets of 5 landings to each side, with 60 seconds rest between sets.

Dosage and Periodization

Off-Season (8-12 weeks)

Focus on building the reflex pathway from a low baseline:

  • Weeks 1-3: Pogo series (Level 1-2) + drop jumps from 20 cm box. 3 sessions per week.
  • Weeks 4-6: Pogo series (Level 2-3) + drop jumps from 30 cm box + contrast pairs (squat-to-jump). 3 sessions per week.
  • Weeks 7-10: Pogo series (Level 3-4) + drop jumps from 40 cm box + all three contrast pairs. 3-4 sessions per week.
  • Weeks 11-12: Maintenance volume (2 sessions per week) at peak intensity.

Pre-Season (4-6 weeks)

Transition from general reflex training to tennis-specific application:

  • Weeks 1-2: Reduce drop jump volume by 50%; increase depth-landing-with-lateral-push drills.
  • Weeks 3-4: Integrate reflex drills into on-court movement sequences (e.g., split step + lateral push after landing).
  • Weeks 5-6: Reduce volume by another 50%; maintain intensity. The reflex pathway is now established and needs only occasional reinforcement.

In-Season (maintenance)

  • 1-2 sessions per week of low-volume reflex work (2 sets of drop jumps from 30 cm, 2 sets of pogo series at Level 2).
  • Focus on quality over quantity — the in-season goal is to maintain the off-season gains, not to build new capacity.

Safety Protocols

  1. Surface. Always perform reflex drills on a surface with good traction and moderate hardness — a sprung hardwood floor or high-quality artificial turf. Concrete is unacceptable; overly soft surfaces (sand, thick matting) reduce the stretch velocity and train the wrong adaptation.

  2. Volume cap. Never exceed 40 ground contacts per session during the off-season and 20 during the pre-season. The stretch reflex is neurologically expensive — excessive volume degrades the quality of each contact and increases injury risk.

  3. Pain monitoring. Any sharp pain in the Achilles, patellar tendon, or knee joint during reflex drills is an immediate stop signal. Dull muscle soreness is acceptable; tendon or joint pain is not.

  4. Progression gate. Never increase box height or add weight until the player has achieved the target ground-contact time at the current level for three consecutive sessions. Skipping steps is the primary mechanism for overuse injuries.

  5. Warm-up. Always precede reflex drills with 10-15 minutes of progressive movement — jogging, dynamic stretching, and submaximal hops. The stretch reflex is most trainable (and safest) when the neuromuscular system is already activated.

Assessing Carryover to the First Step

The ultimate test of reflex training is not vertical jump height or ground-contact time — it is first-step speed on the court. Measure carryover with:

  1. The lateral first-step test. The player stands at the baseline center mark. A coach 5 m to the side drops a ball from shoulder height; the player must push off and catch the ball before it bounces twice. Time from ball drop to first foot displacement (measured by video or pressure mat) is the metric. Target: under 200 ms from stimulus to first movement.

  2. The reactive court sprint. The player stands at the center mark, coach calls a direction, player sprints 3 m to the side and touches a cone. Time from call to cone touch is the metric. Compare pre-training and post-training times after a 4-8 week reflex protocol. Typical improvement: 15-25% (roughly 100-200 ms faster).

Practical Application: "Train the Spring, Not the Muscle"

Implement a 6-week reflex priming block:

  1. Week 1-2: Foundation. Pogo series (Level 1-2) + drop jumps from 20 cm. 3 sessions per week, 3 sets of 5 reps per exercise. Focus on ground-contact time, not height.

  2. Week 3-4: Build. Pogo series (Level 2-3) + drop jumps from 30 cm + contrast pairs (squat-to-jump, calf-to-pogo). 3 sessions per week, 3 sets of 4 reps per exercise. Introduce directional push-offs.

  3. Week 5-6: Transfer. Pogo series (Level 3) + drop jumps from 30-40 cm + depth-landing-with-lateral-push. 2 sessions per week, 2-3 sets of 4 reps. Integrate with on-court movement drills.

  4. Measure. Before and after the 6-week block, run the lateral first-step test and reactive court sprint. If first-step time has improved by 15% or more, the reflex priming has worked. If not, reassess landing quality and exercise technique before repeating the block.

The goal is not to become a better jumper — it is to become a faster first stepper. The stretch reflex is the bridge between landing and launch. Train the bridge.
