ART-374: The Low Forehand Attack — Putting the Ball into the Court

Executive Summary

The low forehand is used to handle balls that bounce below knee height, a common challenge on grass courts, fast hard courts, and against slice shots. Attacking low balls is difficult because the player must lower their body significantly while maintaining the steep low-to-high swing path needed for topspin. Elite players develop specific techniques for attacking low balls, using deep knee flexion, an exaggerated low-to-high swing path, and precise contact point control to generate depth and spin from a disadvantaged position.

The Challenge of Low-Bouncing Balls

Low-bouncing balls present three challenges:

  1. Deep knee flexion required: The player must bend their knees to 100 degrees or more to lower their body to the ball's level, requiring significant quadriceps strength.

  2. Exaggerated swing path: The low contact point requires a very steep low-to-high swing path (35 to 45 degrees above horizontal) to clear the net, generating heavy topspin but limiting forward drive.

  3. Reduced power: The deep knee flexion and exaggerated swing path reduce the kinetic chain's efficiency, limiting the maximum power that can be generated.

The Mechanics of the Low Forehand

The low forehand uses modified mechanics:

  1. Deep knee flexion: The knees flex to 100 to 120 degrees, lowering the body to the ball's level.

  2. The backswing is lower than standard, with the racquet head starting well below the contact point.

  3. The swing path is steep (35 to 45 degrees above horizontal), with the racquet brushing upward through contact.

  4. The contact point is further in front of the body (0.5 to 0.8m) to allow the racquet time to travel upward before reaching the net.

  5. The follow-through continues high and forward, with the racquet finishing well above the head.

Techniques for Attacking Low Balls

Three techniques are used for attacking low-bouncing balls:

  1. The "drive" technique: The player uses a deep knee flexion and steep swing path to generate heavy topspin and depth. This is the standard technique for low balls.

  2. The "chip-and-charge" technique: The player hits a low, flat approach shot and follows it to the net. This is effective on grass courts where the ball stays low after the bounce.

  3. The "slice" technique: The player uses a high-to-low swing path to hit a slice that stays low after the bounce. This is effective for neutralizing the opponent's attack.

Training the Low Forehand

Training involves:

  1. Low-ball feeding drills: Have a partner feed balls that bounce below knee height, practicing each of the three techniques.

  2. Quadriceps strengthening: Strengthen the quadriceps to handle the deep knee flexion required for low balls.

  3. Flexibility training: Improve hip and ankle flexibility to allow deeper knee flexion without losing balance.

Practical Application: "The Low Ball Drill"

Practice the low forehand by having a partner feed slice shots that bounce below knee height. Focus on the deep knee flexion, the steep low-to-high swing path, and the feeling of brushing upward through the ball. Start with slow feeds and progress to faster, more realistic situations. Develop the quadriceps strength and flexibility needed to handle low balls consistently.
