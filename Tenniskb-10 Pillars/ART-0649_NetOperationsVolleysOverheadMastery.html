ART-649: The Overhead Smash in Doubles — Finishing Points as a Team

Executive Summary

The overhead smash in doubles is both an individual technique and a team tactic. When one partner hits an overhead, the other partner must position themselves to cover the court and finish the point if the overhead is returned. The overhead smash is more common in doubles than in singles because lobs are a standard defensive tactic against net players. Mastering the overhead as a team — including communication, positioning, and follow-up — is essential for doubles success.

Who Takes the Overhead?

In doubles, both partners must be able to hit overheads. The partner closest to the lob typically takes the overhead, but communication is essential: call "mine" or "yours" to avoid confusion. If both players can reach the overhead, the partner with the stronger overhead (usually the forehand player) should take it. The other partner must immediately position themselves for the follow-up shot.

Positioning After the Overhead

After one partner hits an overhead, the other partner should: (1) move to cover the open court (the area most likely to be targeted if the overhead is returned), (2) stay alert for a weak return that can be volleyed away, (3) be ready to retreat for another lob if the overhead is returned deep. The covering partner's positioning depends on the overhead's direction: if the overhead is hit down-the-line, cover the cross-court; if cross-court, cover down-the-line.

The Overhead-Putaway Sequence

The ideal doubles overhead sequence: (1) one partner hits the overhead deep to a corner, (2) the covering partner closes to the net, (3) the opponent hits a weak return, (4) the covering partner volleys the ball away for the winner. This sequence — overhead, close, volley — is the bread-and-butter of doubles net play. Both partners must be in sync for the sequence to work.

Covering the Lob When Your Partner Is at the Net

When your partner is hitting an overhead and you are covering: (1) watch the opponent's racket face to anticipate the return direction, (2) position yourself to cover the most likely return (cross-court is the default), (3) be ready to move in any direction, (4) if the overhead is weak and the opponent can hit an aggressive return, retreat to the baseline to reset the point. The covering partner must be both aggressive (closing for weak returns) and defensive (retreating for strong returns).

Communication on Overheads

Clear communication is essential: (1) call "mine" or "yours" before the overhead to avoid confusion, (2) call "lob" if you see the opponent preparing to lob, (3) call "switch" if you need to switch sides after the overhead. Practice communication until it becomes automatic — confusion on overheads leads to both players going for the same ball or neither player taking it.

Common Overhead Errors in Doubles

Both players going for the same overhead (collision, confusion). Neither player taking the overhead (missed opportunity). The covering partner not closing to the net (missed put-away). The covering partner not retreating for a strong return (caught out of position). Poor communication ("mine/yours" not called). Not following the overhead to the net (stranded in mid-court).

Tactical Application

Use the overhead smash in doubles to: (1) finish points when the opponent lobs short, (2) exploit weak lobs, (3) pressure opponents into hitting defensive lobs, (4) create opportunities for the covering partner to volley winners. The overhead is the most common point-ending shot in doubles — practice it extensively.

The Overhead from the Service Line in Doubles

When the lob lands at the service line, the net player hits a compact overhead with less follow-through. The covering partner must close to the net immediately because the overhead is likely to produce a weak return. This service-line overhead is the most common overhead situation in doubles — drill it until it is automatic.

Summary

The overhead smash in doubles is a team effort: one partner hits the overhead, the other covers the court and finishes the point. Clear communication, smart positioning, and reliable overhead technique are the foundations of successful doubles overhead play. Practice the overhead-putaway sequence as a team until it becomes second nature.

Practical Application: "Smash and Cover"

When your partner hits an overhead, your job is to cover. Move to the most likely return direction, close to the net, and be ready to volley the weak return. The overhead is only half the point — the cover is the other half.
