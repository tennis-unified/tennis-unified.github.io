ART-0199: Footwork Efficiency Scorecard — Quantifying Energy Loss in Movement Transitions

Executive Summary

Most players have no idea where their movement leaks — they feel "slow" or "always rushed" without knowing whether the loss is in the split, the first step, the transition after contact, the recovery path, or the stance itself. This article provides a five-category audit scored 1-5 from ordinary phone video, converts each defect into meters and milliseconds of quantified loss, and shows how the two lowest scores become the next training block. The entire protocol needs one phone, one tripod, and 30 minutes of review.

The Filming Protocol

  • Camera: any phone at 120 fps (most modern phones support this), tripod-mounted at the back fence, elevated 2-3 m if possible, framed to include the full baseline area.
  • Content: 10-15 minutes of live play — points, not cooperative feeds. Minimum 20-30 strokes, including wide balls and one fatigued segment filmed at the end of a session.
  • Reference markers: one cone at the standard recovery coordinate, plus the known 4.115 m between the center mark and singles sideline for pixel-to-meter calibration.

Review the footage frame-by-frame on any free slow-motion player. Score only what the camera can verify; feelings are not admissible evidence.

The Five Categories and Their Anchors

Score each category 1-5 (3 is a competent club standard, 5 is professional-grade):

1. Split timing (see ART-0101). 5 = feet touch down within roughly 0-100 ms after opponent contact on at least 8 of 10 balls. 3 = in-window on about half, late but not airborne on the rest. 1 = split frequently skipped, or landing more than 200 ms after contact.

2. First-step quality (see ART-0181). 5 = directional push begins within 50-100 ms of landing, first step traveling toward the ball, not backward or vertical. 3 = push-off delayed 150-250 ms or a false start (a step in place). 1 = visible gather or double-hop before true travel.

3. Transition smoothness (see ART-0191). 5 = zero standstill frames after contact — the follow-through flows directly into recovery; head height varies less than about 5 cm across the cycle. 3 = one brief standstill (under ~300 ms) or a small gather hop on some strokes. 1 = statue finishes on most strokes; head visibly pops up and down.

4. Recovery path (see ART-0185, ART-0188). 5 = straight or correctly arced path to the recovery coordinate, arrival within ±50 cm of the mark, re-split timed to the opponent's swing. 3 = mild banana curve or overshoot of 0.5-1 m. 1 = recovery toward the center mark by habit regardless of geometry, or arrival after the opponent's swing has begun.

5. Stance efficiency (see ART-0183). 5 = stance width and knee angles appropriate to the shot; COM stacked over the base at contact on wide and central balls alike. 3 = occasionally narrow or reaching, balance recoverable within one step. 1 = regularly hitting off-balance, weight parked on the heels.

The Loss Ledger: Converting Scores to Meters and Milliseconds

The scorecard earns its keep by translating defects into currency a player understands:

  • Every 100 ms of standstill after contact is worth roughly 30-40 cm of court at rally speeds (3-4 m/s).
  • Every unnecessary step in recovery is 0.5-0.8 m of extra travel plus its metabolic cost — and a false-start first step typically wastes 150-250 ms outright.
  • Every meter of overshoot past the recovery mark is a meter that must be traveled back, doubling the loss.
  • A mistimed split costs 100-200 ms before the first step can even begin — the single most expensive defect on the card.

Sum the observable losses for a typical point and the totals surprise most players: a 12/25 club mover is commonly giving away 1-2 m of effective court position on every groundstroke exchange — the equivalent of starting each point a full step inside the doubles alley.

Benchmarks: 12-15/25 is typical club level; 16-19 indicates strong club or collegiate movement; 20+ is professional-track. More diagnostic than the total is the profile — the two lowest categories are the training block.

From Scorecard to Training Block

Map the lowest two scores to their modules and install a focused block:

  • Split timing low → the timing calibration work of the 0101-0125 module: landing-window scoring, cue re-training, fatigue rounds.
  • First-step low → ignition training (ART-0181): directional push mechanics, loaded first-step drills.
  • Transition low → flow-through work (ART-0191): stroke-specific crossover entries, bleed steps, statue-count targets.
  • Recovery path low → coordinate training (ART-0193) and arc selection (ART-0188): marked recovery zones, overshoot scoring.
  • Stance low → alignment work (ART-0183) plus brake capacity (ART-0197): COM-over-base drills, eccentric strength.

Re-test every 4-6 weeks with the identical protocol. Expect 1-2 points of improvement per targeted category per cycle — and expect the profile to shift, which is the point: the audit tells you what to train next, not merely how good you are.

Common Review Traps

  • Scoring warm-up feeds. Cooperative balls flatter every category; score points only.
  • Scoring only the favorite stroke. The forehand may flow while the backhand transition leaks; score both wings separately.
  • Ignoring the fatigue clip. If the last two minutes look worse — they usually do — that is the true match condition and belongs in the average (see ART-0187).
  • Chasing the total. A 17 with a 2 in split timing is a worse mover next month than a 15 with no category below 3. Train the profile, not the sum.

Practical Application: "The 25-Point Mirror"

This week, run the audit once: film 12 minutes of live points plus 2 minutes at the end of a hard session, score the five categories with the anchors above, and write the profile on a card — for example, "4-2-2-3-4." That card is now the training plan: the next four weeks belong to the two lowest scores, one module each for the first and second fortnight, everything else at maintenance volume. Re-film in week five under identical conditions. Players who repeat this cycle three times typically add 4-6 points to their total — but the real output is different: they stop describing their movement as a feeling and start describing it as a set of fixable numbers.
