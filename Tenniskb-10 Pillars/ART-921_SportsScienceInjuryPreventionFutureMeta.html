ART-921: Tennis and the Female Athlete Triad — RED-S and Bone Health

Executive Summary

Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) is a serious condition that affects female tennis players who consume insufficient calories to support their training and competition demands. Formerly known as the Female Athlete Triad, RED-S encompasses a spectrum of conditions including menstrual dysfunction, low bone mineral density, and metabolic suppression. The condition is particularly prevalent in tennis due to the sport's aesthetic pressures, high energy demands, and the perception that lighter body weight confers a competitive advantage.

Understanding RED-S

Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport occurs when energy intake is insufficient to cover the energy expended through exercise plus the energy required for basic physiological functions. The resulting energy deficit triggers a cascade of hormonal and metabolic adaptations that impair multiple body systems. The condition was originally described as the Female Athlete Triad, which included three components: amenorrhea (absent menstrual periods), osteoporosis (reduced bone mineral density), and disordered eating. The expanded RED-S framework recognizes that the energy deficiency affects not only the reproductive and skeletal systems but also the cardiovascular, immune, gastrointestinal, and psychological systems.

Menstrual Dysfunction

Menstrual dysfunction in RED-S ranges from subtle changes in cycle length to complete amenorrhea. The hypothalamus reduces gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) secretion in response to energy deficiency, leading to reduced luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels. This suppresses ovarian function and estrogen production. Primary amenorrhea (failure to begin menstruation by age 15) and secondary amenorrhea (absence of three or more consecutive cycles) are both signs of RED-S. Oligomenorrhea (cycles longer than 35 days) is a milder form that may be overlooked. The loss of estrogen has profound effects on bone health, cardiovascular function, and psychological well-being.

Bone Health and Stress Fractures

Estrogen is critical for bone formation and bone remodeling. In RED-S, low estrogen levels lead to increased bone resorption and decreased bone formation, resulting in reduced bone mineral density (BMD). Tennis players with RED-S are at significantly increased risk for stress fractures, particularly in the lower extremities (tibia, metatarsals, pelvis) and lumbar spine. Research shows that female tennis players with amenorrhea have 2-4 times greater risk of stress fractures than eumenorrheic players. The loss of BMD may be partially irreversible, even after energy balance is restored, making prevention and early intervention critical.

Disordered Eating and Energy Availability

Disordered eating in tennis ranges from restrictive eating patterns to clinical eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa. The pressure to maintain a lean physique for performance and aesthetic reasons contributes to these behaviors. Energy availability (EA) is defined as dietary energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure, normalized to fat-free mass. An EA below 30 kcal per kg of fat-free mass per day is considered critically low and is associated with metabolic and reproductive dysfunction. Many female tennis players inadvertently enter a state of low EA during periods of intensified training, particularly when combined with travel, competition stress, and dietary changes.

Screening and Diagnosis

Screening for RED-S should be part of the pre-participation physical examination for all female tennis players. Key questions include: age at menarche, menstrual history (regularity, frequency, last period), history of stress fractures, and eating behaviors. The Female Athlete Triad Cumulative Risk Assessment tool provides a scoring system to determine risk level and guide management. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scanning measures bone mineral density and is recommended for players with menstrual dysfunction or a history of stress fractures. Blood tests may reveal low estrogen, low luteinizing hormone, and other hormonal abnormalities.

Treatment and Management

The cornerstone of RED-S treatment is restoring energy balance through increased caloric intake and/or reduced training load. A multidisciplinary team approach is essential, including a sports medicine physician, registered dietitian, and mental health professional. The target is to restore energy availability above 30 kcal per kg of fat-free mass per day, with many experts recommending 45 kcal per kg for optimal health. Hormonal replacement therapy may be considered in some cases, though it does not fully reverse bone loss. Gradual return to play should be guided by medical monitoring, with emphasis on nutritional rehabilitation before full training resumption.

Prevention Strategies

Prevention of RED-S requires education of players, coaches, and parents about the risks of energy deficiency and the importance of adequate nutrition for performance. Normalizing menstruation as a sign of health rather than an inconvenience is crucial. Regular monitoring of menstrual status and body composition can identify at-risk players early. Creating an environment that does not pressure players to maintain unrealistically low body weight is essential. Coaches should be educated that lighter body weight does not necessarily equate to better performance, and that adequate fueling is a key component of competitive success.

Practical Application: "Fuel to Win"

If you are a female tennis player, track your menstrual cycle and report any changes to your healthcare provider. If your cycle becomes irregular or stops, do not ignore it — this is your body signaling that something is wrong. Aim to eat enough to fuel your training, with a focus on calcium (1,300-1,500 mg per day) and vitamin D (600-1,000 IU per day) for bone health. If you are a coach or parent, create an environment where players feel supported in eating adequately and where performance is prioritized over appearance. RED-S is preventable and treatable — but only if we recognize it.
