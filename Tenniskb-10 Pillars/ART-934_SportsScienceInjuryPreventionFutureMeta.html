ART-934: Endurance Training for Tennis — Building the Engine

Executive Summary

Tennis is an intermittent sport characterized by short, high-intensity bursts of activity (3-10 seconds per rally) separated by brief recovery periods (15-25 seconds between points, 90 seconds at changeovers). The average match lasts 1.5 to 3 hours, with some exceeding 5. This unique work-rest profile demands a specific type of aerobic endurance — one that supports rapid recovery between points and sustained performance across long matches and multi-day tournament schedules. Training the aerobic engine through VO2max development, lactate threshold work, and tennis-specific interval training provides the physiological foundation that allows technical and tactical skills to be executed under fatigue.

Energy Systems in Tennis

Tennis draws on all three energy systems, but in distinct proportions:

  • Phosphocreatine (ATP-PC) system: Dominates during individual points (3-10 seconds of high-intensity work). This system provides immediate energy without oxygen but depletes within 10 seconds. It is the primary source for explosive serves, sprints, and rapid direction changes.
  • Glycolytic (anaerobic) system: Contributes during longer rallies (10-30 seconds) and when the ATP-PC system is depleted. This system breaks down glucose without oxygen, producing lactate as a byproduct. It supports repeated high-intensity efforts but accumulates metabolic byproducts that contribute to fatigue.
  • Oxidative (aerobic) system: Dominates during recovery between points, between games, and between matches. This system uses oxygen to produce ATP and is the primary system for clearing lactate, restoring phosphocreatine, and sustaining energy production over hours of play.

The critical insight is that while the phosphocreative and glycolytic systems produce the energy for actual point play, the aerobic system determines how quickly the player recovers between points. A player with a superior aerobic engine clears lactate faster, restores ATP-PC more efficiently, and maintains a higher level of play across a five-set match. This is why endurance training — which targets the aerobic system — is so important for tennis performance.

VO2max Development

VO2max — maximal oxygen consumption — represents the upper limit of the body's aerobic energy production capacity. It is expressed in milliliters of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute (mL/kg/min). Elite tennis players typically have VO2max values in the range of 55-70 mL/kg/min, well above the sedentary population average of 35-45.

Training methods to improve VO2max:

  • High-intensity interval training (HIIT): Perform 3-5 minute bouts at 90-95% of VO2max (or 90-95% of max heart rate), interspersed with 2-3 minutes of active recovery. Repeat 4-6 times. This is the most time-efficient method for improving VO2max and has been shown to produce significant gains in 6-8 weeks.
  • Continuous moderate-intensity training: 30-60 minutes at 70-80% of max heart rate. This method is effective but requires more time than HIIT. It is best used in the preparatory phase when training volume is high and intensity is moderate.
  • Mixed-methods approach: Combine 1-2 HIIT sessions per week with 1-2 longer continuous sessions. This provides both the high-intensity stimulus for VO2max improvement and the volume for mitochondrial and capillary development.

For tennis players, HIIT is the preferred method because it mimics the intermittent nature of the sport and requires less training time — an important consideration given the demands of on-court practice.

Lactate Threshold Training

The lactate threshold is the exercise intensity at which lactate begins to accumulate exponentially in the blood. It typically occurs at 80-90% of max heart rate in trained individuals. Training at or near the lactate threshold teaches the body to clear lactate more efficiently, delay the onset of metabolic fatigue, and sustain a higher work rate before the "burn" sets in.

For tennis players, lactate threshold training serves two purposes:

  1. Improving sustained performance: A player with a higher lactate threshold can maintain a higher heart rate and work rate during long rallies without crossing into the fatiguing zone. This translates to better performance in the third, fourth, and fifth sets.
  2. Enhancing lactate clearance: Training at threshold intensity upregulates the enzymes and transporters (particularly MCT1 and MCT4) that shuttle lactate from muscle to blood and from blood to other tissues for oxidation. This means the player recovers faster between points and between matches.

Threshold training protocol: Perform 2-4 intervals of 5-10 minutes at 85-90% of max heart rate (or at a pace that feels "comfortably hard"), with 2-3 minutes of easy recovery between intervals. Perform 1-2 sessions per week.

Tennis-Specific Interval Training

The most effective endurance training for tennis mimics the actual work-rest patterns of match play. A typical tennis point lasts 3-10 seconds, with 15-25 seconds of rest between points (including the 25-second shot clock in professional tennis), and 90 seconds at changeovers. A standard set includes 4-6 games, with each game containing 4-6 points.

A tennis-specific interval session might look like:

  • Repeat 30-30s: 30 seconds of high-intensity court movement (shadow swings, footwork patterns, or live rallying) followed by 30 seconds of rest. Perform 10-15 repetitions. This closely matches the work-rest ratio of individual points and games.
  • Set simulation: Play a set of tennis with the actual rest periods (25 seconds between points, 90 seconds at changeovers). Heart rate should reach 85-95% of maximum during rallies. This is the most sport-specific conditioning drill and can be performed 1-2 times per week.
  • Court sprints: Sprint from the baseline to the service line and back (approximately 10-12 meters) in 3-4 seconds, rest 15-20 seconds. Repeat 8-12 times. This trains the repeated sprint ability that tennis demands.
  • Long rally conditioning: Rally continuously for 8-12 minutes with a partner, maintaining a high intensity. This develops the aerobic base and lactate clearance needed for extended baseline exchanges.

Heart Rate Monitoring and Training Zones

Heart rate monitoring provides objective feedback on training intensity and helps ensure the athlete is training in the appropriate zone for each session. Training zones based on maximum heart rate (estimated as 220 minus age, or more accurately determined by a graded exercise test):

  • Zone 1 (50-60% max HR): Active recovery, cool-down.
  • Zone 2 (60-70% max HR): Aerobic base building, long slow distance.
  • Zone 3 (70-80% max HR): Aerobic capacity, tempo work.
  • Zone 4 (80-90% max HR): Lactate threshold, high-intensity intervals.
  • Zone 5 (90-100% max HR): VO2max development, maximal effort.

A well-designed tennis endurance program distributes training across these zones: approximately 70-80% in Zones 1-2 (aerobic base), 10-20% in Zone 3 (tempo), and 10-15% in Zones 4-5 (high intensity). This polarized distribution maximizes adaptation while minimizing the risk of overtraining.

Practical Programming

A weekly endurance program for a competitive tennis player in the preparatory phase might include:

  • Monday: On-court session (technical/tactical) — includes natural interval training
  • Tuesday: HIIT session (30-30s or court sprints) — 20-30 minutes
  • Wednesday: Threshold run or bike — 30-40 minutes at 85-90% max HR
  • Thursday: On-court session (match play)
  • Friday: Long slow distance (cycling, swimming, jogging) — 45-60 minutes at Zone 2
  • Saturday: On-court match play or set simulation
  • Sunday: Complete rest

During the in-season, reduce to 1-2 endurance sessions per week, focusing on maintaining aerobic fitness while prioritizing match play and recovery. The on-court work provides much of the sport-specific conditioning, so the off-court endurance volume can be reduced.

Practical Application: "The Five-Set Simulator"

The single best endurance drill for tennis is one that replicates the demands of a five-set match. Here is a protocol that requires only a partner and a heart rate monitor:

Play five full sets with regulation rest periods (25 seconds between points, 90 seconds at changeovers). Maintain a competitive intensity throughout — this is not a casual hit. Monitor your heart rate during each set. A well-conditioned player should see heart rate peaks in Zone 4-5 during rallies, with recovery to Zone 2-3 during changeovers. If your heart rate fails to drop during changeovers, your aerobic base needs work. If you cannot maintain rally intensity by the third set, your sport-specific conditioning needs attention.

Perform this drill once every 2-3 weeks during the in-season to benchmark your conditioning. Track your heart rate recovery (how quickly it drops during the 90-second changeover) as a key performance indicator. A player whose heart rate drops 20-30 beats per minute during a changeover has a well-developed aerobic engine. One whose heart rate stays elevated is carrying unnecessary fatigue into the next set. This is the drill that separates the pretenders from the contenders.
