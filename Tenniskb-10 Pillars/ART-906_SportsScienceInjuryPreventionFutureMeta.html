ART-906: Shoulder Injuries in Tennis — Rotator Cuff and Instability

Executive Summary

Shoulder injuries affect approximately 25-30% of competitive tennis players, with the serve and overhead smash placing the shoulder through its most extreme ranges under load. Understanding the spectrum of shoulder pathology — from rotator cuff tendinopathy to labral tears — enables players to implement effective prevention and early intervention strategies.

Shoulder Anatomy for Tennis

The Rotator Cuff Complex

Four muscles stabilize the glenohumeral joint during the serve: - Supraspinatus: Initiates abduction; serves to depress the humeral head during arm elevation - Infraspinatus: Primary external rotator; active during the cocking phase - Teres Minor: Assists external rotation; active during cocking - Subscapularis: Primary internal rotator; generates 50% of serve acceleration force

The Labrum

The glenoid labrum is a fibrocartilaginous rim that deepens the glenoid fossa by 50%, providing stability and serving as the attachment point for the long head of the biceps tendon. The superior labrum is particularly vulnerable during the serve's cocking phase.

The Capsule and Ligaments

The inferior glenohumeral ligament is the primary stabilizer against anterior translation in abduction and external rotation (the "cocked" serve position). Repetitive stretching in this position can lead to microinstability.

Common Shoulder Pathologies

1. Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy

  • Mechanism: Repetitive overhead loading causes cumulative microtrauma to the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons
  • Prevalence: Most common shoulder complaint in tennis (approximately 40% of shoulder injuries)
  • Symptoms: Pain during cocking phase, night pain, weakness in external rotation
  • Stages: Reactive tendinopathy → Tendon dysrepair → Degenerative tendinopathy

2. Internal Impingement

  • Mechanism: The undersurface of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons are compressed between the humeral head and the posterosuperior glenoid rim during the cocking phase
  • Associated findings: Partial-thickness rotator cuff tears, posterior labral fraying
  • Symptoms: Deep posterior shoulder pain at full external rotation, "dead arm" sensation
  • Key sign: Painful arc between 90-120 degrees abduction with external rotation

3. SLAP Tears (Superior Labrum Anterior to Posterior)

  • Mechanism: The "peel-back" mechanism — during the cocking phase, the long head of biceps pulls the superior labrum off the glenoid
  • Types: Type II (most common in tennis) involves detachment of the superior labrum and biceps anchor
  • Symptoms: Catching, popping, deep anterior shoulder pain, pain with overhead activities
  • Clinical tests: O'Brien's test, Speed's test, biceps load test

4. Shoulder Instability

  • Mechanism: Repetitive microtrauma to the anterior capsule leads to capsular laxity and humeral head subluxation
  • Types: TUBS (Traumatic, Unidirectional, Bankart surgery) vs. AMBRI (Atraumatic, Multidirectional, Bilateral, Rehabilitation, Inferior capsular shift)
  • Tennis relevance: Players develop acquired laxity from repetitive overhead serving
  • Symptoms: Apprehension, sense of shoulder "slipping," fatigue-related loss of control

5. Scapular Dyskinesis

  • Mechanism: Altered scapular position or motion due to muscle imbalance (typically weak lower trapezius and serratus anterior, tight pectoralis minor)
  • Consequence: Reduced subacromial space, altered glenoid positioning, inefficient force transfer
  • Signs: Prominent medial border of scapula (winging), asymmetry in scapular rotation

Risk Factors in Tennis

Serve Volume

Players who serve more than 100 times per practice session and 150+ times per match have a 2.5x greater incidence of shoulder injury over a 5-year period.

Inadequate Rest

Serving on consecutive days without at least one day of recovery prevents adequate tissue repair, leading to cumulative microtrauma.

Physical Deficiencies

  • Glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD): Loss of internal rotation >20 degrees compared to contralateral side
  • External rotation weakness: External rotation strength <65% of internal rotation strength
  • Scapular dyskinesis: Visible asymmetry in scapular motion

Technical Faults

  • Tossing too far behind the head (excessive horizontal adduction)
  • Serving with the arm alone (insufficient trunk and leg contribution)
  • Inadequate follow-through (sudden deceleration without gradual energy dissipation)

Prevention Program

Phase 1: Mobility

  • Sleeper stretch: For posterior capsule tightness, 3 x 30 seconds
  • Cross-body stretch: For posterior deltoid and infraspinatus, 3 x 30 seconds
  • Thoracic spine mobility: Foam roller extension, 10 repetitions

Phase 2: Stability

  • Scapular retraction: Prone Y-T-W-L raises, 3 x 10 each
  • Serratus punches: Wall slides with plus, 3 x 15
  • Lower trapezius activation: Prone raises with thumbs up, 3 x 15

Phase 3: Rotator Cuff Strengthening

  • External rotation: Side-lying with dumbbell, 3 x 15 (progressive load)
  • Internal rotation: Standing with cable or band, 3 x 15
  • Full can: Scaption with thumb up to 90 degrees, 3 x 15

Phase 4: Integration

  • Plyometric ball throws: Overhead soccer throw pattern, 3 x 10
  • Weighted ball overhead slams: 3 x 8, mimicking serve follow-through
  • Eccentric external rotation: Slow lowering from full external rotation, 3 x 10

Rehabilitation Protocol

Acute Phase (0-4 weeks)

  • Activity modification: Eliminate serving, limit overhead hitting
  • Pain management: Ice, anti-inflammatory modalities
  • Gentle ROM: Passive and active-assisted range of motion
  • Isometric rotator cuff activation: Submaximal, pain-free

Intermediate Phase (4-8 weeks)

  • Progressive rotator cuff strengthening
  • Scapular stabilization exercises
  • Begin serving at 50% with modified toss

Return-to-Play Phase (8-16 weeks)

  • Progressive serve volume: Start with 25 serves/session, increase 20% weekly
  • Monitor for pain: >2/10 VAS during serving warrants volume reduction
  • Full return when:
  • Pain-free serving at full intensity
  • External rotation strength >80% of contralateral side
  • Internal rotation within 15 degrees of contralateral side
  • No scapular dyskinesis visible

Diagnostic & Training Matrix

Observation Failure Result Corrective Strategy
Posterior shoulder pain during cocking Internal impingement from GIRD Rotator cuff undersurface tears Sleeper stretch 4x daily; monitor internal rotation ROM
Pain with overhead serve at full external rotation SLAP tear or rotator cuff pathology Dead arm, reduced serve velocity MR arthrogram for diagnosis; modify technique to reduce extreme external rotation
Visible scapular winging at rest Serratus anterior weakness Reduced subacromial space, impingement Wall slide with plus, serratus punch progression
Fatigue-related loss of serve control in later sets Rotator cuff endurance deficit Reduced serve speed, accuracy loss High-repetition (25+) external rotation endurance training
Toss placement behind head Excessive horizontal adduction Anterior capsule stress, labral pathology Target toss drill: practice landing toss in bucket consistently

Practical Application: "Pre-Serve Shoulder Prep"

Before every serving session, perform a 5-minute shoulder preparation: 20 arm circles (forward and backward), 10 sleeper stretches each side, 10 external rotations with a resistance band, and 10 wall slides. This routine activates the rotator cuff, mobilizes the posterior capsule, and primes the scapular stabilizers — the most efficient insurance policy against shoulder injury you can implement.
