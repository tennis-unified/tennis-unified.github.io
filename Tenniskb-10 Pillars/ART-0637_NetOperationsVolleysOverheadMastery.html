ART-637: The Forehand Volley on the Run — Wide Ball Recovery

Executive Summary

The forehand volley on the run is hit while moving laterally at the net, typically to intercept a cross-court shot that pulls you wide. It is one of the most difficult volley situations because you must maintain balance, control the racket face, and redirect pace — all while moving at speed. The key is to use a cross-step for maximum reach and to contact the ball in front of the body despite the lateral momentum.

The Challenge of the Wide Forehand Volley

Wide forehand volleys present three challenges: (1) lateral momentum carries you away from the court, making recovery difficult, (2) the ball is contacted further from the body, reducing control, (3) the split step must be executed while moving, which is harder than from a stationary position. The result is that wide forehand volleys are often frame shots or weak returns that the opponent can easily attack.

The Cross-Step for Wide Volleys

The cross-step is the primary movement for wide forehand volleys. For a right-handed player moving to the right (forehand side), the left foot crosses over the right foot, landing 12-18 inches to the right. The right foot then steps into the volley position. The cross-step covers more ground than the shuffle step and allows you to reach balls that would otherwise be unreachable. The key is to keep the hips low and the racket in front of the body during the cross.

Contact Point on Wide Volleys

On wide forehand volleys, the contact point is further from the body than on standard volleys — approximately 18-24 inches ahead of the front foot. This extended reach reduces control but is necessary to intercept the ball. The racket face must be carefully controlled because the extended arm amplifies any face-angle errors. Keep the wrist firm and the racket head above the wrist.

Balance and Recovery

After hitting a wide forehand volley, use the crossover step to recover to the center of the court. The foot nearest the direction of recovery crosses over the other foot, allowing rapid repositioning. Do not attempt a winner from a wide volley unless you are confident in your recovery — the priority is to keep the ball in play and get back into position.

Common Wide Forehand Volley Errors

Lunging with the arm instead of moving the feet — reduces reach and control. Breaking the wrist due to the extended reach — sends the ball off-target. Swinging too hard — the lateral momentum adds pace, so a gentle redirect is sufficient. Failing to recover after the shot — leaving the court open for the opponent's next shot. Not using the cross-step — the shuffle step cannot cover enough ground.

Tactical Application

Wide forehand volleys are typically hit: (1) when the opponent pulls you wide with a cross-court passing shot, (2) when poaching in doubles, (3) when covering a wide serve in doubles. The goal on wide volleys is to keep the ball in play and recover to the center — do not attempt winners unless the court is completely open. Aim deep to the open court or down-the-line for the safest options.

Wide Volley Drills

The "wide volley feed" drill has a coach feed wide balls that the player cross-steps to volley. The "wide volley recovery" drill practices volleying wide and recovering to the center. The "poaching volley" drill practices wide volleys while poaching in doubles. The "competitive wide volley" game rewards successful wide volleys and penalizes failure to recover.

Summary

The wide forehand volley on the run demands excellent lateral movement, extended reach, and quick recovery. Use the cross-step for maximum reach, keep the wrist firm despite the extended arm, and prioritize recovery over winners. With practice, wide volleys become a reliable part of the net game rather than a liability.

Diagnostic & Training Matrix

Practical Application: "Cross, Contact, Recover"

Three steps for the wide forehand volley: (1) cross-step to reach the ball, (2) contact in front with a firm wrist, (3) crossover step to recover. The shot is only one-third of the equation — the recovery is equally important.
