ART-917: Muscle Imbalances in Tennis — Asymmetrical Athletes

Executive Summary

Tennis is an inherently asymmetrical sport, with players consistently using one side of the body for serving, hitting, and bearing weight. Over time, this leads to measurable strength, mobility, and neuromuscular imbalances between the dominant and non-dominant sides. Understanding these imbalances and implementing corrective strategies is essential for injury prevention and career longevity.

Types of Asymmetry in Tennis

Upper Body Asymmetry

  • Arm circumference: Dominant arm can be 1-3 cm larger in circumference
  • Shoulder strength: Internal rotation strength 15-30% greater on dominant side
  • Shoulder mobility: External rotation 10-20 degrees greater on dominant side (acquired laxity)
  • Grip strength: 15-25% stronger on dominant side
  • Forearm size: Dominant forearm 5-10% larger

Lower Body Asymmetry

  • Leg circumference: Minimal difference (5-10 mm) if player uses both legs equally
  • Knee stability: Front leg (toward net) experiences greater valgus stress
  • Hip mobility: Internal rotation often reduced on dominant side from serving
  • Calf strength: Slight dominance on the leg used for push-off

Core Asymmetry

  • Oblique strength: Internal obliques (dominant side) and external obliques (non-dominant side) show differential development
  • Trunk rotation: Rotation toward the target is typically 10-15 degrees greater than rotation away

Consequences of Unchecked Asymmetry

Injury Risk

  • Shoulder impingement on the dominant side from excessive external rotation laxity
  • Lower back pain from rotational asymmetry
  • Knee valgus on the dominant leg during open-stance shots
  • Non-dominant side injuries from overuse during compensatory movements

Performance Limitation

  • Reduced ability to hit effectively on the non-dominant side
  • Limited shot selection due to discomfort or weakness on one side
  • Decreased court coverage due to asymmetrical movement patterns

Postural Changes

  • Forward head posture from serving motion
  • Elevated dominant shoulder
  • Trunk lean toward the dominant side
  • Pelvic obliquity

Screening for Imbalance

Upper Body Screening

  1. Grip strength test: Compare dominant and non-dominant sides; >15% difference is significant
  2. Shoulder internal rotation ROM: Compare sides; >20-degree difference suggests GIRD
  3. Shoulder external rotation ROM: Dominant side should be greater, but excessive (>90 degrees) suggests instability
  4. Single-arm plank endurance: >20-second difference between sides indicates core asymmetry

Lower Body Screening

  1. Single-leg hop distance: >10% asymmetry increases injury risk
  2. Single-leg squat quality: Valgus collapse on one side indicates weakness
  3. Hip internal rotation: >15-degree difference is significant
  4. Ankle dorsiflexion: >5-degree difference affects squat and lunge mechanics

Core Screening

  1. Side plank endurance: >20-second difference between sides
  2. Trunk rotation ROM: Measured in seated rotation test
  3. Pallof press endurance: Compare sides for anti-rotation strength

Corrective Strategies

Upper Body Correction

  • Non-dominant arm strengthening: Resistance band exercises, dumbbell work, 3 sets of 12
  • Dominator side external rotator strengthening: Side-lying external rotation, 3 sets of 15
  • Posterior capsule stretching: Sleeper stretch on dominant side, 3 sets of 30 seconds
  • Scapular stabilization: Prone Y-T-W-L raises, both sides equally

Lower Body Correction

  • Unilateral strength training: Single-leg squats, lunges, step-ups (both sides, equal volume)
  • Hip mobility: 90/90 stretches, hip flexor stretches (emphasize restricted side)
  • Plyometric symmetry: Equal volume of single-leg hops on both legs
  • Adductor/abductor balance: Copenhagen plank, clamshells

Core Correction

  • Anti-rotation training: Pallof press, both sides equally
  • Anti-lateral flexion: Suitcase carries, both sides
  • Rotational training: Medicine ball throws, equal volume both sides
  • Side plank: Match volume to weaker side

Implementation Guidelines

Pre-Practice (5 minutes)

  • Perform the restricted mobility movement for the tighter side
  • Activate the weaker muscle group with bodyweight exercises

During Practice (integrated)

  • Hit 10-20% more balls on the non-dominant side
  • Use the non-dominant hand for warm-up volleys
  • Practice serves with non-dominant arm (for fun and balance)

Post-Practice (10 minutes)

  • Stretch the dominant side (pecs, hip flexors, external rotators)
  • Strengthen the non-dominant side (light resistance, high repetitions)

Diagnostic & Training Matrix

Observation Failure Result Corrective Strategy
Grip strength difference >20% Neglected non-dominant arm training Performance limitation, injury risk Non-dominant grip exercises; wrist curls; rice bucket work
Shoulder internal rotation deficit >20 degrees (GIRD) Posterior capsule tightness Shoulder impingement, reduced serve speed Sleeper stretch; thoracic mobility; serve technique modification
Single-leg hop asymmetry >10% Unilateral strength imbalance Elevated ACL injury risk on weaker leg Unilateral strength training; equal plyometric volume

Practical Application: "Train the Other Side"

Dedicate one practice session per week to training your non-dominant side: hit groundstrokes with your non-dominant hand, perform serving motions without a ball, and do single-leg exercises starting with your weaker leg. You do not need to become ambidextrous — but you do need to reduce the imbalance gap. Even 10 minutes per week of non-dominant training reduces injury risk and improves your overall movement quality.
