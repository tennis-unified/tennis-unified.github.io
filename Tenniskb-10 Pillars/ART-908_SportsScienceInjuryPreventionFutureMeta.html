ART-908: Lower Back Pain in Tennis — Disc, Facet, and Muscle Pathology

Executive Summary

Lower back pain affects approximately 30-40% of competitive tennis players, with the serve's extreme trunk flexion-extension-rotation demands placing substantial stress on lumbar structures. Understanding the specific pathology — discogenic, facet-related, sacroiliac, or muscular — is essential for appropriate management and return-to-play decision-making.

Lumbar Spine Anatomy for Tennis

Vertebral Structure

The lumbar spine consists of five vertebrae (L1-L5) separated by intervertebral discs. The discs function as shock absorbers and allow controlled motion in flexion, extension, lateral bending, and rotation.

Disc Anatomy

  • Nucleus pulposus: Gelatinous core, 70-90% water content, bears compressive loads
  • Annulus fibrosus: Concentric layers of collagen fibers oriented at alternating 30-degree angles
  • Endplates: Cartilaginous interfaces between disc and vertebral body, allow nutrient diffusion

Muscular Support

  • Global movers: Erector spinae, rectus abdominis, external oblique
  • Local stabilizers: Multifidus, transversus abdominis, pelvic floor, diaphragm
  • Rotators: Internal oblique, multifidus, quadratus lumborum

Common Lower Back Pathologies

Disc Herniation

  • Mechanism: Repetitive flexion-rotation causes posterior-lateral annular tears, allowing nucleus pulposus to extrude
  • Most common levels: L4-L5 and L5-S1
  • Symptoms: Low back pain with radicular leg pain (sciatica), worsened by sitting, coughing, or forward flexion
  • Natural history: 90% resolve with conservative treatment within 6-12 weeks

Facet Joint Syndrome

  • Mechanism: Extension-rotation stress compresses the facet joints during serving and overheads
  • Symptoms: Unilateral pain worse with extension and rotation, relieved by flexion, referred pain to buttock/thigh
  • Diagnosis: Pain relief with diagnostic facet joint injection

Spondylolysis/Spondylolisthesis

  • Mechanism: Stress fracture of the pars interarticularis from repetitive hyperextension
  • Prevalence: Higher in adolescent athletes performing repetitive extension
  • Diagnosis: Oblique radiograph ("Scottie dog" sign), CT or SPECT scan
  • Management: Bracing for acute fractures, activity modification, core stabilization

Sacroiliac Joint Dysfunction

  • Mechanism: Asymmetric loading of the SI joint during serving
  • Symptoms: Pain over the PSIS, radiating to groin or posterior thigh
  • Provocation tests: Gaenlen's test, FABER test, distraction test
  • Treatment: Manual therapy, stabilization exercises, SI belt

Muscle Strain

  • Mechanism: Eccentric overload of erector spinae or quadratus lumborum during rapid trunk rotation
  • Symptoms: Localized pain, spasm, worse with specific movements
  • Healing: 2-6 weeks depending on severity

Tennis-Specific Risk Factors

Serve Mechanics

  • Excessive lumbar hyperextension during the cocking phase
  • Rapid extension from the fully loaded position
  • Asymmetric trunk loading (one-sided serving)

Training Factors

  • Inadequate core stability training
  • Excessive serving volume without adequate rest
  • Poor hip mobility forcing compensatory lumbar rotation

Prevention Strategies

Core Stability Program

The core stabilizers must activate before movement begins to provide segmental stability:

  1. Abdominal drawing-in maneuver: Transversus abdominis activation, 10 sets of 10-second holds
  2. Bird-dog: Alternating arm/leg extension from quadruped, 3 sets of 10 each side
  3. Dead bug: Supine alternating arm/leg with lumbar neutral, 3 sets of 10 each side
  4. Side plank: Lateral endurance, 3 sets of 30-second holds each side
  5. Pallof press: Anti-rotation standing exercise, 3 sets of 10 each side

Hip Mobility

  • Hip flexor stretching: Thomas stretch position, 3 sets of 30 seconds each side
  • Hip internal rotation: 90/90 stretch, 3 sets of 30 seconds each side
  • Hip extension: Prone hip extension with posterior pelvic tilt, 3 sets of 12

Rehabilitation Protocols

Acute Disc Herniation

  • First 48-72 hours: Relative rest, ice/heat as tolerated
  • Avoid: Prolonged sitting, forward flexion, heavy lifting
  • Begin: McKenzie extension exercises if centralizing symptoms
  • Progress: Core stabilization, nerve gliding, cardiovascular fitness
  • Return to tennis: 6-12 weeks for non-surgical cases

Facet Joint Syndrome

  • Avoid extension: Modify serve to reduce lumbar hyperextension
  • Manual therapy: Mobilization of hypomobile segments
  • Core stabilization: Emphasis on multifidus activation
  • Anti-inflammatory: Consider facet joint injection

Spondylolysis

  • Bracing: TLSO brace for 12-20 weeks
  • Activity modification: No extension activities for 6-12 weeks
  • Bone health evaluation: Rule out RED-S, vitamin D deficiency
  • Return to sport: 3-6 months with pain-free extension

Diagnostic & Training Matrix

Practical Application: "Brace Before You Rotate"

Before any rotational movement — serving, groundstrokes, or even picking up a ball — gently draw your navel toward your spine and hold that contraction. This activates the transversus abdominis, creating an internal "corset" that stabilizes each lumbar segment. Practice this 10 times daily until it becomes automatic. Your core should fire before your limbs move, not after. This single neuromuscular retraining reduces lower back injury risk more than any external support.
