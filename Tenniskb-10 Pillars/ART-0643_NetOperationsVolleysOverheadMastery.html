ART-643: The Volley Against the Serve — Return of Serve Net Rush

Executive Summary

The volley against the serve is hit when you rush the net behind a return of serve, intercepting the server's first groundstroke or half-volley. This is a high-pressure situation because the server has the advantage of dictating play, and you must volley a ball that is often hit with pace and precision. The chip-and-charge return is the most common way to execute this tactic, and mastering the volley against the serve is essential for aggressive return players.

The Chip-and-Charge Return

The chip-and-charge return is a short backspin chip hit low to the server's feet, followed by an immediate rush to the net. The chip must be low enough to prevent the server from hitting an aggressive groundstroke and short enough to force the server to hit up on the ball. The charge to the net must be rapid — hesitation leaves you stranded in no man's land. The return is typically hit with a continental grip and a short, chopping motion.

The Volley After the Chip

After chipping and charging, you will encounter the server's reply at the net. This reply is often: (1) a half-volley from the server's feet (if the chip was well-placed), (2) a weak groundstroke (if the server was forced to hit up), or (3) an aggressive passing shot (if the server had time to set up). Your volley must handle all three possibilities. Use a punch volley for weak replies, a block volley for hard passing shots, and a slice volley for low balls.

Positioning After the Chip-and-Charge

After the chip-and-charge: (1) split step at the service line as the server contacts the ball, (2) read the direction of the reply from the server's racket face, (3) move to intercept the ball at the net. The split step at the service line is critical — it allows you to move in any direction for the first volley. Do not continue running past the service line without split stepping.

Common Errors in the Volley Against the Serve

Chipping too deep — the server has time to set up an aggressive passing shot. Chipping too hard — the chip goes long or sits up for an easy groundstroke. Not split stepping at the service line — flat-footed against the server's reply. Swinging too hard on the first volley — the first volley is a setup shot, not a winner. Failing to close to the net after the first volley — leaving you stranded in no man's land.

Tactical Application

The volley against the serve is used to: (1) pressure the server by taking away their preferred groundstroke, (2) finish points at the net after forcing a weak reply, (3) disrupt the server's rhythm, (4) exploit servers who struggle with half-volleys or low balls. Use the chip-and-charge selectively — on second serves, against servers who struggle with low balls, and when you want to change the rhythm.

The Drive Return-and-Voltage

A variation is the drive return-and-charge — hitting a hard, flat return at the server's feet and rushing the net. This is more aggressive than the chip-and-charge because the drive return puts immediate pressure on the server, but it is also higher risk because a well-placed drive return can still be passed. The drive return is hit with a compact swing (similar to a drive volley) and directed at the server's feet or body.

Volley Against the Serve Drills

The "chip-and-charge volley" drill practices the full sequence: chip the return, rush the net, split step, and volley the server's reply. The "drive return-and-charge" drill practices the more aggressive variation. The "first volley placement" drill focuses on placing the first volley deep to the server's weakness. The "competitive return-and-charge" game uses the chip-and-charge in competitive situations.

Summary

The volley against the serve is a high-pressure shot that requires a well-placed chip-and-charge return, a split step at the service line, and a reliable first volley. Practice the full sequence regularly, and the chip-and-charge will become a devastating weapon that puts immediate pressure on the server.

Practical Application: "Chip Low, Rush Fast, Split Step"

The chip-and-charge sequence: (1) chip the return low to the server's feet, (2) rush the net as fast as possible, (3) split step at the service line, (4) volley the server's reply. The split step is the most commonly forgotten element — drill it until it is automatic.
