ART-975: String Technology and Performance — Polyester, Gut, and Hybrids

Executive Summary

Tennis string technology has evolved from natural materials to sophisticated synthetic composites, with polyester strings revolutionizing the modern game by enabling unprecedented spin generation. Understanding string properties — stiffness, tension maintenance, snap-back, and feel — allows players to optimize their string setup for performance, comfort, and durability.

String Materials

Natural Gut

  • Source: Serosa layer of cow or sheep intestines
  • Construction: Multiple strands twisted together
  • Properties:
  • Exceptional tension maintenance (loses tension slowest)
  • Highest elasticity (softest feel)
  • Excellent energy return (powerful)
  • Best comfort (lowest arm fatigue)
  • Drawbacks:
  • Most expensive ($20-40 per stringing)
  • Least durable (breaks easily)
  • Affected by moisture (loses tension in rain)
  • Requires careful handling
  • Best for: Players with arm injuries, those seeking maximum comfort and feel

Nylon (Synthetic Gut)

  • Construction: Single solid core (monofilament) or multiple thin filaments wound together (multifilament)
  • Monofilament: Durable, less expensive, moderate feel
  • Multifilament: Softer, more comfortable, better feel, less durable
  • Properties:
  • Good balance of durability and comfort
  • Moderate tension maintenance
  • Moderate power and control
  • Cost: $3-15 per stringing
  • Best for: Recreational players, those seeking good value

Polyester (Co-Polyester)

  • Construction: Mono-filament polyester, often with coatings or texturing
  • Properties:
  • Low elasticity (low power, high control)
  • Excellent durability (resists notching)
  • High spin potential (due to snap-back effect)
  • Stiff feel (higher arm loading)
  • Revolution: Enabled modern heavy-topspin game
  • Popular brands: Luxilon ALU Power, RPM Blast, Hawk Touch, Hyper-G
  • Cost: $5-20 per stringing
  • Best for: Advanced players with long, fast swings seeking spin and control

Kevlar

  • Construction: Aramidal fibers, extremely stiff and durable
  • Properties:
  • Most durable string available
  • Very stiff (highest arm loading)
  • Low power, high control
  • Drawbacks: Highest risk of arm injuries
  • Best for: Extreme string breakers (often in hybrid with softer crosses)

Aramid/Co-Poly Hybrids

  • Construction: Combining different string types in mains and crosses
  • Common setups:
  • Polyester mains + nylon crosses (spin with comfort)
  • Polyester mains + natural gut crosses (spin with luxury feel)
  • Kevlar mains + nylon crosses (durability with comfort)

How Strings Affect Performance

Power

  • More elastic strings (natural gut, multifilament nylon) = more power (higher coefficient of restitution)
  • Stiffer strings (polyester, Kevlar) = less power but more control
  • Lower tension = more power
  • Higher tension = more control

Spin

  • Snap-back effect: Polyester strings slide across each other at contact, then snap back, imparting additional spin
  • Textured strings: Shaped surfaces (hexagonal, octagonal) grip the ball better, increasing spin
  • Open string pattern: 16x19 allows more string movement than 18x20
  • Lower tension: Allows more ball embed and string snap-back, increasing spin
  • Longer swing path: Modern polyester rewards aggressive, fast swings

Tension Maintenance

  • Natural gut: Best (loses tension slowest)
  • Multifilament nylon: Good
  • Monofilament nylon: Moderate
  • Polyester: Poor (loses tension quickly, becomes dead)
  • Kevlar: Good initially, then breaks

Comfort

  • Softer strings (natural gut, multifilament) = less shock transmission
  • Stiffer strings (polyester, Kevlar) = more shock transmission
  • Lower tension = more comfort
  • Vibration dampeners: Modest effect on perception of vibration

String Gauge (Thickness)

Thinner Gauges (17, 1.20mm)

  • Better feel and ball bite
  • More spin potential
  • Less durable
  • Preferred by advanced players

Thicker Gauges (16, 1.30mm)

  • More durable
  • Less spin potential
  • More control
  • Preferred by recreational players and string breakers

Middle Gauges (16L, 1.25mm)

  • Balance of feel, spin, and durability
  • Most common gauge for competitive players

String Tension

Lower Tension (40-48 lbs)

  • More power
  • More spin (string bed deflects more, snaps back more)
  • Larger sweet spot
  • Less control
  • More comfortable

Higher Tension (52-62 lbs)

  • More control
  • Less power
  • Smaller sweet spot
  • More precision
  • Less comfortable (more shock transmission)

Recommended Tensions by Player Type

Player Type Recommended Tension Reason
Beginner 45-50 lbs Comfort and power
Intermediate 48-54 lbs Balance of power and control
Advanced (baseline) 44-50 lbs Spin and power
Advanced (all-court) 50-56 lbs Control and precision
Polyester string users 42-48 lbs Maximize snap-back spin

When to Restring

General Guidelines

  • Hours-based: Restring as many hours per month as you play per week (e.g., 8 hours/week = restring every 8 hours of play)
  • Tension-based: When strings feel dead or lose their responsiveness
  • Breakage-based: When strings break (polyester often performs poorly before breaking)

Signs Strings Need Replacing

  • Noticeable loss of power
  • Difficulty controlling ball direction
  • Strings feel dead or lifeless
  • Visible notching or wear on strings
  • Loss of spin effectiveness

Diagnostic & Training Matrix

Observation Failure Result Corrective Strategy
Tennis elbow symptoms after switching to polyester String too stiff for arm tolerance Chronic tendinopathy Switch to softer string (multifilament or gut); reduce tension
Strings break within 2 hours Too thin gauge or too low tension Frequent restringing cost Increase gauge (17 to 16); increase tension; try more durable string
Loss of spin control after 10 hours of play Polyester losing tension, becoming dead Reduced spin effectiveness Restring on regular schedule (every 15-20 hours for polyester)
Too much power, shots flying long String tension too low or too elastic Control issues Increase tension by 3-5 lbs; switch to slightly stiffer string
Dead, lifeless string feel Loss of tension or old strings Reduced performance Restring; consider natural gut or multifilament for livelier feel

Practical Application: "The String Test"

Try this experiment: string one racket with polyester at 48 lbs and another identical racket with multifilament nylon at 52 lbs. Play with each for a week. You will likely find the polyester gives you more spin and control but may bother your arm after long sessions. The nylon will feel more comfortable and powerful but may lack the bite you want on heavy topspin shots. Most competitive players compromise with a hybrid setup: polyester mains for spin and durability, nylon crosses for comfort and power. Find your ideal setup through experimentation, and do not be afraid to change as your game evolves.
