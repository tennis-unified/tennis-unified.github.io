ART-907: Knee Injuries in Tennis — ACL, Meniscus, and Patellar Tendinopathy

Executive Summary

Knee injuries account for 15-20% of all tennis injuries, with the sport's demands for rapid deceleration, lateral movement, and explosive direction changes creating significant stress on the joint. Understanding the mechanisms of ACL tears, meniscal damage, and patellar tendinopathy allows players to implement targeted prevention programs and make informed decisions about surgical and non-surgical management.

Knee Anatomy for Tennis Players

Ligamentous Stability

  • ACL (Anterior Cruciate Ligament): Prevents anterior tibial translation and rotational instability; most vulnerable during pivoting with foot planted
  • PCL (Posterior Cruciate Ligament): Prevents posterior tibial translation; less commonly injured in tennis
  • MCL (Medial Collateral Ligament): Resists valgus stress; commonly injured with wide-stance sliding
  • LCL (Lateral Collateral Ligament): Resists varus stress; rarely injured in isolation

Meniscus Function

The medial and lateral menisci distribute load across the tibiofemoral joint, absorbing 30-50% of compressive forces. They also contribute to joint stability, lubrication, and proprioception.

Extensor Mechanism

The quadriceps tendon, patella, and patellar tendon form the extensor mechanism. The patellar tendon transmits forces exceeding 6-8 times body weight during jumping and landing.

Common Knee Injuries in Tennis

ACL Tears

  • Mechanism: Non-contact pivoting injury — the player decelerates with the foot planted, the knee collapses into valgus, and the tibia internally rotates relative to the femur
  • Tennis scenarios: Directional change on hard court, sliding on clay with foot sticking, lunging for wide balls
  • Risk factors: Female sex (4-6x higher risk), prior ACL injury, hamstring-to-quadriceps strength imbalance
  • Symptoms: "Pop" sensation, immediate swelling, inability to continue

Meniscal Tears

  • Mechanism: Compression with rotation — the meniscus is trapped between the femoral condyle and tibial plateau during twisting
  • Types: Acute traumatic (vertical/longitudinal in younger players) vs. degenerative (complex/radial in older players)
  • Symptoms: Joint line pain, clicking, locking, effusion

Patellar Tendinopathy (Jumper's Knee)

  • Mechanism: Repetitive overload of the patellar tendon during jumping, landing, and directional changes
  • Location: Inferior pole of the patella (proximal tendon insertion)
  • Prevalence: Most common chronic knee complaint in tennis; affects 30-40% of players over age 35
  • Symptoms: Anterior knee pain with activity, pain descending stairs, pain with prolonged sitting

Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome

  • Mechanism: Maltracking of the patella in the trochlear groove due to VMO weakness or increased Q-angle
  • Symptoms: Retropatellar pain with stairs, squatting, prolonged sitting
  • Common in adolescent tennis players during growth spurts

Tennis-Specific Risk Factors

Surface Type

  • Hard courts: Higher friction coefficient increases torque during directional changes; higher ACL injury risk
  • Clay courts: Sliding can cause foot to stick unpredictably, increasing meniscal injury risk
  • Grass: Lowest friction increases slip risk but reduces torque on the knee

Training Load

  • Rapid increases in training volume (>20% weekly) overload the tendon-bone junction
  • Playing on consecutive days without recovery prevents tissue adaptation
  • Insufficient rest between matches in tournament play

Prevention Programs

Neuromuscular Training

  • Warm-up: 5 minutes light jogging with direction changes
  • Strength: Squats, lunges, Nordic hamstring curls (3 sets of 10 each)
  • Plyometrics: Box jumps, lateral bounds, single-leg hops (3 sets of 8 each)
  • Agility: T-drill, 5-10-5 shuttle, lateral shuffle (2 minutes)

Eccentric Decline Squat Protocol (Patellar Tendinopathy)

  • Stand on a 25-degree decline board with heels elevated
  • Perform single-leg squats with slow eccentric phase (3-5 seconds)
  • Progress from body weight to weighted
  • 3 sets of 15 repetitions, twice daily for 12 weeks

Hamstring-to-Quadriceps Strength Ratio

  • Optimal H:Q ratio is 0.6-0.7 (concentric) and 0.8-1.0 (eccentric)
  • Nordic hamstring curls increase eccentric hamstring strength
  • Players with H:Q ratio below 0.5 are at elevated ACL injury risk

Rehabilitation Protocols

ACL Reconstruction (Post-Surgical)

  • Weeks 0-2: ROM restoration, quadriceps activation, partial weight-bearing
  • Weeks 2-6: Full ROM, closed-chain strengthening, proprioception
  • Weeks 6-12: Progressive strengthening, stationary bike, pool work
  • Months 3-6: Running progression, agility training, sport-specific movements
  • Months 6-9: On-court drills, hitting without serve
  • Months 9-12: Full training, match play progression
  • Return to competition: 9-12 months minimum

Patellar Tendinopathy (Non-Surgical)

  • Heavy slow resistance training: 4 sets of 6-15 reps, 3 times per week, 12 weeks
  • Isometric holds: 5 sets of 45-second holds at 70% MVC for pain relief
  • Progression: Isometric to heavy slow resistance to energy storage loading
  • Return criteria: Pain-free with sport-specific loading

Practical Application: "Land Like a Cat"

When you land from any overhead or lateral movement, think about absorbing force through your hips and knees simultaneously. Practice single-leg landings with a "soft" knee — land with your knee tracking over your second toe, hip and knee both flexed, and your weight centered over your landing foot. Hold the landing position for 3 seconds. If your knee collapses inward, you need more hip strengthening. This single drill prevents more knee injuries than any brace ever could.
