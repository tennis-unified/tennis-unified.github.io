ART-804: Technical Development of the Backhand Stroke

Executive Summary

The backhand remains the most technically demanding stroke in tennis, with the ATP tour split between one-handed and two-handed varieties—each offering distinct advantages. Two-handed backs provide greater stability and power generation, particularly on high balls and defensive situations, while one-handed backs offer reach, slice versatility, and natural transition to net play. Coaching the backhand requires careful attention to the non-dominant arm role, body rotation, and the unique coordination demands that distinguish it from the forehand.

The Two-Handed Backhand

Grip Configuration

The modern two-handed backhand uses a dual-grip system:

  • Dominant Hand (bottom): Typically an Eastern forehand grip or slight semi-western (bevel 2-3). Provides racket face stability and guides the follow-through.
  • Non-Dominant Hand (top): Eastern backhand grip (bevel 1) or neutral grip. Controls the backswing and adds power through the contact zone.

The hands work together through preparation, then the non-dominant hand releases or lightens grip pressure through contact to allow the dominant hand to drive through the ball.

Technical Progression

  1. Unit Turn and Preparation: Both hands guide the racket back in a unified shoulder turn. Shoulders rotate 90+ degrees while hips rotate approximately 45 degrees, creating hip-shoulder separation.

  2. Backswing Position: Racket drops below ball height with both hands maintaining grip. The chin rests over the front shoulder to maintain visual tracking of the ball.

  3. Forward Swing: Leg drive initiates hip rotation, followed by shoulder turn. The non-dominant arm pulls the racket forward while the dominant arm extends through contact.

  4. Contact Point: Slightly in front of the body, at waist height. Both arms are extended (non-dominant arm may begin to release for additional reach).

  5. Follow-Through: Dominant arm continues across the body; non-dominant arm releases and extends backward for balance. Racket finishes over the front shoulder.

Advantages

  • Greater stability on fast, heavy balls
  • Superior high-ball handling (can drive shoulder-height balls)
  • More power potential due to two-arm drive
  • Better defensive capabilities when stretched
  • More consistent on returns of serve

Limitations

  • Reduced reach on wide balls (two hands limit extension)
  • More difficult to generate slice variations
  • Net transition less natural (two hands slow volley preparation)
  • Requires more precise footwork to get behind the ball

The One-Handed Backhand

Grip Configuration

The one-handed backhand uses the Eastern backhand grip (bevel 1) exclusively:

  • Base of the index knuckle on bevel 1 (the top bevel of the racket handle)
  • Heel of the hand slightly behind the handle
  • This grip naturally closes the racket face, requiring conscious attention to maintain proper face angle at contact

Technical Progression

  1. Preparation and Unit Turn: The non-dominant hand guides the racket back during the unit turn, then releases the racket as the backswing completes.

  2. Backswing: Single arm positions the racket behind the body. The hitting arm is bent with the racket head pointing toward the back fence.

  3. Forward Swing: Leg drive and hip rotation initiate the stroke. The hitting arm drops the racket head below the ball, creating the upward swing path.

  4. Contact Point: Arm is extended, contact slightly in front of the body at waist height. The body is coiled and uncoiling through the stroke.

  5. Follow-Through: Arm continues upward and across the body, finishing high with the racket near the opposite shoulder.

Advantages

  • Greater reach on wide balls
  • Natural slice backhand capability (defensive and offensive)
  • Smoother net transition (one hand on racket at all times)
  • Easier to disguise direction and spin
  • Classic aesthetic and versatility on all surfaces

Limitations

  • Less power on high balls and heavy pace
  • Requires greater individual arm strength
  • More demanding on timing and preparation
  • Vulnerable to high, heavy balls on the backhand side
  • More difficult to develop at junior levels

The Backhand Slice

Both one-handed and two-handed players can hit backhand slices, but the one-handed slice is far more prevalent on the ATP tour:

Mechanics

  • Preparation: Racket taken back high with the face slightly open
  • Forward Swing: A high-to-low chopping motion, cutting down and through the ball
  • Contact: In front of the body with a firm wrist
  • Follow-Through: Extension toward the target, maintaining the high-to-low plane

Tactical Applications

  • Defensive: Buying time when stretched wide or pulled off court
  • Approach Shot: Low, skidding slice approaches the opponent's weakness
  • Rhythm Disruption: Changing pace and ball trajectory
  • Net Approach: Slice approaches are standard at the net
  • Drop Shot: Extreme under-spin for short balls

Common Backhand Errors

Error Cause Correction
Two-handed backhand pulls across body Dominant hand overpowering, insufficient shoulder turn Drill with non-dominant arm only; emphasize shoulder rotation
One-handed backhand lacks power Late contact point, insufficient leg drive, no body rotation Shadow swings from service line; step into court for shorter balls
Backhand inconsistent on high balls Two-handed: arming the ball; One-handed: grip too weak Two-handed: drive with body; One-handed: consider switching to two-handed or using slice
Slice floats and sits up Insufficient forward momentum through contact, weak wrist Emphasize extension toward target; strengthen forearm for stability

Choosing Between One-Handed and Two-Handed

The choice involves multiple factors:

  • Age and Physical Development: Junior players often benefit from the stability of the two-handed backhand. One-handed development may be introduced in the mid-teens.
  • Playing Style: Aggressive baseliners and counter-punchers typically prefer two-handed. Serve-and-volleyers and all-court players benefit from one-handed versatility.
  • Coaching Philosophy: Some coaches advocate for one-handed development early, citing the slice benefits and net play transition.
  • Physical Considerations: Players with exceptional upper-body strength may prefer the one-handed game. Players with quick footwork and coordination may succeed with either.

Practical Application: "The Wall Drill for Backhand Consistency"

Use a wall or ball machine for high-repetition backhand practice:

  1. Stand 3-4 meters from the wall, positioned slightly behind the baseline.
  2. Hit continuous two-handed backhands focusing on consistent contact point.
  3. Count consecutive successful clears of the wall (target: 50+).
  4. Progress to alternating forehand and backhand to simulate rally conditions.
  5. Add movement: hit and recover to a central position between shots.

This drill builds muscle memory, contact point consistency, and the rhythm essential for match-quality backhands. Perform daily for 15-20 minutes during technical development phases.
