ART-525: Doubles Serve Strategy: Placement, Movement, and Partner Signals

Executive Summary

Serving in doubles requires fundamentally different tactics than serving in singles. The presence of a net player (your partner) and the ability to cover less court changes the optimal serve placement, spin usage, and follow-up strategy. This article provides a complete framework for doubles serving tactics.

Key Differences Between Singles and Doubles Serving

Court Coverage

In doubles, the alleys are in play, meaning: - Wide serves are riskier because the returner has more court to aim at - The net player can poach (cross to intercept the return), adding pressure - The server must cover only half the court after serving, making aggressive positioning possible

Net Player's Role

Your partner at the net serves as: - A target for the returner (they must hit around or over the net player) - A poaching threat that makes the returner hesitant - An additional target to aim at (serving at the net player can be effective)

Returner Positioning

In doubles, returners typically stand: - Deeper than in singles (to handle the net player's poaching) - Closer to the center (to protect against poaching) - On the baseline or slightly behind (to have more time)

Doubles Serve Placement Strategy

The Middle Serve (Most Common in Doubles)

Serving down the middle is the most common doubles serve because: - It reduces the return angle (the returner cannot hit sharply crosscourt) - It gives the net player the best poaching opportunity - It keeps the ball away from the returner's power zone - It minimizes the risk of being passed down the line

Target: The center of the service box, ideally low and with spin

The Wide Serve

Wide serves in doubles are used selectively: - To pull the returner off the court and open up the middle - When the returner is standing too close to the center - On the deuce court to pull a right-handed returner wide to their forehand

Risk: The returner can hit sharply crosscourt to the open court

The Body Serve

Body serves in doubles are effective because: - The returner has less room to swing with a net player nearby - It jams the returner, forcing a weak return - It keeps the ball in the middle of the court

Spin Usage in Doubles

Slice Serve

The most common doubles serve because: - It stays low (making it harder for the net player to poach) - It curves into the body or wide - It provides good control

Kick Serve

Less common in doubles because: - The high bounce gives the returner time to set up - It can be poached more easily than a low slice serve - However, it is effective against returners who struggle with high balls

Flat Serve

Used selectively in doubles: - As a surprise weapon to take time away from the returner - When the returner is standing deep - Riskier than in singles because of the net player

Net Partner Signals and Communication

Pre-Serve Signals

Before the serve, your partner signals their intentions: - Fist behind back: No poaching (stay in position) - Open hand behind back: Poach on the return - Pointing to one side: Move to that side (shift to cover) - Touching the net: Move to the middle (protect the middle)

Movement Coordination

After the serve: - If partner poaches: The server covers the open court - If partner stays: The server covers their half of the court - If partner shifts: The server adjusts to cover the new gap

The Serve-and-Volley Combination

In doubles, serving and coming to the net is more common than in singles: - The server follows their serve to the net - The server and partner form a two-person net wall - The server covers the middle, the partner covers the alley

Server Movement After the Serve

Serving from the Deuce Court

  • After serving wide: Move to cover the open court (toward the center)
  • After serving down the middle: Stay in position, ready for poach
  • After serving body: Move toward the center

Serving From the Ad Court

  • After serving wide: Move to cover the open court (toward the center)
  • After serving down the middle: Stay in position
  • After serving body: Move toward the center

Common Doubles Serving Mistakes

Serving Too Wide

Excessive wide serving in doubles opens up the middle and makes poaching difficult. Keep the majority of serves down the middle.

Ignoring the Net Partner

The server must be aware of the net partner's position and signals. Serving without communication leads to confusion and open courts.

Not Covering After Serving

The server must move after serving to cover the open court. Staying at the baseline after serving leaves the court exposed.

Predictable Serving

Serving to the same location every time allows the returner to cheat and the net partner's poaching to become predictable. Vary placement.

Diagnostic & Training Matrix

Practical Application: "The Middle-First Rule"

In doubles, establish a rule: serve down the middle at least 60% of the time. This keeps the ball away from the returner's power zones, maximizes your partner's poaching opportunities, and reduces the risk of being passed. Once the returner starts cheating toward the middle to protect against the middle serve, you can begin mixing in more wide serves as a change-up.
