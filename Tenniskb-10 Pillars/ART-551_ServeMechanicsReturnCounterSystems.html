ART-551: Return of Serve Fundamentals: Grip, Stance, and Readiness

Executive Summary

The return of serve is the second most important shot in tennis (after the serve itself) and the shot that determines whether the server or returner controls the point. This article covers the fundamental elements of the return: grip selection, stance options, and the ready position.

Why the Return Matters

The Return's Importance

  • The return is the shot that begins 50%+ of all points in tennis
  • Players who return well are rarely dominated by big servers
  • A strong return neutralizes the server's primary advantage
  • Breaking serve is the foundation of winning matches

Return Success Rates

  • Elite returners win 35-45% of return points
  • Average players win 25-35% of return points
  • The difference between good and great returning is often just 5-10%

Grip for Returning

Forehand Return Grip

For forehand returns, use your normal forehand grip: - Eastern forehand: Good for flat, driving returns - Semi-western: Good for topspin returns - Western: Good for high-bouncing returns

Backhand Return Grip

For backhand returns: - Eastern backhand: Standard grip for backhand returns - Continental: Used by some players for two-handed backhands - Two-handed backhand: The top hand uses a continental or eastern backhand grip

Grip Changes

The key to effective returning is changing grips quickly based on where the serve is directed: - Forehand serve → Forehand grip - Backhand serve → Backhand grip - Body serve → Whichever grip allows the quicker reaction

Grip Pressure

Maintain moderate grip pressure (4-5 out of 10) when waiting to return: - Too tight: Slow reaction time; difficulty changing grips - Too loose: Racket may twist on contact - Just right: Quick grip changes; stable contact

Stance Options

Square Stance (Open to the Net)

  • Feet parallel to the net
  • Weight on the balls of the feet
  • Easy to move in any direction
  • Best for: Aggressive returns; stepping into the return

Neutral Stance (Slightly Open)

  • Feet at a 45-degree angle to the net
  • Weight slightly on the back foot
  • Balanced between stability and mobility
  • Best for: All-purpose returning

Closed Stance (Sideways)

  • Feet perpendicular to the net
  • Weight on the back foot
  • Provides stability for long strokes
  • Best for: Defensive returns; deep returns

Crouched Stance

  • Knees deeply bent
  • Weight low and forward
  • Quick first step in any direction
  • Best for: Returning big serves; staying low to handle low balls

The Ready Position

Body Position

  • Feet shoulder-width apart
  • Knees slightly bent
  • Weight on the balls of the feet
  • Racket held in front of the body at chest height
  • Eyes on the server

Racket Position

The racket should be held in a neutral position that allows quick transition to either forehand or backhand: - Option 1: Racket in the middle (allows equal access to both grips) - Option 2: Racket slightly toward the backhand (most players are slower to reach the backhand) - Option 3: Racket slightly toward the forehand (if the server rarely serves to your backhand)

Mental Readiness

  • Anticipate the serve (think about where the server is likely to serve)
  • Be ready to move in any direction
  • Focus on the server's toss and racket
  • Prepare to split step at the moment of contact

The Split Step

What Is the Split Step?

The split step is a small hop that the returner takes just as the server contacts the ball: - Both feet leave the ground briefly - The returner lands with feet apart and knees bent - This preloads the legs for a quick first step

When to Split Step

The split step should occur at the exact moment the server's racket contacts the ball. This timing ensures: - The returner is in the optimal position to move - The legs are preloaded for explosive movement - The returner is balanced and ready to react

Common Split Step Faults

  • Too early: Landing before the serve is struck; must reload to move
  • Too late: Landing after the serve is struck; slow to react
  • Too high: Wasting time in the air; slow to move
  • Too low: Not preloading the legs; slow first step

Diagnostic & Training Matrix

Observation Failure Result Corrective Strategy
Slow grip changes Poor grip transition practice Rushed returns; mishits Grip change drills; quick-switch practice
Wrong stance for the situation Inflexible stance Poor balance and reach Stance adaptation drills; situational practice
Poor ready position Racket held too low or too high Slow preparation Ready position drills; racket height adjustment
Split step mistimed Poor timing Slow reactions Split step drills with a partner serving
Weight on heels Incorrect weight distribution Slow first step Weight transfer drills; balls-of-feet practice

Practical Application: "The Grip Change Drill"

Have a partner call out "forehand" or "backhand" randomly while you are in the ready position. Practice changing your grip as quickly as possible in response. Time how long it takes to get to the correct grip. Aim for under 0.5 seconds. This drill develops the quick grip changes needed for effective returning.
