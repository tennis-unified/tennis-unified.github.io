ART-924: Tennis During Pregnancy — Safety, Modification, and Benefits

Executive Summary

Tennis during pregnancy can be safe and beneficial for healthy women with uncomplicated pregnancies, following the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guidelines. However, trimester-specific modifications are necessary to accommodate the physiological changes of pregnancy and protect both mother and baby. Understanding when to modify, when to stop, and how to return to play postpartum ensures a safe and healthy experience.

Benefits of Tennis During Pregnancy

Regular exercise during pregnancy, including tennis, offers numerous benefits. Cardiovascular exercise improves maternal fitness, reduces the risk of gestational diabetes, and helps maintain healthy weight gain. Weight-bearing exercise supports bone health and reduces the risk of osteoporosis. Exercise improves mood, reduces anxiety and depression, and improves sleep quality. Women who exercise during pregnancy have shorter labors, fewer cesarean deliveries, and faster postpartum recovery. Tennis, as a social sport, provides psychological benefits through social interaction and sense of normalcy. The key is modification, not cessation, of physical activity.

ACOG Guidelines

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends that pregnant women with uncomplicated pregnancies engage in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week. This is the same recommendation as for non-pregnant adults. ACOG advises that women who were regularly active before pregnancy can continue their previous exercise regimen, with modifications as needed. Women should avoid activities with high risk of falling or abdominal trauma, including contact sports, horseback riding, and scuba diving. Tennis is classified as a low-risk sport for pregnant women who were already playing, provided they modify intensity and avoid overheating.

First Trimester Modifications

The first trimester (weeks 1-12) is a critical period of fetal organ development. The primary concern during this period is overheating, as elevated core temperature in early pregnancy is associated with neural tube defects. Players should avoid playing in hot and humid conditions, ensure adequate hydration, and take frequent breaks. Fatigue is common in the first trimester, and players should reduce training volume and intensity accordingly. Nausea and breast tenderness may affect comfort, requiring adjustments to clothing and playing schedule. The volume of play should be reduced by approximately 20-30% from pre-pregnancy levels.

Second Trimester Modifications

The second trimester (weeks 13-27) is often the most comfortable period for pregnant tennis players. Energy levels typically improve, and nausea usually subsides. However, the growing uterus shifts the center of gravity, affecting balance and agility. The hormone relaxin increases joint laxity, particularly in the pelvis, hips, and spine, increasing the risk of joint injuries. Players should modify their movement patterns, avoiding aggressive direction changes and diving. The expanding belly affects swing mechanics, particularly on the forehand and serve. Intensity should be maintained at a moderate level, with attention to hydration and avoiding overheating. The talk test — being able to hold a conversation during play — is a useful guide for appropriate intensity.

Third Trimester Modifications

The third trimester (weeks 28-40) presents the greatest challenges for tennis participation. The significantly altered center of gravity, increased body weight, and reduced lung capacity make movement more difficult and tiring. Balance is compromised, increasing the risk of falls. The uterus can compress the inferior vena cava when lying supine, reducing cardiac output. Players should reduce play to recreational levels, focusing on social interaction rather than competitive intensity. Shorter sessions (30-45 minutes) with frequent breaks are recommended. Many players find that they naturally reduce their activity level as the pregnancy progresses, and this should be respected.

When to Stop

Certain signs warrant immediate cessation of tennis during pregnancy. Vaginal bleeding, regular painful contractions, amniotic fluid leakage, decreased fetal movement, dizziness, headache, chest pain, calf swelling, and sudden onset of shortness of breath are all reasons to stop playing and seek medical attention. Any player experiencing these symptoms should be evaluated by their obstetrician before resuming activity. Additionally, if a player feels that tennis is no longer enjoyable or comfortable, it is perfectly acceptable to stop — the goal is health and enjoyment, not athletic achievement.

High-Risk Pregnancies

Women with high-risk pregnancies, including those with a history of preterm labor, placenta previa, incompetent cervix, gestational hypertension, or multiple gestations, may be advised to avoid tennis entirely. The decision should be made in consultation with the obstetrician, weighing the benefits of exercise against the risks of the specific condition. Women with a history of miscarriage or those who have undergone fertility treatments may also be advised to limit activity.

Postpartum Return to Play

Return to play after delivery should be gradual and guided by medical clearance. The ACOG recommends that women who had uncomplicated vaginal deliveries can begin light exercise within days of delivery, while those who had cesarean deliveries should wait 4-6 weeks. The postpartum period involves significant physiological changes, including continued joint laxity from relaxin (which can persist for up to 5 months), abdominal muscle separation (diastasis recti), and pelvic floor weakness. Core and pelvic floor rehabilitation should precede return to tennis. A gradual progression from walking to light hitting to full play over 4-6 weeks is recommended. Breastfeeding players should ensure adequate caloric intake and hydration to support both exercise and milk production.

Practical Application: "Play Smart, Not Hard"

If you are pregnant and want to continue playing tennis, first obtain clearance from your obstetrician. Modify your play by reducing volume and intensity, avoiding overheating, and listening to your body. Use the talk test to monitor intensity — if you cannot hold a conversation, you are working too hard. Stay hydrated, wear supportive clothing, and take frequent breaks. As your pregnancy progresses, shift your focus from competition to social enjoyment. After delivery, obtain medical clearance before returning to play and invest time in core and pelvic floor rehabilitation before resuming full tennis. Your health and your baby's health come first.
