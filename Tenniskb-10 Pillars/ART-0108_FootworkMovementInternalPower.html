ART-0108: Directional Readiness Footwork — Preparing the Kinetic Chain for Lateral vs. Forward Drive

Executive Summary

The first step is always in a specific direction, but most players land in a neutral, symmetrical stance and then rotate, shift, and push — a sequence that costs 30-80 ms. Directional readiness means pre-setting the feet, hips, and shoulders toward the likely direction during the split step, so the first step is a drive, not a construction. Lateral-ready and forward-ready are two distinct presets with different geometry. This article explains the presets, the kinetic chain orientation, and the 30-80 ms they save.

The Neutral-Stance Tax

The neutral stance — feet square, weight 50/50, hips level — is the default ready position. It is safe, but it carries a tax. The first step is always directional, so a neutral landing requires a sequence: shift the weight to the push-side foot, rotate the hips, orient the foot, then push. Each step in the sequence takes time, and the total is 30-80 ms.

The tax is visible in the first step's mechanics. A player who lands neutral and then shifts shows a "stutter" — a small, preparatory movement before the real step. The stutter is the weight shift, and it is the tax. A player who lands pre-set shows no stutter; the first step is a drive from the first frame.

The Lateral-Ready Preset

The lateral-ready preset is for the most common first step: the lateral drive to a groundstroke. The geometry:

  • Feet. Slightly staggered — the push-side foot back, the free-side foot forward. The stagger is small, 5-10 cm, but it pre-sets the push.
  • Hips. Square or slightly open — rotated a few degrees toward the likely direction. The rotation is small, but it pre-loads the hip abductors and the push-side glute.
  • Shoulders. Square to the net, or slightly closed. The shoulders stay level; the rotation happens at the hips.
  • Center of mass. Shifted 2-4 cm toward the push side, so the weight is already over the loaded foot.

The result is a landing that is ready to drive laterally. The push-side foot is back and loaded; the free-side foot is forward and light. The first step is a drive along the pre-set vector.

The Forward-Ready Preset

The forward-ready preset is for the approach shot, the short ball, the net approach. The geometry is different:

  • Feet. More parallel, or slightly staggered with the non-dominant foot forward. The stagger is smaller than in the lateral preset.
  • Hips. Square, not rotated. The forward drive is symmetric; there is no need to pre-rotate.
  • Shoulders. Square, level. The trunk is upright, ready to drive up.
  • Center of mass. Slightly forward — over the balls of the feet, not the heels. The weight is ready to drive up and forward.

The forward drive uses the glutes and quadriceps more than the lateral drive; the lateral drive uses the hip abductors and the push-side leg more. The preset matches the muscle recruitment to the intended direction.

The Kinetic Chain Orientation

The kinetic chain is the series of links — foot, ankle, knee, hip, trunk, shoulder — that transmit force from the ground to the racket. Each link can be oriented in advance, and each orientation removes slack from the chain.

  • Foot. The push-side foot is oriented in the direction of the push, so the drive is along the foot's long axis, not across it.
  • Ankle. The ankle is stiff and aligned, so the force transmits up the leg, not into the joint.
  • Knee. The knee is flexed 20-30° and aligned over the foot, so the drive is along the leg's axis.
  • Hip. The hip is rotated and loaded, so the drive starts from the hip, not the foot.
  • Trunk. The trunk is stacked and stable, so the force transmits up and out, not into a lean.
  • Shoulder. The shoulder is back and ready, so the swing can start from a stable base.

Each link pre-oriented removes a degree of freedom. The first step is then a whip — a coordinated drive along a pre-set chain — not a wind-up.

Cutting the 30-80 ms

The presets cut the 30-80 ms by moving the decision before the landing. The neutral-stance player decides after landing: which way, how much, which foot. The pre-set player decides before landing: the direction is read, the geometry is set, the landing is the execution.

The 30-80 ms is the difference between a drive and a construction. A drive is a single movement along a pre-set vector; a construction is a sequence of movements that build the vector. The drive is faster because it has fewer steps.

The evidence is in the first-step times. Elite movers — who land pre-set — show first-step ground contact at 150-200 ms after landing. Club movers — who land neutral — show 250-350 ms. The difference is the 30-80 ms tax, plus the compounding effect of a slower start on the rest of the movement.

Diagnostic & Training Matrix

Stutter before the first step Neutral landing, then shift 30-80 ms tax on every first step Practice pre-set landings: stagger the feet, shift the COM, drive

Forward drive is slow and flat No forward preset Late approach; weak net play Practice the forward-ready preset: parallel feet, COM forward

Practical Application: "Preset the Push"

Give the player one image: land ready to go, not ready to decide. Then build the preset in three steps:

  1. The read. During the opponent's swing, the player reads the likely direction — lateral or forward — and selects the preset. The read is a probability, not a certainty (see ART-0104).
  2. The geometry. During the split step's flight, the player sets the preset: staggered feet for lateral, parallel feet for forward, COM shifted, hips rotated. The geometry is set before the landing.
  3. The drive. The landing is the execution. The first step is a drive along the pre-set vector — no stutter, no shift, no construction. The coach watches for the drive: a single, explosive movement from the first frame.

A player who lands pre-set does not move faster in the first step — they simply never pay the 30-80 ms tax that neutral landers pay on every exchange.
