The Time to Start Tennis is When You Are Young

Tennis is without a doubt the most wonderful of sports. Fans of other sports might well object to such a bold statement, but tennis can

be enjoyed by the young and old alike, from little boys and girls to el-

derly grandparents, regardless of sex or age. If you play tennis at your

own level of ability, you naturally enjoy the benefits of healthy physical exercise. Tennis is also a great way to socialize; regardless of nationality or creed, you and your opponent can have a good time together. It can therefore be fairly said that tennis is decidedly superior to other sports.

Boys and girls wishing to learn the game can do so beginning at an early age, either at school or a community sports club. Most schoolchildren first learn such games as baseball and football, but unless they are very good they cannot continue to play these sports indefinitely. When

their school days are over they become involved in work or study and

find it difficult to participate in team play. Only then do many people

begin to play tennis.

Had these boys and girls started to play tennis as youngsters, they

would be able to enjoy the game at all ages and continue playing for

years.

Though light and somewhat short junior rackets designed for youngsters are available, it is sometimes difficult for children to handle these properly, as their hand may be too small to get a firm grip on the

racket, or it may be too heavy for their physical strength. Usually, however, tennis is the most suitable sport for a growing child to start with.

Starting at an early age, children can develop a correct, beautiful swing. To impart the ball with the proper speed requires a good forward swing,

while a long follow-through is needed to send the ball on a precise trajectory.

| learned how to execute a correct, natural swing very young, and it has remained with me to this very day. Adults beginning to learn the

sport often exert themselves unnecessarily, rather than hitting the ball

with a natural swing.

For your own sake, | urge you to begin playing tennis while still in grade school or junior high, if possible. If you begin at an older age, re-

member that the game does not require great strength or overexertion.

THE GRIP—The First Step in Tennis

The first step in playing tennis is your grip. In general, there are three kinds of grips: the Eastern, the Continental, and the Western, and they each have their own special characteristics.

| use the Eastern Grip, and since it is considered the most international one, it is the one | will basically discuss in this book.

The Eastern Grip (Shake Hands Grip) is the one | use for my forehand stroke.

Take the racket in your left hand with the handle pointing towards you, so that the edge of the racket face is at right angles to the ground.

Now, place your right hand against the strings and, without changing the angle of it, slide your hand down to the grip and grasp the racket firmly.

In other words, your hand is in the same shape as when you shake hands. The “V” made by your thumb and index finger should be almost

exactly above the grip of the racket.

This grip is the most commonly used forehand grip. Recently, almost

all the top players are using it. | believe that there are a great many people who, if they do not exactly use this grip, utilize something quite similar.

| should like young people, and those who are about to take up tennis for the first time, to really make an effort to use this grip. It can be used comfortably for all forehand strokes, for high shots, average balls and for the low ones too.

Many players in other parts of the world, especially the Orient, hold

their hand further over; in other words, a grip which is much closer to the

Western Grip. Perhaps this is influenced by the old soft tennis style that

has been handed down over the years.

There are times when this grip would be effective: to hit a ball that comes hard and fast, to hit a gentle ball as strongly as possible, and to

hit a deep ball, but generally it is impossible to get favorable results with this grip. This was the basic grip used in top class tennis when players

hit the ball softly on slow courts. In tennis today, it is seen being used on

all types of courts.

No matter how much your tennis improves, the importance of the grip never varies. Fletcher receiving instruction on his “grip” from Rosewall.

number of synthetic hard courts. From this point of view, | believe the Shake Hands Grip is the one that gives you the most effecfive forehand stroke.

Remember, however, that even if your grip is good, if your technique is bad, you'll be unable to hit even the easiest of shots.

Opening Stance—Ready to Receive

As a sport, hitting a ball is what tennis is all about, but preparing yourself before you hit the ball, and while you are waiting for the ball, is extremely important. This is because your stance also greatly influences

the form with which you make your shots.

The stance you take when you are getting ready to receive is most

important—and this should be as natural as possible. Without making yourself too tense, stand with your feet a suitable distance apart, so that you can move quickly in any direction; forward, backward, or to the left

or right. It is undesirable to take too low a stance, or alternatively, to

stand too upright.

Hold your racket in front of you, but do not let it slope downward from the wrist. Avoid getting tired by holding your racket lightly with your

right hand, and always supporting the center part of the racket with your

left. In short, with this stance you are ready to respond immediately to whatever type of ball comes at you.

Wait with your racket in front of the center of your body, facing straight at your opponent.

The Role of the Eyes is Important

It is necessary to watch the ball closely for every shot, to judge quickly how your opponent's ball has been hit, how it is coming to you,

and on the basis of this, to make your next move.

When you have been playing tennis for a long time, it becomes

possible to take your eyes off the ball and still be able to hit it. Again, you will probably develop the ability to predict your opponent’s moves.

However, to achieve really good shots you should endeavor to keep your eyes on the ball for as long as possible, right up until it actually hits the strings. | have good eyes, and try as much as possible not to take my eyes off the ball. Footwork—The Basis of Good Play

Tennis is a sport where, for most of the time, you are moving about a court. No matter how skillfully you hit the ball, if you move too slowly,

or if you lack stamina, there is no way that you can win the game. But it

is still not enough to simply be quick on your feet and have staying power.

All footwork has an important connection with making a player's

form stable. If you look at the form of top class players, their play some-

how always seems to be steady, because they constantly pay careful attention to their footwork, and keep the necessary distance between their feet. Take a careful look at how they go after a ball. The more skil-

led the player, the less noise he makes on the court with his feet, and

the fewer times he slides on the court.

The surface of a tennis court may be grass, or it may be a hard

court, asphalt, rubber or matting. They each have their own peculiarities. Among these, is that it is impossible to slide on asphalt and mat courts,

and so no matter how small your steps, your footwork will always be

such that you will be able to make your feet float. With a grass court, if it

is only slightly damp it is very easy to slide, but’in that case try to slide more on the balls of your feet. It is easiest of all to slide on a clay court, and especially so in the case of the en-tout-cas type, as it becomes difficult to perform perfect footwork.

With ground strokes, there are three types of returns that you can make, depending on the spin of the ball.

There is the top spin, where you put a forward spin on the ball; the slice, where you put a backward spin on the ball; and the flat stroke, where, when you hit the ball, the edge of your racket is at right angles to the ground. Although it is called a flat stroke, you can put a spin on the

ball either by your follow-through, or by the proper racket angle, but these are not balls to which you consciously add spin.

The service is the only shot in tennis that you are completely free to

hit in any way you want. You toss the ball to where it is easiest for you

to hit, and you are then free to choose between a full force blow, a spin,

a slow serve to your opponent's court. To help win the game you should,

of course, try to deliver a powerful, effective service.

The service consists of tossing the ball up with the left hand and swinging at it with the right shoulder action while it is in the air. This is called the service swing. Ideally, you should hit the ball from the highest

point possible with your body and arm fully stretched. The service can be executed without the slightest interference from your opponent. Un-

like other shots, it is very difficult to achieve proper form, since you have

to co-ordinate the use of both hands. The service is a shot in which

rhythm and balance are essential. The whole body must move very smoothly and, if you are a beginner, this is very difficult to execute properly.

Combining an accurate toss with a rhythmical swing is most

important in your service.

The right shoulder action used in the service is very much like throwing a ball. Think of the racket as an extension of your arm. The

swinging action depends on your body type and physical characteristics, | so that everyone’s service is somewhat different. It is obvious, however, | that unless you use your whole body to produce a good, smooth swing,

you cannot attain proper speed and control of the service shot.

In tennis today, it is power that wins games, but among the players, Rosewall has a small build, standing just 5’6” (169 cm) tall and weighing

In addition, he has better than average eyesight, and this, combined

are the basis for his fine play close to the net, and the excellent way in which he covers the court. With fine arm and leg muscles developed through training since he was a child, he has the power necessary for tennis. It is easy to see why he has been given the nickname of “Muscles.”

life, which is centered around tennis, that is the reason for him retaining

the rank of the world’s Number Two Player in 1974. There are no other

obvious examples of players who make every effort to get sufficient sleep, avoid stimulating foods, and most important of all, to avoid extremes of psychological fatigue.
