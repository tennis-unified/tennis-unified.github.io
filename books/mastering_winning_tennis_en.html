Because I have seen so many tennis matches where the better tennis player lost, due to the psychological advantage the oppos-

ing player either had before the match, or was able to gain during the match.

When I walked onto the tennis courts in a tournament, I used to feel a little like a lone Christian in the Colliseum facing the roaring lions. So I asked an outstanding tennis player how

he felt when he began a tennis match. His reply, “I always expect to win. You need to have a positive attitude, think positively!” Great, I thought. The next time I went out I thought positively. This attitude quickly reversed, when my opponent whizzed ball after ball past me. Many things my opponent did

or said shook my confidence — “Let’s get this over, the winner will have to play another round and I want to rest a little be-

fore I play the next round. I just finished the circuit.” Frankly,

my circuit consists of the two tennis tournaments we have in our town, so I turned to psychology to improve my game.

It has been shown that psychology plays an important role in every aspect of life. Emotions play an important role in work, religion, play and our interpersonal relationships. The way we

feel may affect our work and may also affect those with whom we work. If this is the case, then the way we feel may affect those with whom we play. The big question is, “How can we affect those with whom we play tennis so that we can “beat them?”

How can I prepare myself psychologically to win and _ stay “psyched up” to “psyche out” my opponent?

That is what this book is all about.

Stan Smith was once rejected in his try to become a tournament ball

boy on the basis that he was too clumsy, but he devoted almost every waking hour to become today one of the finest, if not the most skilled, player in tennis.

Just about everyone likes to win. Tennis players are no exception. Some people claim they play tennis for fun only, and that

they don’t care whether they win or lose. Those of you who feel that way need not read any further. This chapter is for those who like to win. We have a deepseated, unrelenting need to win.

I maintain that we also have fun playing tennis.

Mental attitude is crucial in tennis. Psychological factors are very important. Concentration is a must. You have to be think-

ing all the time. Any tennis book and any top-flight player will tell you this. Harry Hopman, the renowned Australian coach

has frequently stated that success on the tennis court results twenty-five per cent from strokes and seventy-five per cent from mental factors. But what is the correct mental attitude? What are the psychological factors? How do you use them to

your advantage? How do you improve your ability to concen-

trate? How can you be thinking all the time?

This chapter will deal with these questions in a manner that hopefully will explain the psychological factors involved in playing winning tennis. The intent is to show (a) how to improve

one’s game, (b) how to use mental factors to one’s own advantage, and (c) how to use mental factors to the disadvantage

of one’s opponent.

The Psychology of Improving Your Game

A great player having a bad day will always beat a poor player having a good day. Therefore, you should become a great player. How do you do that? Correct practice, proper coaching,

analysis of mistakes, and physical conditioning are all essential,

and all have their psychological aspects.

The sheer amount of practice is critical. Other things being

equal, the man who has logged 1,000 hours on the court will beat the man with 100 hours behind him every time.

When you play a set against somebody, that’s good practice. When you rally from the baseline, that’s also good practice. When you hit the ball against the wall, that’s good practice too. But there’s even better practice. Which are the most important

shots? The earliest ones: serve and return of serve. You have to hit one of these every single point (unless your opponent is accommodating enough to double fault). The other strokes may

or may not come up. If you hit many serves in a row, say 75 or 100, you will eventually develop consistency, or “get in the

groove’. Merely hitting a great number of serves, however, is not sufficient. On every practice serve one should be thinking

specifically about what he is doing as to type of serve (flat, spin, slice, American twist), placement (forehand, backhand, right at the opponent), and whether it’s a first or second serve.

If youre thinking about exactly where and how you serve in practice, it will be much easier to also do this during a match.

One technique which has proved valuable for many is to tape

a small piece of bright-colored cloth to the court, two feet inside the service line and two feet from the sideline. See how many times you can hit the spot.

Also, get someone to hit serves to you so that you can practice the return. Just as with the serve, each return should have a definite plan: deep down the line to the backhand, low crosscourt to the forehand. Again, if you think about it in practice, it will be easier to think about it in a match.

With respect to the ground strokes, one should find his weaknesses and systematically work on them. If you are unhappy with your high backhands, have your partner hit you nothing

but high backhands for half an hour or more.

A good coach or teacher can be highly valuable. A knowledge-

able non-participant observing and suggesting what to change

and what not to change is generally preferable to making guesses by yourself. Basically, tennis coaches serve the purpose of attempting to improve the player’s stroke production. There are, however, several psychological factors involved with coaching.

Your coach may insist that you play the “Big Game”, but your

make-up is such that this simply is not your style. Possibly, the

coach gives more criticism and less praise than is right for your temperament. Just as in psychotherapy where not just any ther-

apist is right for every patient, not every coach is right for every tennis player. One of the most important functions of a coach

is to provide an atmosphere in which the player will enjoy tennis and want to improve his game. If tennis is starting to bore you, you should think seriously about switching coaches.

A tennis coach is in a position to develop the strategy of a player. Besides improving his strokes, the alert coach should

help the student with things such as: where to hit it and when; how to change his game; how to play different players differ-

ently. In short, the coach should make it a point to concem himself with the psychological factors in tennis.

Unquestionably, a future champion must find out what he is doing wrong and change it. A coach can help. But not every-

one can afford a coach and a coach can’t be there all the time. The lack of insight that some players have as to the mistakes they are making is amazing. Billie Jean King tells of a young

man who spent all aftemoon without hitting one successful pass-

ing shot. Afterwards, when asked why he didn’t lob more or try hitting right at the opponent, he just shrugged his shoulders.

In other words he didn’t know, or apparently care, why he lost all those points! After every point you lose, you should verbalize, whether out loud or to yourself, exactly why you lost

it. Not just the obvious, like “T hit it into the net.” If you netted

it, was it because you took your eye off the ball, because you didn’t bend your knees to that low ball, because you loosened

your grip, or what? If the reason you lost the point was that “he put it away”, how did he get in a position to put it away? Was that short lob you hit the only shot available? Would a stronger return of service have prevented him from taking the

offensive?

Initially, it may not always be immediately clear what you did wrong, but you will get better at this analysis with practice. Very often players who tally their reasons for losing each point

are astounded. to discover the great number of points that were lost in almost exactly the same way. One player with a reason-

ably strong first serve (which he always followed to net) found

that well over half the service points he lost resulted from hitting a weak first volley or missing the first volley altogether. What to do? Work on first volleys? Work on service placement? Quit

going to net on serve? Certainly this player must change something. Just what he changes is another issue. The important point

here is that by analyzing the reason for every lost point, he was

able to identify a significant area of weakness. Another way to find your mistakes is to make films of your strokes. The camera

is brutally frank.

One’s success on the court is also dependent on his physical condition. Tennis is more enjoyable and winning is easier if one is in good shape. If you're tired, it’s more work to hit your

best serve, it’s more effort to run to the net, it’s harder to do everything. According to Rod Laver, the benefits from physical

conditioning extend beyond the physical. Laver insists that con-

ditioning aids concentration and decreases nervousness. This makes good psychological sense. Being tired works on your mind.

It reduces the desire to chase the ball, to try your best. It’s demoralizing to be tired, especially if you think your opponent isn’t tired. It takes your mind off your game. If you play tournament tennis, there will frequently be matches in which you're

tired to one degree or another. Make the best of the situation. Remind yourself of the following: you can rest as much as you

want after the match; your opponent may be just as tired, if

not more so, than you are; a tired winner is better than a tired loser! In this way, you can partially compensate for the mental

effects of being tired. Better than that, however, is don’t get tired. Get in shape. Run at least a mile a day. That’s right — even if you're over fifty. Check with your physician. I’m posi-

tive he'll agree. In addition to running, which is perha S) the most universally beneficial exercise, there are a number of exer-

cises specifically for tennis. The late 1972 and early 1973 issues of World Tennis have described a number of these. Anyone who really wants to win will work to improve his skill, and physical

condition. This should include incorporation of the psychological factors discussed above. The next two sections are concerned with what can be done mentally with one’s given level of skill.

Every tennis player has heard a thousand times, “concentrate”. Without exception, he agrees, “that’s right, I ought to concentrate.” What, specifically, does one do to concentrate? While

some players are innately better concentrators than others, there are some techniques that can help one’s concentration.

Saying to yourself, “watch the ball” is one way. Just before each point, say “watch the ball” and mean it. Watch it even when

you don’t think it will help. When your adversary is about to

serve, holding the ball before he has tossed it, that is the time to start watching the ball. Watch it continuously from that time

until after you have hit it, including when it is on your racket.

As you are swinging, don’t watch where you expect to hit it.

Watch the ball. While your focus is on the ball, your peripheral vision can take in most of the court including, in particular, the moves of your opponent. According to Tom Brown, even at the

most advanced levels of the game, one still needs to remind oneself continually, to watch the ball.

One should also concentrate on where he is going to try to hit the ball. Before every serve, plan to hit it to forehand, backhand, or right at him; and know whether or not you're going to

follow it to net. There should also be an overail plan of how often you're going to hit the ball to each area of the court. You may decide to serve approximately seventy-five per cent to the backhand, and twenty-five per cent to the forehand. Rather than serve every fourth ball to the forehand, you should

mix them up randomly. This means occasionally two and three

in a row to the forehand, and maybe up to ten in a row to the backhand. The receiver should be kept guessing.

The best players simultaneously insist that: you have to be thinking all the time; and your reactions to given situations

should become automatic. On the surface this may seem like

an impossible paradox. Let’s see if it can be resolved. It has been stated above that one should note mentally the reason for

every losing point. If one has several mental notes that he should have bent his knees on low shots, it will finally take effect. He

will start bending his knees on low shots without thinking about it.

It is possible to concentrate on more than one thing at a time, if you will but identify what those things are. When you are on your way to the net, there will be times when your adversary hits a hard low drive that just clears the net and forces you to bend down and hit a defensive “volley up”. You feel you are doing well just to get the ball back. Thus you are satisfied with

a shot which pops up into the middle of your opponent’s court.

Chances are very good that he passes you on the next shot and you chalk up the loss of the point to his excellent return of service. Let’s consider this situation a little further. If he had

given you an easier-to-handle return of service, you probably

would have placed the first volley deep to the backhand corner.

But it really wouldn’t have been much more difficult to hit that defensive volley also deep to the backhand comer. The reason you didn’t hit it there is you didn’t think of it. You had your hands full just getting it back. If you teil yourself enough times “hit defensive volley ups to the backhand comer”, you'll soon

find yourself able to do just that automatically.

Some participants “choke up” under pressure. Choking up means not playing as well as usual, and typically takes the form of hitting the ball softer than usual, ostensibly for the purpose

of “playing it safe”. It is common to tap the second serve when the pressure is on, to make sure to get it in. Surprising to some is the fact that double faults in this situation are quite common.

There are two reasons against tapping the second serve. The first

and most obvious is that you might get it shoved down your throat. The second is that it simply is not a safer shot. How

many times have you practiced your normal second serve? Prob-

ably many hundreds of times. How many times have you practiced a tap? A few times. So the probability is higher that you will be able to get in a harder serve than a softer one.

Consider another pressure situation. It’s 5-4 and your opponent’ serve. You have been returning his serve with good pace

deep down the line to his backhand most of the afternoon. Some of your returns — about twenty-five per cent — have been out, but when they have been in you have taken away his serving

advantage quite successfully. In fact you have broken his serve twice. Now, in a crucial game, you start playing it safe and floating your returns back. In all probability he rushes the net

and starts putting it away, by the second volley if not the first. Which is the safer shot: the one which has a seventy-five per cent probability of going in, and, if it does, gives you a betterthan-even chance of winning the point, or the one which has

a ninety-five per cent probability of going in, and, if it does, will almost surely result in loss of the point? The conclusion is obvious: keep driving it back, pressure or no. Sure, you're going

to feel the pressure, but the more you try to play as if you're not feeling it, the less it will affect you. Besides, if your foe

feels that you are upset, it will give him a psychological edge.

Better you tum things around and put the pressure on him.

While ideally one should bear down on every point, as there are few if any unimportant points, some points are clearly more

important than others. Naturally, if the score becomes deuce,

all subsequent points are important. The fourth point of a game is also among the most important. If it’s a 40-love or love- 40, the significance is obvious: it’s game point. If it’s 15-30, it is also a pivotal point, because if you win it, youre right back

in it at 30-all. If you lose it, your opponent has two game points and a big advantage. Similarly, 30-15 is crucial.

Also, while no game is unimportant, some games are more important than others. Once the score reaches four-all, every

game thereafter is important for that set. Before that, the seventh game is pivotal. The set could be over at 6-0, in which case there was no contest anyway. If it’s 5-1, it’s set game. If it’s 4-2,

a win would put you up 5-2 and make a comeback extremely unlikely. On the other hand, a loss of the game, bringing the score to 4-3, would nearly equalize things. Never relax, regardless of

the lead you may have. The tennis literature abounds with harrowing tales of commanding leads that were blown because

the fellow in the lead let down. At the same time, if one is knowledgeable of which are the most important points and which

are the most important games, it may help him bear down even more in these situations.

One’s temper is always a vital factor controlling performance. There is some difference of opinion as to whether getting mad

is more help or hindrance. Certainly one’s anger shouldn’t continue beyond the immediate point. If, following a point which upsets you, slamming the ball into the fence or muttering a few

oaths seems to help, fine — if that is the end of it. Remind yourself that the last point is over, worry about the next point.

Arnold Beisser points out that several world-caliber players talk to themselves unmercifully, as to another person, with

expressions such as “You're terrible”, “You're lousy”, “You can’t play”. Beisser further claims that this self-criticism and malign-

ing is almost invariably followed by improvement. It’s as if they needed this form of self-abasement to arouse them to victory.

Next time you hit a few bad shots, try leveling a vicious verbal attack upon yourself. See if it works. Just make sure you don’t take your own words too seriously!

For those of us who like to talk to ourselves, there are still other psychological techniques. Let’s say your name is Jones. Just before you serve, keep saying to yourself, “I’m big Jones.

I'm powerful Jones. I’m terrifying Jones!” This technique is

tailor-made for those who subscribe to the power-of-positive-

thinking school. One caution: don’t let anyone hear you. You

don’t want to be laughed off the court. On ground strokes you can say to yourself, “bounce, back, hit” at the exact moments that the ball bounces, you take your racket back, and you hit it,

respectively. It sounds like a trick for beginners, but it can’t hurt

and it may just help your timing.

There are a great number of environmental conditions that can be distracting: wind, sun, noise, imperfections of the court, balls that don’t bounce right, linesmen who don’t call properly, the girl in the red shorts three courts away. Anything within the

field of vision or range of hearing is a potential distractor. Have you ever noticed that on a windy day, when you're winning, the

wind doesn’t bother you a bit? But when your're losing, it really

disturbs you and you start blaming it for everything. This is a

good example of how one’s psychological reaction can be as important as the external situation. When you find yourself playing in the wind, first accept it, remind yourself that your rival

is playing under precisely the same conditions, then do every-

thing you can to allow for it. As you rally, take note of how the wind affects the flight of the ball. When you are playing against

the wind, remind yourself on every serve to throw the ball just

a little more in front of you than usual. On ground strokes say to yourself “hit harder’. When you are playing with the wind

at your back, do the reverse. When the sun is bad on one side, tell yourself that your opponent is on that side half the time too. Hit him some lobs right into the sun. Make it his problem as well as yours.

As for other distractors, it is extremely difficult not to think about that joker in the stands who howls with laughter every time you miss a shot. On the other hand, the harder you work thinking about the relevant things: the ball, the moves of your

opponent, your position on the court; and the longer you have

been working at this, the easier it will be to shut out the irrelevant.

Mental Attitude: Your Opponent

The previous section was primarily concerned with the psychological factors affecting you as a player. This section deals with ways in which psychological factors affect the guy on the other

side of the net. Begin by finding his weaknesses and capitalize

on them. If possible, scout your prospective foe before the match. During the rally before the match, hit him some deep balls and some short balls and find out how he takes them. Note whether he moves better to the left or to the right and whether he shies away from hitting his backhand. Much can be discovered during this period and during the first few games of the match. If he has a weak backhand, hit it to his backhand the

majority of the time. But by no means all the time. The element of surprise is one of the most important strategies that can be used. Hit him a forehand when he least expects it. Also a good mixture of lobs and drop shots will keep him guessing.

Speaking of guessing, there are times when a guess on your part is the percentage move. You are at the net and have just found it necessary to hit a weak short shot to mid court. You

can be passed pretty easily on either side. If you wait to see which side he’s going to pass you on, then lunge for that side,

it’s too late. Better you guess which side he’s going to hit it to, then move quickly to that side before he hits it (although not so soon that he can still change his shot). If you guess wrong, you would have lost the point anyway. If you guess right, inter-

cept his shot, and win the point, it can be worth dozens of points in psychological advantage. Nothing is more demoralizing than losing a “sure” point.

The importance of getting into shape so that you won't get unduly tired was stressed earlier. If you are tired, don't give your opponent a lift by revealing it to him. At the same time,

work on tiring him. He should be chased from side to side and made to hit several in a row on the same side, so that he will have to turn and retreat. Even more tiring than running from

side to side is running up and back. A series of alternating short angled shots and deep lobs will tire, mentally and physically, all but those in the best condition.

Some of the techniques of psyching out your foe tread a thin line with court ethics. Obviously, you don’t emit loud catcalls

while your adversary is waiting for your lob to fall, even though

it would very likely unnerve him if you did. Several top players are well known for their inordinate frequency of stopping the match and questioning the linesman’s calls. Some critics insist

that they do this in a deliberate attempt to break the opponent’s

concentration. At any rate, it seems to help these players compose themselves.

Gardner Mulloy writes of the occasion on which he was called several times for footfaulting. Righteously indignant, Mulloy felt that his opponent, not he, was guilty of footfaulting. Every time they changed ends thereafter, Mulloy remarked, “Would you please quit footfaulting!” Mulloy subsequently won the match

and the opponent claimed with some justification that Mulloy’s words, not his tennis, had defeated him. If you really want to

win and have absolutely no shame, you might repeatedly query your opponent as to why his forehand is so innocuous. You have

to be careful with whom you tangle, though. He might be the type who would counter with the observation that in your tennis outfit you are “without a doubt the most pathetic-looking creature anywhere”.

In addition to the above gross attempts to talk your antagonist out of the match, there are a few, somewhat more genteel psy-

chological practices that are or should be part of every top player's game.

Get your serves in. Many players smash the first one as hard as they can but rarely get it in. Then they tap the second one. As a result the opponent has a tap to return about eighty per

cent of the time. Better you hit both of them with moderate speed and with accuracy. There is a big psychological advantage to not having to hit your second serve. The first serve is not hit under as much pressure. Since you don’t have to get it in you

can be freer and looser when you're hitting it. Double faulting has been referred to as the cardinal sin of tennis. It should be recognized, however, that it is bound to happen on occasion. The best of the world-class players have been known to double fault. On the other hand, the psychological effects of a double fault can hardly be overemphasized. The server has a let-down.

The returmer gets a lift: he won the point without having to lift a racket. You should practice that second serve until you can get it in with reasonable pace and depth ninety per cent of the time as an absolute minimum. Then when you do double fault,

as is inevitable, remind yourself the best players do it and just keep hitting it — the same way. This is not the time to make a major change like suddenly going into an unfamiliar American twist or hitting it underhand. If your serve has been accurate

in the past, it will be accurate again.

Move in on your opponent’s second serve and let him see you moving in. Occasionally move in even closer than you think

you should and make sure he sees you then. You may not have

as much time as you would like to return it, but he will start to wonder how weak his serve really is. Once in a while “stunt”

back and forth while he is preparing to serve so that he can't be sure where you'll be for the return. (Don’t get so carried

away with your dance that you forget to watch the ball.)

Don’t telegraph your shots. Try to prepare almost exactly the same way, including position of your feet, whether you are

going to hit it cross-court or down the line. Never be dependable about what you will do in any given situation.

Try especially hard to win the long rallies. This will tend to create a sense of futility in your rival: that “all that work was for nothing”. A long rally, by the way, is an excellent place to throw in an unexpected drop shot or lob.

Break the momentum. If you have lost several points in a row or several games in a row, walk slowly and deliberately when changing courts. Tie your shoelace. Carefully examine your racket strings. Bounce the ball eight or ten times before serving. These perfectly legal practices, analogous to calling time out in

basketball, may cool off your adversary as well as allow you to

collect yourself.

Be a pokerface. If you are nervous, angry, depressed, or elated, conceal it. When you serve an ace or hit a brilliant passing shot, even though it may ‘shock you to the core, act nonchalant.

Let him think that this was the most routine of shots.

Try the techniques I have offered and some of them will become a regular part of your game. One must keep in mind, however, the wide range of individual differences in tempera-

ment and in style. Not everyone will benefit from talking to

himself. While some people are better off exploding all over the court when they get mad, others do better holding it in.

Find out what works for you.

Let me close with a re-emphasis upon what I consider to be the most important psychological factor in winning at tennis:

watching the ball. If you keep your eye and mind glued on that little white (or yellow) object, it will help you slaughter your opponents, to murder them! After all, that’s what this

gentleman’s game is all about.

James Johnson stroked the ball across the net to his opponent with casual ease. As an old timer, experienced in the ways of

tennis, he neither slammed the ball, angled it, tried many over-

head shots, rushed the net, nor left the base line at the center

stripe when returning shots. He was loosening up for his match. His baggy warm-up suit made him look even more stocky than his 70-inch, 200-pound frame. His bald head and bifocal lenses

accentuated his sixty years. His opponent on the other side of

the net was a true contrast. He was young, lithe, and rangy. He kept banging away with great vigor at each ball hit to him. His two-handed backhand belted the ball. When at the service

line, he cannonballed each stroke as his long hair dangled, and swished in all directions. He emphasized his youthful nineteen years. By mutual consent, the warm-ups ceased. The racket was

spun for choice of serve and side. They retired to their respective baselines. There was, however, a difference in individual approach. As Johnson took off his trousers and coat, he seemed to

shed twenty or thirty pounds. He looked big and burly, like the football player he once was. He strode to the center of the court, quickly jumped over the net and half-ran, half-walked to the baseline, calling out a sharp “let’s go”. The sight of a baldheaded, heavy-muscled, net-jumping opponent, albeit sixty, shook up the young opponent so much that the older man quickly garnered three games before the long-haired one realized that he was being psyched.

With very few exceptions, there are few action sports involving direct confrontation in which psyche plays such a major role as in tennis. It is estimated that in an ordinary tennis match between two (or four) opponents, psyching plays a sixty per cent role. There are few sports where téte-a-téte confrontations not

only pit skills and natural athletic talent, but where mental and

emotional attitudes play such a vital role. The ability of one player to disarrange, disparage, discourage, irritate, frustrate,

disorganize, and break the concentration of his opponent, be-

comes a major determining factor in the outcome of a close match. This action, when deliberately employed by the experienced player (or inadvertently by a neophyte when playing

a skilled competitor) is called psyche. Properly applied, psyche is intended to disrupt the plan of action, natural reflexes, or expected motions of his competitor across the court.

Deliberate psyching must not break the fairly rigid rules of play established by the International or U.S. Lawn Tennis

Association. In tournament matches, the number of linesmen will vary according to the importance of the specific contest,

but an umpire who is the last word on the court (he can refer certain problems to the outside-of-the-court referee or tournament chairman) will generally not brook continued or obvious

attempts at psyche by a player (especially one known to be a chronic “bad actor’). Therefore, the psyche needs finesse, new wrinkles, different innovations and a careful technique.

There are many ways to psyche an opponent. A trite method is to delay a game due to an alleged injury. It will upset his rival if a player feigns a turned ankle, a leg cramp, or heat stroke. There’s a natural human tendency for a player to be concemed over his opponent’s injury or illness. Even if his concentration is not altogether wrecked, the “well” player will still feel un-

easy. If the alleged injury is due to being struck by a ball, or a fall into the net or posts or fence, the opponent will have a normal tendency to slacken his own attack purely out of sympathy. Another ploy for the player who is tired, hot, and behind in the

score may be to claim injury, ilmess or need for a medical examination from an official in order to gain a short rest.

Also, there is always equipment-adjustment psyche. The com-

monest way is to stop play by bending over to tie or untie,

and tie again a dragging shoelace. Or readjust clothes, or put on a cap (or take one off) or put on (or take off) sweat bands from forehead or wrist. Or he might take an extra moment at

the sidelines to wipe his brow, sip a drink, dry his hands on

the towel, or change his shirt or get another tennis racket, while his opponent is raring to go and champing at the bit along his own. baseline.

There is always a way to make an opponent cringe. A topseeded player meeting a rival in the early rounds introduces himself modestly by using his given name only. This emphasizes

his established greatness, since he correctly assumes that every-

body knows him. The use of his given name tends to put down his rival, usually an unknown or an inexperienced tournament

player. The seeded great one further cows his intended rival | by calling him by his last name, but during the match he might throw his young rival off-guard and off-pace even more by con-

descending to question a close call by saying, “What did you

call that, Bill?” If it’s a really close one in the absence of an

attending linesman, the great one will get the benefit of the option.

Calling out at intervals during a match which is not being umpired, can be a contrived bit of psyche. This is particularly

upsetting when the call is made just as the serve is about to be

Only very recently the latent potentialities of the human mind have been recognized. The brain is now acknowledged as a human machine impassioned with a great power to exert both

intrinsic and extrinsic controls. In its simplest form the phenomenon is called mind over matter, and in its most complex form “cyberation”, a term that implies self-regulation of activ-

ity. It is a process of conscious control over our own behavior

in relation to the objects and events around us.

Historically, the human mind was seen as mystical. A museum piece. A cosmic playground beyond scrutiny. But today the

electrical business of the brain has been opened to investiga-

tion, and now it has become clear that its goings on are in inter-

action with events far outside of itself. And, while the mind may of course be influenced by events, the more fascinating

aspect is that the mind may also connive to exert its own influence on events. Moreover, it is both subservient to, and mas-

This interaction between mind and body has been proved beyond doubt by recent medical findings that a great many bodily malfunctions are apparently prompted by psychological

stresses. Thus, the term psychosomatic illness has become household. But, if mental processes can cause detrimental changes in the body, it becomes possible to anticipate that, through training, the mind could also effect positive changes. And indeed, it has been done. Some persons with high blood pressure have learned to reduce that pressure by thought alone. Others with racing hearts have been able to slow them down. Epileptics

have had some success in suppressing the abnormal electrical brain activity characteristic of their condition. And insomniacs

have been taught to voluntarily create the brain waves typical

of sleep.

an expectation and anticipation of failures. By contrast, if the data we give to our cybemetic servant is positive, our self-image

Through the years that I have played and taught the game of tennis I have gradually come to realize that there are two kinds of players. I’m not referring to good ones and bad ones,

Mental Practice of Tennis Skills

The second way in which cybermetic programming can be used to improve your tennis skill may seem to be, at first examination, a bit mystical.

Recently the field of physical education has put forth a theory which contends that merely thinking about doing something

creates an electrical patterning in the muscles that would be involved were the person actually doing it, even though that person may be sitting in a chair at the moment. For example, when one sees oneself going through the sequence of hitting a

tennis ball, minute electrical action potentials which would be

generated in the actual overt act are duplicated, although on

a smaller scale. This mental picturing of an act, properly directed, can thus be used to aid in attaining a coordinated performance of that act.

Such mental imagery has been used for centuries, and has been scientifically researched at least as early as 1890. But it

has been only very recently that the full possibilities of its application have begun to be realized. While controlled laboratory investigations have been taking place to discover the potential, popular books have been acquainting the general public with the basic idea. The hypothesis in all cases is that the individual induce a sort of conscious self-hypnosis, or self-suggestion, in which he

actually visualizes himself attaining a certain goal, or performing a certain act, and that this effort will increase his chances

of it actually happening. It is a procedure of establishing an objective, a clear-cut goal, and then creating a mental picture, through the use of imagination, of the attainment of that goal. It consists of setting rational goals, practising and experiencing

mental imagery of those goals, and then consciously acting toward the attainment of them.

Now, teachers of tennis, or of any physical activity, have traditionally thought of practice only in terms of overt performance. The amount of learning and improvement in an activity was considered to be in direct relationship with the time spent

in physical practice. A sort of leaning by doing. But recent research dealing with cybemetic possibilities has indicated that

improvement in a physical activity may be brought about merely by thinking about performing that activity.

The literature has used the term “mental practice” to denote this introspective, or covert practice of a physical task. It involves conceptualizing, actually creating a mental picture, of a

physical performance. It means, for example, actually seeing

yourself going through the motion of serving a tennis ball, or hitting a smooth backhand, or charging the net for a point-

ending volley. And it is all based on the now recognized close relationship between mind and body.

For some time it has been known that physical performance increases the electrical activity of the brain. And it was also known that, even though a person might be resting, he could

increase the electrical activity of his muscles simply by concentrating on a mental problem. What is now being realized is that

it is possible to direct one’s mental activity to produce increased efficiency in muscular responses.

All of us have used mental practice to some extent. We may visualize ourselves buying a new car, or getting a promotion in our job, or being an outstanding athlete. How much this has

affected or directed our energies is a matter of speculation, for in most cases it is just daydreaming. Perhaps more exemplary

of mental practice is the type of visualization we do when trying to figure out a mechanical puzzle, or when we are making

a home improvement and we can see the final product even

as we review our mistakes instead of using mental practice to

There are two very good reasons why mental practice should be utilized by everyone who wishes to improve his tennis game.

Most importantly, it will lead to a better game. And secondly, it may partially make up for the lack of time we all have for physical practice of tennis skills.

I should point out that I am in no way proposing mental rehearsal of tennis as a complete substitute for overt physical practice. But by creating a habit of thinking about and analyzing

your game you will be able to ensure a greater use of your own ability. Research verifies this. Controlled studies of mental practice of sports skills have shown that it is beneficial to the execution of that skill. Let me give you a few examples.

In one study, three groups of high school boys, each group equal in ability, were given the following variables. One group practised twenty-five basketball free-throws daily; another group

did nothing; and the third group came to the gymnasium, but sat down and imagined themselves shooting twenty-five free-

throws. After fourteen days the groups were tested. The physical practice group showed a twenty-five percent improvement, the

resting group showed no improvement, and the group which used mental practice showed an amazing twenty-four per cent

improvement. Similar results, all reported in recognized scientific journals, have been shown in studies applying mental practice to

gymnastic skills, throwing for accuracy, swimming, and such novel skills as juggling. All have found mental practice to be beneficial to performance.

Two studies have been reported in which mental practice was applied to tennis. One study used 183 male college students

as subjects, and the other used 75 female college students. Both studies divided their subjects into three equated groups, then had one group practise forehand and backhand drives daily,

another group do nothing, and the third group only mentally practise hitting forehand and backhand drives. As might be

expected, the group that practised physically in each study showed considerable improvement, while the groups that did nothing showed no improvement. But the amazing finding was,

that in both studies, the groups which practised mentally im-

proved almost as much as the physical practice group, even

though they never once touched a tennis racket during the

practice sessions.

But now, before we accept on blind faith that mental practice can help our game, we must establish some guidelines for its use. To be effective, mental practice must be properly employed. One cannot just sit idly by, think about becoming,a Ken

Rosewall or a Billie Jean King, and have it happen. That would just be hallucinating. But by more closely examining those re-

search studies that have employed mental practice, we can come up with some realistic conclusions as to how it can best be used.

There is not one bit of evidence to show that mental practice is superior to physical practice, but test results have shown that mental practice is most effective when used in combination with overt practise. When, therefore, one practises both physically

and mentally at a sports skill, improvement can be expected to be over and above what would be accomplished by use of either one alone.

But there is one very important consideration before anyone can use mental practice. This cybemetic-type of skill rehearsal

can be used only by persons who have had enough experience in the sport to be able to recognize what is as opposed to what is not effective technique. An inexperienced person would be

unable to know what to focus his attention on, sort of like trying to mentally practise flying an airplane if you’ve never seen an

instrument panel. Unless you know the mechanics which produce effective tennis strokes, it is impossible to mentally practise those

mechanics. Conceivably, if wrong technique were mentally practised, wrong technique would be leamed.

Then, with the recognition that mental practice can result in improvements in skill — that it does produce electrical activity in the muscles and probably creates a neurological channeling

of the movements involved in a skill — the question is how to properly apply its potential. For the many of us wanting to im-

prove our tennis play, what is the best way to use this mental practice?

It should first be stated that no one has all the answers yet. There is no scientific knowledge as to whether there are differ-

ent kinds of mental practice. Nor does anyone know exactly how much time should be spent in mental practice as compared to

physical practice. But it appears that it can benefit every tennis player alive, within the following guidelines.

First, and perhaps most important, the mental practice should actually produce a kinetic feeling of the overt movement. For example, if you were mentally practising serving, you should

genuinely feel yourself going through the motion, sometimes trying it with your eyes open and at other times with your eyes closed. This does require some mental discipline, but is is important since when you overtly perform the serve you do not

actually see it, therefore the mental practice should be directed to the feeling of the serve.

Normally the mental practice should be visualized at the same speed at which you would outwardly perform the skill. See yourself hitting a backhand at the same speed that you physi-

cally perform the act. But, as a change of routine, occasionally visualize yourself doing the skill in slow motion. In this manner

you are able to see, feel, and analyze every aspect of the skill.

The whole of the skill should be mentally rehearsed, not just a portion of it. This includes any activity leading up to the actual execution of the skill. As an example, suppose you were to mentally practise the serve. Your mental imagery should then

include any readiness movements you make before you serve,

such as adjusting your foot position behind the base line, bouncing the ball, taking aim, etc. Some persons suggest that you might even visualize yourself walking up to the line after the previous point. And then the mental practice should take you through the entire execution of the serve, including the pictur-

ing of the flight of the ball as it leaves your racket.

Keep the mental practice sessions fairly short, perhaps no more than five minutes, but have the sessions often. An excellent time to inject short mental practice sessions is while you are

reading a description of a tennis skill. Stop your reading frequently, visualize the skill being discussed, and then return to the reading.

Utilize mental practice immediately before, during, and after a tennis match. Flash a mental picture of every skill in your mind just before you execute it. And after each point quickly

review your performance, analyze it, and form a picture of your adjustments, if any are needed. Assuming that you may never have actually thought about mental practice before, you prob-

ably have been doing this one all along, although subconsciously.

Several players I know will, after they have hit a poor shot, mimic the swing they should have used instead. What they are

doing, although they may not have actually thought about it, is kinetically rehearsing the correct swing instead of remem-

bering the poor one. And some players will verbally remind themselves throughout a match to perform certain basic funda-

mentals which should be second nature in their game.

Use mental practice not only for improvement in the physical skills of tennis, but also to rehearse the strategy of the game.

Often, when a player shows an uncanny ability to seemingly

always make the correct response in terms of ball placement

and his own court positioning, he is thought to have good “game sense”. Yet very likely what he has done is to mentally consider the possibilities in each situation and to make his choices on the basis of probability. A good baseball outfielder does this when

he mentally speculates on the various possibilities of a given

situation — the number of outs, the speed of the runners on base, where the ball is likely to be hit, ete — and then should

the ball be hit to him, he is much more likely to throw to the proper base. Tennis provides this same opportunity in that there

is a pause between each point at which time you can conceive of your strategy for the next point. For example, in receiving the serve, you might think of where you want to place your

return, depending on where it is hit to you, before your opponent makes the delivery. And throughout the match you should

cognitively review the relative success of your game plan, and make changes as may be necessary to increase the chances of future success.

Mental practice, then, can be utilized to program success into your tennis game. It is a preparation for physical performance,

and it is vital that all players who wish to improve their game learn to incorporate this cybernetic imagery into their practice

schemes. Our brains cannot really tell the difference between

a real. experience and one which is vividly imagined, consequently mental rehearsal of a physical skill, properly used, can indeed program us for attaining smooth, easy, coordinated, and successful performance.

In conclusion, few people realize the potential of the mental side of tennis. Mostly, practise of the skills has been limited to the physical. Yet there is a great cybernetic “operator” in each of our brains that can program us for success or for failure. It

can transcend sensory experiences, becoming automated to produce more effective overt performance. And rather than being

a passive, receiving system, cybemetics is an active, producing

system.

These are the points to remember:

1. Have definite, attainable goals in mind for your tennis progress. And as you approach the achievement of those goals,

re-establish them at even higher (but attainable) levels.

2. Mentally picture yourself accomplishing your objectives. Try to actually generate the feeling of successful tennis.

3. Use this mental picturing to practise the actual physical skills, again trying to see and feel yourself performing those skills.

4. Concern yourself with positives about your game instead of negatives. If you cannot avoid worrying, at least worry about

a positive aspect, such as whether you will be able to get seventyfive per cent of your first serves in, rather than worrying about whether you will get any in.

5. Remember that your attitude about your own ability to perform, your tennis self-image, is a product of your own mind.

The only thoughts you have about your ability are the ones you have put there yourself.

Winning and Losing Tendencies in Tennis

In tennis, in sport, and in life, there are many many people who fade in and out, over-achieve and under-achieve, win some

and lose some. For a thousand different reasons they never seem to be anything other than commonplace. However, at each end of the spectrum, there are those individuals who consistently

come in last, and others who seem to emerge time after time at the head of the pack. It is the characters of these people on the

extremes that I wish to inspect. It is this characteristic toward winning or losing that often completely dominates a player, a

whole team, and in the end, the outcome of the competition.

Tennis is a sport in which being a winner is very important

due to the individual nature of the game and because there

are no ties or draws; only a win or a loss can result. Through tennis we will look at the phenomena of success- and failure-

Perceptual psychology has been summarized by Combs, Avila and Purkey, (1972) when they state that:

havior is the function of the perceptions existing for an individual at the moment of his behaving, especially those per-

ceptions he has of himself and of the world in which he is operating. Each of us does at every moment what seems

appropriate at that instant. When the nature of perceptions is understood, even the weirdest behavior becomes comprehensible.”

Obviously this statement expresses a subjective view of reality. We see things in a particular way, accept this as the way things really are, and act in accordance with this view. Thus a person’s

actions at a particular point in time are a function of all those perceptions which exist for him at that time. Perceptions, of

course, used in this sense signify “meanings”, the “peculiar significance of an event for the person experiencing it.” Therefore,

a person behaves in a certain way because this makes sense for him in the light of his current situation.

For example, one player is being badly beaten by another

whom he considers inferior in ability, and it looks as though the better player is not going to have much chance of pulling

the game out of the fire. It would be a severe blow to his self-

concept to perceive himself as being beaten by a player he regards as inferior, therefore he changes his perception of the match so that it no longer seems important to win and he can explain away his defeat afterwards by saying he was not feeling well or just couldn’t get interested.

haven't arrived yet, but I can enjoy this point. I'll just concen-

closely, stroke it smoothly and feel the satisfaction of moving

around the court with a definite purpose in mind. This is my goal — to play this point well without thought to anything else.” By narrowing perception in this way, much of the psychological strain is lifted. The despair of facing a big deficit tends to be forgotten and the threat to self is replaced by a single minded

concentration on a sub-goal which is relatively easy to attain. It seems too easy, doesn’t it, and, of course, it may not always work but I can draw on my own experience as an indication that it does often produce miraculous changes. from the global concept of self and the match as a whole, to the micro concept of one point at a time. Another beneficial

change might involve the type of game being played. If the player is being beaten playing his normal game, a variation is indicated. Yet if he persists in thinking that he is superior and

can win doing what he has always done, he is likely to be disappointed. A change in perception is required along the following lines. In today’s situation John X is playing well and I am playing badly. What can I do to alter this pattern? Although it

will be painful for me, I must give up playing my normal game

because it is losing. Instead, I must focus my perception on the

game of John X, my opponent. What shots is he missing? How must I change my game to give him more of these? If this means giving up some of my favorite shots and playing a sort of game

cannot take place until it has been preceded by a change in

perception.

The same thing is true of the player who gets himself more and more upset through his errors. Every time he makes an error he blames himself for it and thus becomes increasingly

tense and anxious. His perception here is that every point he loses is due to his own poor play. But surely this is rather unreasonable. Some credit must go to his opponent for putting him in a situation where he is likely to make errors. If he can

change his perception so that he sees his opponent as being responsible for winning points rather than he himself losing them, his behavior will change in that he will probably become

more relaxed and less anxious. An attitude of concentration

upon doing as well as possible and giving full credit to the opponent for his good play replaces that of soul-destroying castigation of oneself for one’s errors. Nowhere is such a change more immediately noticeable than when one tries to hit passing

shots against a net player. The net player’s greatest advantage is the pressure he exerts upon the baseliner to look up before he hits, a procedure almost guaranteed to produce error. If the player endeavoring to hit the passing shot perceives it as neces-

sary to look up to ascertain his opponent’s position at the net before he strokes the ball, and makes an error, he blames himself trenchantly. After this happens a number of times he per-

ceives himself as unable to beat a net rusher. Yet, if he changed his perception the whole pattern of his play would change. For

example, he could argue that he really knows where the net

man will be, for the good player follows in behind the line of his approach shot. And if his opponent is not a good player,

he probably doesn’t have much to worry about anyway. Once

he perceives that it is unnecessary to look up to check on the net man’s position, he can decide where he is going to hit the

ball and then concentrate on playing his shot as well as pos-

sible. If the net player cuts it off and hits a winner, that is too

bad, but is not nearly as upsetting as having given the point

away with an error.

The whole concept of what is meant by exerting pressure during a tennis match is also a matter of perception. If one asked most players what they meant when they said they put pressure

on an opponent, they would probably answer in terms of hitting

the ball hard and storming the net. To the man who doesn’t play well at the net and who is uncomfortable in that position, this is a rather discouraging view of “exerting pressure”, for it

seems to preclude the possibility that he will ever be able to effect this most desirable exercise. But there are other ways of

perceiving the situation, for there are many ways of putting pressure on an opponent which are just as difficult for him, and

much safer for you. For example, when you have a clear lead in a set and an error will cost your opponent an important point, you place him under great strain by simply keeping the ball in play, swinging it around the court to keep him moving but

always giving him the chance to err while you leave yourself with a wide margin for error. By varying your speed, direction

and spin, always within a safe margin, you place considerable pressure upon your opponent because he knows an error will

be costly and you are obviously not going to let him off the hook by making mistakes yourself.

Pressure, too, can be perceived as a matter of playing to the score, for not all points in a game, or a set, or a match are of the same value. The player who can perceive these vital points

and exert more effort to win them is actually exposing his opponent to considerable pressure. To illustrate, in any game winning the first point is important because of the psychological

lift one gets from a good start, but it is the third and fifth points which are really vital. If you lead 30-0, winning the third point for 40-0 will normally give you the game. At 15-15, this point

gives you the edge while, if you are down 0-30, winning it keeps you in the game. The fifth point, if you work out the possibilities, is similarly crucial. Within a set, the seventh game

is vital, giving you the set at 6-1, a commanding lead for 5-2, the edge at 4-3, or at least keeping you in the game at 3-4.

Other important games are the fourth and the ninth, and extra effort should be expended at these times.

It does seem clear, from illustrations such as these, that the way one plays a tennis match will be greatly influenced by

one’s perception of the situation, and whether one is able to change such perceptions to adapt to changing conditions.

In one way, this approach through changed perception relates to Frankl’s logotherapy which was, of course, evolved in a very

different context. Frankl has argued that, to survive, a person needs a purpose in life. If he cannot accept a ready-made pur-

pose, then he must invent a meaning for his life and the logotherapy model would help here by showing such a person how

he might turn life’s everyday events into challenges to be faced.

One could also perceive a tennis match in such terms, either on a macrolevel of the match as a whole, or on the microlevel

of one point at a time. If one’s present poor form is a result of a “couldn’t-care-less” attitude to the game, by altering this perception to one of seeing the challenge generated, one creates

a more purposeful attitude and changed behavior. Here is a task, winning a match, or enjoying the experience of playing a

game, or any other way one likes to phrase it, and the successful meeting of this challenge will make one’s life more meaningful

through the sense of purpose thus engendered. This might sound far-fatched but, again based on personal experience, I know that it can work.

Probably, many of the points I have made in this chapter have occurred to readers before. What I have endeavoured to do is to draw them together within the framework of perceptual psy-

chology, indicating that if one is able to deliberately alter the way one perceives the activity of playing tennis matches, one

can, to a large extent, control one’s behavior and by so doing improve one’s chances of success as a tennis player.

Choking up, clutching, freezing, clutched-up or simply “the choke” is that sickening experience that occurs when a player

gets so tense that his basic strokes disintegrate, his coordination

and timing are terrible, and his mental and physical abilities deteriorate to the point where he ends up losing to an opponent of substantially inferior ability. This miserable state of affairs

is something that has been experienced in one form or another by nearly every serious tennis player. The ability to avoid this

situation, or overcome it once it has begun, might be the difference between a successful competitor and a mediocre one, hence, a consideration of the most common factors involved and some techniques for meeting these, will be helpful for those who want to improve their game.

The term “choke” may derive from the fact that under conditions of high tension, one’s pharynx becomes extremely dry

as the salivary and mucus-secreting glands are inhibited, and the ensuing absence of lubrication, together with heightened

tension of the throat muscles, makes it nearly impossible to swallow — hence a choking sensation is experienced. Moreover, under conditions of high arousal, sensations of indigestion, nau-

sea and imminent regurgitation often occur, lending further

support to the appropriateness of the colloquialism “choking up”, used to describe blocked or inhibited performance under con-

ditions of stress.

The most important questions to be answered for the serious player regarding the choke are: (1) what are the environmental

and psychological conditions that precipitate this phenomenon,

and (2) what steps can be taken to prevent it or overcome it once it has occurred?

Conditions that Cause the Choke

The choke, which is a part of every serious player's experience, is different from similar symptoms that can occur due to

lack of concentration, physical fatigue, or overconfidence. While separate and distinguishable from these, there is, nevertheless,

an inter-relationship with them and, as we shall see, it is impossible to understand or intelligently discuss the choke without understanding the optimal conditions of psychological pre-

paredness for a match.

of emotion expressed through physiological arousal. Psycholo-

General Techniques for Overcoming the Choke

For the player who is interested in learning to avoid or overcome the problems of choke when entering new levels or areas of competition, two basic strategies are available.

The first can be described as a kind of “crash program” and is often followed by younger players entering into serious competition for the first time. The strategy here is simply to enter

a great number and great variety of tournaments as rapidly

as the schedule allows. Following this procedure most players will become desensitized to the stress of play in new surround-

ings in four to six months. At first the circuit may seem like a nightmare with strange environments, new people and the in-

evitable defeats repeating themselves over and over. But in a

short while, other players become familiar, the courts of play begin to have recognizable similarities and, most important of

all, different styles of play are faced repeatedly and adjustment to the major styles is begun. After playing against a variety of styles and under diverse conditions, there simply are no really novel conditions that can be encountered. The player finds that he is mentally as well as physically “tournament tough.”

While this strategy may work well for younger players, they do run the risk of becoming over-saturated with the constant

competition, and experience a subsequent loss of the fine edge that is necessary for top performance. The older player who wishes to enter new levels of competition is even more likely

to tire of the rapid pace and constant strain of successive tournaments and might do well to consider the second strategy.

The second major method for adjusting to the stress of entering new levels of competition is to utilize a method of successive approximations. Here, the player should first attempt to deter-

mine which factors are most likely to make him “freeze up” in a match. Then he should place himself in situations (either within or out of formal toumaments) in which he will be exposed to gradually increasing amounts of the troublesome ingredients

of each situation.

For example, let’s say that spectators are especially troublesome to John Smith and cause him to play about 20% below par. John can set up situations where he will play before a

handful of people and gradually work up to as large a group

as he is ever likely to perform before. The first steps can be engineered by intentionally choosing a certain court or a certain

time of day for his practice matches. At most clubs and public facilities it is a simple matter to pick a time and location which will virtually guarantee that a few spectators will be present.

Then the situation can be made slightly more difficult by playing in somewhat more popular locales, or in toumaments with a larger draw.

Under these conditions of gradually increasing exposure to the relevant stresses, the player will find that a constant low level of tension may accompany the progression up the ladder

of difficult situations. If, at some point, he is suddenly flooded with excessive anxiety and a corresponding deterioration of play, he should retreat temporarily to a slightly less stressful set of

conditions, wait until he is thoroughly adjusted to those conditions and then proceed to the next level of difficulty.

This same method can be applied to whatever factors are most bothersome to the player — whether it be slow play, different

court surfaces, or new surroundings. The player, at his own pace, can choose the next grade of difficulty and play under those conditions until adaptation results.

These general approaches to overcoming anxiety in novel situations are basically long-term strategies to be applied over one or several seasons of play. At another level, there are general

strategies that can be applied in an individual tournament or

even in a single match.

One of the things to avoid when entering a situation where you think there is a chance of choking is a long period of soli-

tude just before an important match. While you may have heard

of certain pros “psyching themselves up” by intense solitary

concentration before a match, this is not likely to work for the novice. Instead, if you must wait alone for an hour or two

before a match, you will begin to fret and worry and build up

tension which can only be destructive when you get on the court. All the other players (even ones you have defeated be-

fore) look vastly superior out there on the courts; you'll look

at a crowd of spectator-players and imagine they will all be critically watching you; and your every ache and pain and questionable stroke will be etched vividly in your mind. By the

time you get on the court, you may feel you are encased in a block of ice.

If this happens to you, there are at least two things you can do to help prevent it. You can mix socially with the other players, or, if this isn’t easy or convenient, bring a friend or two with you to the match. The mere necessity of making conversation and roaming the grounds with company will help distract you and keep you from dwelling on the imagined disasters

in store for you.

A second preventative measure is to avoid spending a lot of time waiting for a match at the tournament site. Usually a telephone call to the tournament headquarters will tell you if your

match will go on when scheduled, or if there will be a delay, and you can then arrive a comfortable ten or fifteen minutes before you go on. If you are in between matches, check with the tournament chairman and be sure he agrees to your re-

appearing in an hour for your next match, and then take a Coke break or go lie down for an hour and rest.

If you get on the court and feel a rising tide of tension, there are still several things you can do. Some people, after clutching up and blowing a couple of easy put-aways, can take a few deep breaths, tell themselves “it doesn’t mean that much” and “stop

trying so hard” and work themselves out of it. The ensuing relaxation can have an immediate positive effect. Others may work to get themselves slightly physically fatigued; to some extent there is a basic incompatibility between high tension and

the muscular relaxation of physical fatigue. I know one person who will do calisthenics and run vigorously after loose balls

at the outset of a match just to reduce the level of muscle ten-

sion and anxiety.

Occasionally during a match a crisis situation will develop that can lead to a choke reaction even when normal play has preceded it. Currently this is most likely to occur during the tie-breaker used in many tournaments. Most of the techniques

mentioned above cannot be applied in this context. One approach that can be used is this gambit that I learned from my partner as we played the mixed doubles semi-finals of the 1970

Denver Starter Tournament. We were in the third set and had fallen behind 4-2 in a nine point tie-breaker. My partner, Mona, was fairly new at tennis but an experienced competitor in other

sports. The crucial seventh, eighth and ninth points were hers to serve. On the first point she drove in a clean winner on her

first serve; on the second point she missed the first serve but hit a hard second serve that was returned as an easy set-up to me, and on the third point the first serve was again blasted in for

a clean winner to give us the tie-breaker and the match. I tumed around in complete amazement and asked her where in blazes

she had found the nerve to do that. She coolly told me that she simply figured that there was no way we were going to win three points in a row if she let tension rule and “babied” her

serves in. So, assuming the match was lost if she succumbed to the natural tendency to tighten up and play to avoid the double fault, she turned around and did exactly the opposite.

Naturally, this approach is not going to work every time; but if you play “choked”, you very seldom have a chance of winning. If you are way down in a match — a 4-2 tie-breaker or a

5-2 set score against you — you might as well figure all is lost unless you go for broke. You might as well try anything that gives you even a slim chance of winning the match.

Specific Factors that May Cause Choke and Methods of Combating Them

When a player is already highly aroused by the existing situation, any slightly unexpected or discomfitting experience can push him over the top of that inverted “U” performance curve.

Here are some of the more common ones.

Slow Play. One of the most common causes, especially in young and eager players, is the encounter with a slow and methodical

opponent. Often it is not just slow play per se, but also unusually long intervals between points and between games can drive an

eager competitor into a choke reaction. Every individual who wants to be successful at this game must learn to cope with this problem. One thing that can facilitate this adjustment enor-

mously is to set up a situation where this style of play is en-

countered, and then try to slow down your own pace of play to match that of your opponent in between points, reminding

yourself that these intervals are totally irrelevant to the out-

come of the match. However, once a point is begun, you must mentally pick yourself up to an optimal level of concentration

and pace. In other words, your petty impatience with the overall pace of the match has to be differentiated from the playing of an individual point; the slowness of play must not be allowed to blunt your competitive edge once the point is under way.

If one can achieve a positive experience or two — meaning a well-earned win against a deserving opponent — using this tech-

nique, the chances are it can be recalled and used again when slow play is encountered in more trying circumstances.

New Courts. Another common contributing factor to choking is

the sudden disintegration of one’s game that occurs when a player enters competition on a different playing surface and in new sur-

roundings. The first thing noticed may be less efficient ground strokes, difficulty in judging the speed of the ball, or problems in

estimating the depth of a lob. The first thing to note is that these

are very real problems, not just a matter of temporary excitement or your psychological orientation. Less experienced players have a tendency to underestimate or ignore these problems dur-

ing warm-up, figuring they will iron themselves out as the match

progresses. In actual fact, they may well get worse; the lob continues to fly long and the forehand is repeatedly contacted late; then, as you fall behind, tension builds, hasty and varied corrections are attempted until even more basic strokes are lost and your game is a shambles.

What went wrong? The problem started with an underestimation of the importance of familiarity with the courts of play. Even experienced tournament players appear to slight this factor, ignoring or dis-

counting the fact that there are major differences in background, visual characteristics, wind currents and other subtle factors from one set of courts to another. We seldom witness players working out on the chosen courts a day or two in advance of

a tournament. But the experienced player will keenly observe

every nuance of difference in the play of the ball during warmup, and work diligently to diagnose any problems and make

corrections.

The mistake that starts a less experienced player on the way to chokesville is the naive belief that everything will straighten itself out as play progresses. This illusion and the contrasting

disillusionment that soon follows, combine to provide the unsettling psychological shock that results in the final “coming

apart” of his game.

Spectators. One of the most frequently encountered “freezefactors” is the presence of spectators. Not only does their appear-

ance lead to a self-conscious tightening up by players who are not familiar with playing in front of observers, but there are additional distracting and complicating factors that usually accompany the

presence of spectators. Those who are watching a match are never just anonymous people to the player on the court. Spec-

tators invariably contribute at least one of these influences: (a) they are almost always aficionados and therefore critics of the

game and, (b) they may be friends and fellow competitors or, alternatively, representatives and friends of your opponent, and

(c) spectators nearly always provide a set of non-personal distracting influences that can affect your game.

For some players the greatest tension is induced by having friends or fellow players observing. This may be especially true

if one has recently joined a club and is eager to impress his new acquaintances. This is a simple case where “trying too hard”

can tempt a player to hit his serve a little harder than usual, bringing his 40% first-serve rate down to 20% and increasing

his double faults drastically. It can lead to him trying to hit flashy cross-court backhand winners when he doesn’t really have

the shot mastered. After a game or two played in this manner, it may develop that the player cannot return to his basic strokes

and game, and the total cost of the “trying to impress” choke takes its toll.

A somewhat more complex episode occurs when one journeys

to another setting for an inter-city or inter-club match of some

kind. In this context the observers may be biased in an overt

and vocal manner. The first time or two it occurs, hearing applause only for your opponent’s good shots can be an unnerving

experience. Occasionally, the partisanship may go well beyond one-sided applause and approval and involve ill-mannered and

derisive cheering when you muff an easy shot. Although rare, this writer has witnessed such events more than once during inter-city matches and the effect has usually been devastating on the quality of the visitors’ play.

Inevitably there will be some conversation and some movement among the spectators and the novice in this situation may

be easily thrown off his game if he allows himself to be distracted by this activity. He may begin to catch parts of a sentence of conversation here and there and try to make sense out of them, or he may simply become generally distracted from his game by an over-awareness of the spectators’ presence.

What is the cure for all these difficulties that spectators can present to the inexperienced competitor? As discussed earlier

in this chapter, a player can attempt to desensitize himself to

these circumstances, or he can build up resistance to it via the method of successive approximations. But prior to the appli-

cation of either of these general principles, a player needs some device to face the immediate situation the first time or two he

comes up against the specter of the spectators.

Unlike the other problems we have described, there is almost no recourse to direct action against this problem. If the situation

is really desperate, an appeal to the tournament chairman (or

his equivalent) might result in a request from him to the crowd

to be quieter or perhaps even a change of court could be arranged. But these steps are unlikely to be taken and then only in extreme cases. For the circumstances more commonly en-

countered, the only recourse is to attempt to shut out the dis-

traction of the crowd, ignore them as completely as possible,

and concentrate entirely on the match. If your focus of attention is fully on the ball at all times of actual play, the crowd noises

at these times will actually seem to disappear and will cease to be a factor detracting from your play.

The “Father of Psychoanalysis,” Dr. Sigmund Freud, very wisely suggested to prospective scientists that they should only

A Psychological Glimpse at the Game of Tennis

From time immemorial men have engaged in athletic contests, pitting themselves against another man or men in competition. The motivations for athletic contests are many, in-

cluding the thrill of competition, the satisfaction of winning, the strengthening of one’s body, winning recognition, and a host of other rewards. The excitement of competition and contest with

another human being is certainly one of the joys of the game of tennis. The kind of athletic activity represented by tennis involves some very special physical changes in the body which

are part of the body’s preparation to meet stress and challenge.

It is these bodily changes that occur in connection with the game of tennis which can either enhance or detract from our game. When we are engaged in physically competitive activities, chemical changes help our bodies to deal with the in-

creased demands which we place on them. In addition to the physical changes which tennis involves, there occur emotional

changes which relate to our desire to win and/or to make a good showing. Both these physical and emotional changes re-

athletic competition. Many of us are able to achieve just enough increase in our state of body arousal so that physically and emotionally we perform better while playing a sport like tennis.

However, it is possible to become aroused, keyed up and excited excessively to the point where it begins to hurt your tennis game. In short there is a certain optimum or best level of arousal or excitement involved in any good game of tennis. What happens when you get keyed up or psyched up beyond this optimum or

best point? We know from studies in animals and humans that performance at a complex task like a game of tennis is likely to

become erratic and disorganized when overly high levels of arousal have occurred. In other words, if you become too ex-

cited, tense and keyed up, your game will suffer. Over-arousal resulting from tension and stress is clearly one of the most

common factors involved in interfering with our ability to play tennis as well as we might. It is tension and stress arising from this over-arousal which forms the invisible roadblock to tennis improvement which I mentioned previously.

Tennis, like life, is what you make of it. You can play tennis for fun, for exercise, to relax, to compete, to destroy. For some, it is a game; for others, it is their life. When I first saw a tennis match, I thought only the rich played. Then at nineteen, at a summer camp, I tried my hand. Although the frustrations of that day were hard to forget, I eventually played two more times that summer. Basketball, a

Three years later I bought a racket and tried to teach myself by hitting the ball against a wall. I also played a few games with friends. My life with tennis changed only slightly in the next decade. Occasionally I played with friends and colleagues.

Doubles meant a total social game where you moved from side to side and didn’t get much exercise. You also did not know what was happening. Mixed doubles meant even less exercise.

The only person who played tennis with me consistently was my wife. Mostly we hit forehands. Our backhands were hit

almost as a last resort. Serving was attempted only once in a while. We ran a lot and thoroughly enjoyed tapping the ball

serve. I was ready to be weaned. Yes, I was about to play tennis with men, the big time.

At first I thought tennis was a game, a sport. I assumed you played to have a good time. Soon I realized that men, myself included, brought many of their needs with them to the court.

Some men waited all week to give vent to their frustrations. The weekend tennis match was their day in the spotlight. If, all week long they were forgotten or unimportant, here at least was proof of their powers. The match took on gladiator-like proportions — victory and survival were synonymous.

The men (and women) not only wanted to win, but as Vince Lombardi said, “Winning is everything.” Some men chose to win by the most honest way — straightforward hard work and no nonsense. It was a serious and deadly decapitation of the

enemy. Every offensive weapon was employed — hard serve, net game, overhead, lob, volley, backhand, etc. The pressure was unrelenting. Their aim was to win 6-0. If two such players

competed, it was an interesting but humorless match. Challen-

gers arose to dethrone the king (the top player). A lesser player would compete, and if he lost, invariably he would blame the

loss on a bad day, or that he had worked hard all week and was exhausted.

Machismo was ever-present and the tennis buffs could never relax. Some players were always seeking to win and would not

dare challenge better players, but only weaker ones. Here victory was certain and the games were really a means to attain the feeling of being a conqueror.

Some players were not always victorious; to them the way they played was important. Shots had to be hit hard and at the opponent’s vulnerable spots. The racket was used as a weapon. A good shot was more memorable than the final score. The aggression would take on frightening proportions and intimidate weaker or less aggressive players. Although most aggressive

players used their rackets, some used their mouths. They would try to “psyche out” or instill fear by what they said or by quick aggressive moves at the net.

This American motif of winning and attainment can be seen on any tennis court. Winners feel pride in their accomplishment. But why is it that we need to win to feel good? And if we need this to feel our best, what happens when we lose?

We no longer need to win to survive. One can live without always having this hunger for being the victor. Is this our institutional heritage of being aggressive? I wonder.

I am surprised to see how personalities change on the tennis court. Meek and retiring persons can become not only aggress-

ive and hostile but ugly too. Many have very responsible jobs during the week: lawyers, teachers, physicians, salesmen, psy-

chologists, etc. What happens if they do not release their aggression? What happens if they lose on Sunday and take more frustrations with them to work on Monday? Do they foist their

anger on others? Are they only capable of being decent, helpful and relaxed if they win?

There are players who cheat. There are others who see a shot and then have to wait before they call it. The need to win enters perception and thinking. What one sees is colored by

the need to win. For others the long pause before calling a shot entails thinking of what should be called. The intricate thought processes go beyond calling a shot as it happens. The

player thinks of the game, the kind of guy the other player

is, the tempo of the game, what is morally correct.

If winning is everything, then losing must be nothing. Here we see how players must learn to be good sports. Some ration-

alize their losses, others hate themselves, their rackets, the playing conditions, wind, time of day, noise, sun, not enough

rest the previous night, etc. Very few of us can lose graciously. If winning yields smiles, glee, and proves we are worth something, losing proves we are worthless, empty and discardable.

Tennis as a Communication Medium

In addition to reflecting one’s personality and life style, tennis can also be useful as an analogue for communication and for describing some of the ways people relate to each other off the court. In order to look at tennis and communication we need to describe two important aspects of the latter. In every com-

munication there is the message which carries the content or information and an accompanying meta-message which is an implicit statement about how the message is to be taken. The meta-message thus defines the nature of the relationship and

how it is to be controlled. A simple statement such as, “You're

wrong,” carries far more than its evaluative information. It im-

plies that the sender has the right to confront the other person

with his evaluation (something a slave, for example, would never

take the position of saying to his master). What he says and the way he says it indicates, “This is the sort of relationship we have with each other.”

In tennis the ball is the message and the way it is sent is the meta-message. Around every point, each player attempts to

maneuver the other into a court position such that he can gain

control and achieve his objective, whether it is playing to win, playing for fun and exercise, sociability, or for improvement.

Similarly, in everyday relationships we strive through our communications to gain control and define the type of relationship we desire and a life position in which we are most comfortable. It’s illustrative to use tennis vernacular for describing various types of relationships. For example, the person who is

extremely aggressive or assertive, and tries to win his arguments in a hurry, might be called a “net-rusher”. Or the individual who never seems to commit himself in a relationship but plays it safe by responding carefully and well within the limits

of what’s socially acceptable might be classified as a cautious “backcourt lobber”. Then there’s the subtle remark which catches

one off guard as though it were a “drop shot”. Or the irrefutable point in a conversation which no one can deny because of its

power similar to an “overhead smash”. In effect, we tend to see

ourselves in a certain basic life position relative to others which we then consciously and unconsciously set up and perpetuate

through our methods of communicating. This is true whether were involved in tennis or other types of relationships.

Tennis as an Intimate Relationship

It is also possible to see tennis as an “intimate” relationship where there are no games and manipulations, where one can

be open and take risks, make mistakes, express feelings and empathize with the other person. In tennis this may occur when

one does not need to prove anything about himself or his basic life position at the expense of the other person, and can temper his desire to win with a warm acceptance of his opponent, trust-

ing his calls and feeling no need to “win at any price”. Thus tennis can include relationships at various levels, from a cold, impersonal ritual, to an activity or pastime, to “games within a

game’, and finally to genuine intimacy. At this level the relationship has actually become more important than the game.

Intimacy in tennis is, of course, not automatic, but can be a goal that must be worked toward and continuously re-established just as the player works toward improving skills and winning. Let-

ting go and taking risks is not easy in any relationship and can

also be quite costly in a match. For example, consider the role of anger in both tennis and other interpersonal relationships. Appropriate anger and its expression are fine, but too much

or too little can be upsetting for either a tennis game or interpersonal satisfaction. Even those at the professional level where

winning is all important, express this desire for closeness, an

example being Nastase wanting “to play until my opponent

and I fall down with exhaustion. To beat him and then em-

brace”.

The experience of intimate relationships is lacking for many

people in our culture. For some, tennis may provide a genuinely therapeutic possibility for the development of such relationships

and the personal growth that can be the result.

If tennis is indeed a microcosm of the “real world” and the

broader aspects of life, an interesting possibility arises: besides

reflecting how the individual deals with life, might not the

tennis court provide a “safe” setting for one to practice new ways of being, of living, of interpersonal relationships? We know that it is not enough just to think or talk about alternative ways of behaving, they must be practised and carried out, developed

step by step through action. We can see and hear and read

about our stroke, but it will not change until we practise it and actively involve our muscles, along with our mental images.

We discover that as we practise it and do it, change takes place. As it is in tennis, so it is in life. Perhaps there can be

some carry-over from success in learning on the court to success in dealing with the broader problems of living.
