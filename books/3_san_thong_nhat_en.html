Chapter 1 — Hidden engine hides: biotensegrity, kinetic chains and spirals

There's one thing I want you to think about before reading any further, because it changes the way you look at every remaining shot in this book. When you watch Roger Federer hit a forehand, or Pete Sampras throw an explosive serve, your eyes are being deceived. It kept following the blur of the racket's surface, but the racket was just the tip of the last whip, obediently following. The real blow had begun hundreds of milliseconds earlier, deep into the soles of the feet.

Almost everyone who picks up a racket for the first time makes the same mistake. Seeing the ball coming, they tense up, grip the handle of the racket as if to suffocate it, then swing their arms to hit the ball using only their shoulders and hands. You know the result, even though you've never thought about why: the ball falls short and lifeless, the stroke feels stiff and jerky, and after just a few months the elbow starts screaming every time you pick up the racket. That's tennis elbow, microscopic tears that gradually accumulate because the arm has to do a job it was never born to do.

The arm is tricking you

Your hitting arm generates almost no forward energy on its own. It's not the engine. It is a whip, an elastic conduit whose sole job is to transmit and release force that has been generated elsewhere. High-speed video and force sensors, the kind of work that John Yandell, Howard Brody or Mark Kovacs spent their careers doing, demonstrate this very concretely. Watch Jannik Sinner or Carlos Alcaraz hit an 85 mph rally shot, about half of that ball's energy, about 51 to 54 percent, comes from the ground and the feet. Another 30% is multiplied through the body as it swings open. Shoulders contribute about 10%. Forearm and wrist? Only 6 to 8%. They are not resources, they are valves, triggers that release something that has been built in advance.

So if your foundation is dead, if your legs aren't charging, if the ground isn't being utilized, your swing is dead, no matter how hard your arms are trying. And your shoulders and elbows will be the ones paying the price. Imagine a six-link chain: ground, knees and hips, torso and core, shoulders and shoulder blades, forearms and wrists, and finally the racket head. The energy must pass through each of those links, in the correct order, for the whip to snap off properly at the end.

Why don't you brake suddenly?

This is the part that almost no one explains well, and it is the biggest lever you have to apply force gently: the near part must decelerate for the far part to accelerate. Think of a whip. The person holding the whip does not try to swing the tip of the whip quickly, it is physically impossible to do it with just the arm. Instead they jerked the heavy handle forward and stopped suddenly. That stop creates a wave that runs along the tapering skin, and when the wave reaches the paper-thin tip of the whip, the tip of the whip travels faster than the speed of sound. That's the chirp you hear.

In your forehand, the front hip is the handle of the whip. When Novak Djokovic loads an open forehand, he pushes about 2,200 newtons down through his back foot into the court, equivalent to more than two and a half times his body weight, straight into the ground. As he opens his body, his hips rotate toward the net at more than 450 degrees per second. But what the high-speed camera recorded, about 40 milliseconds before he touched the ball, was that the previous hip did not continue to rotate. It slammed into an invisible wall and braked more than 80% almost immediately.

That braking is real muscle work, the glutes and anterior intercostal muscles tighten to hold the pelvis still. Since the rotational momentum has to go somewhere, it doesn't disappear but is transferred to the lighter, faster section above: the chest, which itself reaches about 700 degrees per second before braking and transferring the momentum to the arms and racquet, which are now flying past the ball at more than 2,400 degrees per second. This is why the advice to "just swing through the ball" is actually so bad if you take it literally. If the hips continue to rotate through contact, you leak the accumulated momentum instead of releasing it. What you need to learn is the deceleration fulcrum: accelerate the base, brake the base, then let the tip of the whip fly freely.

The body is not built as shown in the bone diagram

Throughout much of the 20th century, sports science viewed the body as a machine made of hard bones that acted as levers, with muscles as the separate motors that pulled them. The model is neat, but wrong, or at least seriously flawed. That's part of the reason many traditional training methods treat the arms and shoulders as discrete parts instead of a single continuous system.

A more accurate picture, developed by researchers such as Stephen Levin and Thomas Myers, is called biotensegrity. In a tensegrity structure, the hard parts, your bones, never push directly against each other. They float, suspended within a continuous network of stretched connective tissue: fascia, tendons, ligaments. So when you load the back leg when serving or hitting the forehand, that tension is not confined to the calf. It spreads, up through the Achilles tendon, into the gluteal muscles, diagonally across the back through the thoracic lumbar fascia, to the opposite lat muscles, out to the shoulder and then into the arm. One continuous spiral mesh, not a series of separate parts.

What is amazing is that this model was drawn up more than 400 years ago, not in a biomechanics laboratory, but in Tran Thuc Tai Chi Quan, under the name Trien Ty Kinh, or silk coiling energy. The ancient masters did not talk about individual muscle contractions. They talk about sinking the center of gravity down into the Dan Dien, a few fingers below the navel, then rolling open in a continuous spiral from the legs, through the spine, out to the fingertips. The Tai Chi Sutra has an instruction that honestly is no worse than any modern biomechanist: "Origin in the legs, emanating from the legs, controlled by the waist, expressed through the fingers. From the legs to the waist must form a continuous breath."

Translating that to the tennis court, you get three simple ideas. The root: the hind leg loads the force into the ground, pushing the force back up through the body. Operator: The hips and lower back roll in and out around a stable axis; you want about 45 to 55 degrees of tension between the hips and shoulders at the peak of loading. The performance: a truly relaxed arm, a grip strength of just 2 out of 10, bouncing through the ball like a flick of silk, not like a club being swung. When you feel this instead of just using force, you are no longer hitting the ball. You let a wave starting from your feet meet the ball with real mass behind it, and your joints barely feel the impact.

Why do you swing so fast it doesn't feel like you're trying?

Have you noticed that the best tennis players you've seen live don't look like they're swinging hard? It's not an illusion, nor is it just superior technique. It is a specific mechanical phenomenon called stretch-shortening cycle. When a muscle and its tendons are stretched rapidly, subjected to eccentric loading, two things happen at the same time. First, the tendon itself and an elastic protein called titin store actual mechanical energy, like a drawn bow. Second, the spirit nervous system detects the sudden stretch through sensors in the muscle and fires an involuntary reflex, recruiting additional muscle fibers automatically, without you even thinking about it.

But there's a catch, and that's why rhythm is so important. If too much time passes between tension and release, more than about a quarter of a second, that stored energy leaks out as heat. It's gone forever. This is exactly why the best hitters never stop or stall at the end of the swing. Watching Alcaraz or Federer at slow speed, the racket falls into the slot in a continuous, looping motion. The external rotators of the shoulders, chest, and intercostals are all stretched for an extremely short period of time, about 120 to 180 milliseconds, before rebounding. That's not strength from burning muscle. It is a freely exploited tendon spring.

The racket and strings are also part of the chain

All that biology must eventually transfer energy to a piece of equipment, and the physics of the racket and strings are more important than many people realize. The number 315 grams written on the box hardly affects the feel when swinging. What matters is swingweight: how the weight is distributed relative to where you hold it. Adding just three grams of lead tape at the 12 o'clock position on the racket head increases swing weight by about 9 to 10 points. That extra swing weight keeps the racket frame from being thrown back during the 4- to 5-millisecond moment of impact with the ball, so that more momentum is transferred into the shot instead of being absorbed by the frame's misalignment.

Adding weight at 3 and 9 o'clock instead of 12 o'clock does something else, it prevents the racket frame from twisting on off-center hits, creating a larger effective sweet spot and protecting the forearm from strong vibration shocks. Then there's the snapback string, which surprises many people. The 500 fps video shows that modern polyester pendants don't just deform straight back. The vertical strings are deflected horizontally, sliding over the horizontal strings, because polyester is very slippery. During the last 1.5 milliseconds of impact, those misaligned strings rebound into position like a released sling, and that rebound alone adds about 800 to 1,200 rpm topspin on top of whatever your swing created. Your racket's strings are silently doing the real work of creating spin.

Read the leak

When something goes wrong in the swing, the symptom you see is rarely where the problem actually lies. Here's how I trace common errors back to their source. If your arms are tired and you know you're using them to hit the ball, you're not getting force from the ground, and your upper body is swinging before your hips can open. Correct by sinking your center of gravity about 10cm lower into your back heel, feeling the ground push your hips before your hands leave the slot.

If the ball flies long with almost no spin, your chest opens too early and flattens too much before contact, eliminating the upward combing action. Keep the non-dominant shoulder toward the contact area for as long as possible, allowing the arm to snap through while the body remains in position. Sharp pain on the outside of the elbow is often caused by gripping the racket too tightly, level 9 or 10 out of 10, combined with hitting late behind the body. Reduce grip strength to 2 out of 10 when loading, bringing the contact point about 30cm further in front of the front hip. And if the ball feels weak, being pushed but not rebounding, you are failing to decelerate, your hips keep spinning through impact with nothing to brake. Imagine a steel post at your front hip, pinning your front leg, locking your hips, and letting it bounce the racquet head through.

Three exercises to really feel this

The first exercise is to throw the medicine ball in deceleration style. Take a two-handed medicine ball weighing 2 to 3kg. Come into an open stance on baseline, load your back leg, roll your pelvis about 45 degrees, and then throw the ball toward the wall about 4 meters away using a throwing rotation. The key is the moment immediately after releasing the ball: focus all your attention on braking the front hip the moment the ball leaves your hand. If your hips continue to rotate and you lose balance, that's a sign you're not braking properly. You should feel your core activate strongly to pop the ball out.

Lesson two, two-fingered feather whip. Holding the racket with just the thumb and index finger, the grip strength is really loose about 1.5 out of 10. Do a rally at the end of the court with a partner at 50% speed. Pay attention to what the wrist does on its own: as the hips push forward, it should naturally drop back into a deep bend, then snap back on release without you forcing it. There is no tension in the forearm. Lesson three, check the whooshing sound when holding the racket upside down. Turn the racket upside down, holding it just below the head, then swing that bare handle through the air like a whip. Listen for where in the arc the loudest whirring noise occurs. If it's in the back of your body, you're leaking energy too early. What you want is a whoosh that peaks about six inches in front of your front hip, right where the ball is actually waiting for you.

Chapter 2 — Satori's eye: why top players "see" the ball before it arrives

I want to start by breaking down something that almost everyone has been taught from their first lesson: watch the ball hit the string. Sounds very reasonable. Every coach says this, even the one who taught you. But there's a problem, physiologically, it's impossible.

Your eyes only smoothly track an object moving at a maximum speed of about 100 to 120 degrees per second. That is the limit of so-called smooth pursuit. But as the ball enters the final 15 feet of its flight path toward you, it crosses your field of vision at more than 500 degrees per second, many times faster than your eyeballs can rotate. No matter how much you focus, you can't change that. Your eyes simply cannot follow the ball until it hits the string. So what are the world's best tennis players actually doing?

Why do top players see the ball before it arrives?

Researcher John Yandell answered this question by filming Roger Federer at 250 frames per second, with the camera focusing only on his head and eyes. What he discovered completely changed the way we understand vision in tennis. Federer never followed the ball to the point of contact. About 150 milliseconds before he hits the ball, his eyes jump forward and lock onto the point in space where he predicts contact with the ball. His head then stands completely still, and stays that way for another 150 to 200 milliseconds after the ball has left the racket.

That state of stillness has a name in sports science: Quiet Eye, a concept pioneered by Joan Vickers. This is the clearest, recurring visual cue that distinguishes top athletes from the rest, appearing in many sports from basketball free throws to golf putting. In tennis, it means locking the eyes on the contact zone and staying there, not chasing the ball, not glancing at the opponent, simply anchoring the eyes on a point that for a moment is just empty air.

This is what happens when players fail to do this. Amateur players track the ball with jerky eye movements, and in the last 50 milliseconds before contact, they lift their heads to glance at the net or their opponent's position. Not only is lifting your head a bad habit, it actually shifts the cervical spine, raises the hitting shoulder a few inches, and tilts the racket face a few degrees out of the correct direction. That's the mechanics of a bad shot. You don't need to guess why the ball went three feet out, your head told your shoulders to move before the ball even left the strings.

Why must your head always be balanced?

Look at Carlos Alcaraz running sideways chasing a ball, his upper body shaking, his feet pounding the ground, and notice that his eyes never seem to shake. His visual world remained as stable as if it were filmed with a Steadicam. That stability comes from one of the body's fastest reflexes, called the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR).

The mechanism is roughly this: as the head accelerates, the fluid inside the semicircular canals of the inner ear shifts and bends microscopic hair cells. The signal goes straight to the brain stem, which then sends a reverse signal to the eye muscles to rotate the eyes in the exact opposite direction of the head movement, canceling it out. The entire loop takes place in less than 10 milliseconds, faster than almost anything else the spirit system can do.

But this system has one condition to work best: the eye's line of sight needs to stay relatively parallel to the field horizon, within about five degrees, no matter how low you bend your knees or how hard you slide into the corner. Tilting your head to one side when lunging disturbs that balance. Your brain has to expend extra processing power just to reconcile the conflicting signals, depth perception is really affected, and so is the rhythm of the shot. This is why coaches are adamant about keeping the head straight, even when the rest of the body is squirming to reach the ball. Not for good posture, but to protect the unique system that keeps vision stable when moving quickly.

Read serve and read the ball before it bounces

How could Djokovic catch a 140 mph serve falling exactly into the middle of the court? He did not react to the ball. Actually no. He is reading the server's body before the ball is even hit, then predicting where the ball will land immediately afterward.

The first part is called predictability, and it starts sooner than you think. The receiver's visual system begins to receive signals about 100 milliseconds before the racket even touches the ball. Top handlers read things like the position of the ball toss, the angle of the chest and shoulders in front of the server, the depth of knee bend before kicking. Combining those signals, they predict the direction of the serve with over 80% accuracy before the ball even leaves the racket.

Part two happens the moment the ball is hit. Instead of tracking the ball throughout its flight, the receiver's eyes make a very quick jump, called a saccade, straight to the point on the court where the brain calculates the ball will bounce, arriving about 150 milliseconds before the ball. While your eyes are jumping like that, you're effectively blinded for a few milliseconds, called saccade inhibition, but your brain doesn't need that visual stream, it's already running an internal simulation of flight path, spin and speed, and then fills in the gaps on its own. When the ball actually comes, your eyes are already there. You see the bounce before it happens, because your brain has already constructed it before your eyes can do it.

Quiet the voice in your head

In 1974, W. Timothy Gallwey's The Inner Game of Tennis named something every tennis player has felt but has difficulty putting into words. Gallwey describes two minds coexisting in your head on the field. The head, which he calls Ego 1, is the inner critic. It speaks with words, judges, constantly lectures: don't lose, keep your wrists steady, don't double fault. The second is Ego 2, the vast, silent, automatic system built up over years of practice, controlling hundreds of muscles with a precision that no conscious thought can achieve. Ego 2 already knows how to hit that shot. The problem is that Ego 1 refuses to get out of the way.

When the pressure is high, at break point, at 4-5 in set three, Ego 1 tends to rush in and gain control. And what actually happens in your arm is almost mechanical: the muscles begin to contract simultaneously, the biceps and triceps contract at the same time, locking up the forearm and freezing the smooth chain of motion that you normally trust without thinking. French tennis has a name for this phenomenon, petit bras, short arms. This is not nervousness in the vague emotional sense, this is your conscious mind directly jamming your own swing.

The goal, which every top tennis player pursues whether they express it or not, is what Zen calls satori, a state of natural, effortless awareness, or mushin, mindlessness. When the inner critic finally quiets, the player describes the feeling of time slowing down and the ball appearing larger, as if floating. The body simply moves, and moves right, because you have finally stopped hindering yourself.

Gallwey's way of achieving that state is surprisingly simple: when the ball bounces on your court, say bounce in your head; When the ball touches the racket string, say touch. That's all. It sounds too small to be effective, but it works because it gives your chattering left brain a simple, purely sensory task that leaves no bandwidth for judgment or worry. Give Self 1 something harmless to do, and Self 2 will be free to work undisturbed.

What to watch out for, and how to fix it

If your head lifts up prematurely to glance at the net or your opponent, you will miss shots and make false contact even on easy balls. Force your eyes to stay at the point of contact for a full second after the ball has gone. If your eyes chase the ball with jerky movements instead of anticipation, you'll constantly misjudge how deep or short the ball is. Train your eyes to jump straight from your opponent's racket to the ball's bounce in one motion, instead of following the entire flight.

If your arms stiffen and push the ball timidly at key points, your shot will be constrained and decelerate just when you need it most decisively. Exhale loudly at impact, the whooshing isn't just for show, it actually helps prevent your mind from freezing your muscles. If your reflexes clearly slow down in set three, and you're out of step with the balls you normally handle easily, add stroboscopic exercises to one workout per week. The brief flashes that obscure vision help train visual memory to stay sharp even when tired.

Two exercises worth actually practicing

Numbered ball exercise. Take a dozen tennis balls and write bold numbers, from one to six, with a black and red marker on opposite halves of each ball. Have your coach or partner hit volley for you from the other side of the net with different spins and speeds, and call out both the number and color before the ball bounces on your court. It sounds simple, even a little funny, but it forces your eyes to lock right onto the spin of the ball, which is exactly the skill you're trying to build.

Two-second freeze exercise. During regular grounding practice, choose a point on the field, a cone, a line mark, whatever, as your contact zone reference point. After each base shot, keep your head completely still and your eyes locked on that empty contact area until your partner's return has bounced onto their court. At first it will feel unnatural, almost forced. That's the purpose. You're overriding the instinct to glance, and with enough repetitions, this will become the new default instead of something you have to remember to do mid-play.

Chapter 3 — Dynamic gravity: split-steps, slides, and the art of deceleration

Imagine two tennis players warming up before a match. A person stood motionless at baseline, waiting. The other person was almost bouncing up and down, a small, smooth bounce, repeating over and over, not following any specific rhythm. Then the opponent hits the ball. A stationary person must overcome his own immobility before he can move, like pushing a stalled car. The person who is bouncing is already in a falling state. Their first step is not a push, but a catch of the fall.

That's the secret behind the movement of top tennis players, and it's not about speed. It lies in gravity.

Why split-step really works

This is what most movement players misunderstand about the split-step: they think it is a jump. That's not true. If you watch Djokovic or Alcaraz at slow speed, that swing happens before the opponent touches the ball, about three hundredths of a second earlier, faster than you can actively decide to do something. At the moment the opponent hits the ball, the top player is already in the process of falling. As soon as their feet touch the court, they read the direction of the ball and swing their body sideways.

Why is falling beneficial? Because a body at rest is heavy and sluggish. To go from standing still to sprinting, your feet have to push down on the field hard enough to overcome both your body weight and inertia, which is very slow. But in the moment you are falling off the swing, your body is almost weightless. There is almost no force pressing down on the field. And weightless things change direction very easily. That is the brief moment that top tennis players seek, when changing direction costs almost nothing.

When the foot lands after the swing, three things happen almost at the same time: the standing posture widens to create a solid foundation, the front leg rotates toward the direction it is about to move, and the calf and thigh muscles, which are being stretched like a compressed spring, release at the same time. It is that release that creates the explosive first step, not the muscular effort from a stationary position.

Try this: don't worry about how high you can jump in your split-step. Let's run with the moment. A good split-step barely leaves the court, about the height of several coins stacked on top of each other. If you find yourself jumping up quite high on the split-step, you're making the opposite mistake of the person who stands motionless: you're now floating helplessly for a quarter of a second, with no grip at all, and a century before a quick, flat shot. The goal is not time in the air. The goal is a quick, smooth fall, timing the opponent's contact with the ball.

Skating on hard courts is not reckless

For most of tennis history, skating has been limited to clay courts. Try skating on a hard court and you're likely to sprain your ankle or tear the sole of your shoe. Then Djokovic, Clijsters, and Alcaraz made sliding on hard courts look completely normal, and now it's a skill that any player with good movement needs.

Here's what makes it effective instead of damaging your ankles: a hard court grips better when you're standing still than when you're moving. As soon as the foot begins to slide instead of grip, the friction underneath is noticeably reduced. The trick is that you're not trying to defeat friction, you're taking advantage of the fact that a moving foot slides more easily than a stationary foot.

The part that really matters with your ankle is the angle. Place your ski foot at about a 40 to 45 degree angle to the ground, think of it as a snowboard cutting across the snow, not straight down. If you place your foot perpendicular to the direction of travel, the sole of your shoe will grip the ground instead of sliding, and that's exactly how you tear your ligaments. Keep most of the weight, about 70%, on the back supporting leg, and let the front leg slide forward like a blade, sliding about one to one and a half feet before you stabilize.

The benefits are bigger than just looking good. Because you can hit the ball while still decelerating instead of having to stop first, sliding the course gives you about a third of a second more recovery time. It's the difference between a desperate defensive shot and a controlled counterattack from the same corner of the court.

Stopping is harder than starting

Here's a truth that surprises many players: winning points actually doesn't depend on how fast you accelerate. It's about how well you stop, decisively, repeatedly, without the knee paying the price.

When you sprint to chase a ball into the corner of the field, the force put through your legs in the last step can be up to four times your body weight. If only your knee had to absorb all that force in each sprint, you wouldn't last an entire match, let alone an entire season. The patellar tendon is simply not designed to bear that load alone.

So the body does not let the knees carry the load alone. Players who move well spread the shock across three joints in a coordinated sequence, a bit like a car's suspension system absorbs a pothole in stages instead of all at once. The ankle bears the brunt of the impact first, bending and flexing to absorb about one-third of the impact force. The knee flexes next deeply, ranging from 45 to 90 degrees, and the vastus lateralis does the heaviest work, absorbing nearly 40%. The hips finish their work, flex back and let the glutes take care of the rest.

Because the ankle, knee, and hip all bend in sequence instead of just the knee carrying the load alone, force is distributed to the largest, strongest muscle groups, the glutes and hamstrings, instead of concentrating on the cartilage and tendons. That's what protects your knees throughout your career, and it's also what energizes you to bounce back to the center of the field with elasticity.

Return to the center of the yard

After you've hit your shot, you have a moment to decide: how do you get back to your recovery position? If you're only moving a short distance, less than one and a half meters, use a side-shuffle. It keeps your hips and shoulders squarely facing the net throughout, so you're ready to react immediately if your opponent hits the ball down the line.

If you are pulled away, toward a double hallway, don't try to walk across the entire way back, it will be slow. Use a crossover step instead: your outside leg swings across the front of your body, your hips open, and you turn that rotation into an actual sprint. It's about 40% faster than side stepping over long distances, and it's often the difference between being ready for the next ball and still struggling.

There's also a third option worth knowing, the carioca step, where the back leg crosses behind the front leg as you move. Good movers use this move when they need to cover distance quickly while keeping their eyes and chest facing the opponent across the net.

There is no one type of movement that is suitable for all surfaces

If there's one mistake I see even good players make, it's using the same movement instincts on every surface. Clay, hard courts, and grass each require a different approach, as each grips your shoes differently.

Red clay slides the most freely, so you start sliding early, about a meter and a half before hitting the ball, keeping your body upright, and letting your body go with the slide instead of fighting it. The hard court (Plexipave) grips well but allows for controlled sliding on the move, so you split-step decisively, keep your feet wide, and treat the slide as a braking tool, not the main way of movement. The grass grips the least and is unpredictable, so avoid big slides, keep your center of gravity low, take short, rapid steps, and never put all your weight on one foot before you are sure of the grip.

Coach's perspective

A lazy split-step, flat feet, or no split-step, slows your reaction time by about 200 milliseconds, so you're always in a hurry with deep or fast balls. Correct it by timing the swing to the moment the opponent swings the racket back, not to the sound of the ball hitting. Jumping too high when split-stepping leaves you hanging in the air for a quarter of a second with no grip, useless against a counterattack. Keep your lunge close to the ground, just past a dime, and think about falling instead of jumping.

Placing your sliding foot vertically instead of at an angle carries a high risk of spraining your ankle, and you will begin to avoid sliding out of fear. Tilt your legs forward 40 to 45 degrees, never perpendicular to the direction of movement. A narrow stance at impact pushes your center of gravity too high and causes you to lose balance before powerful shots. Widen your stance and lower your hips into the shot.

Exercises on the field

Lesson one, split-step reflex. Stand at baseline while the coach stands at the opposite service line, holding two different colored balls. Without warning, they dropped one. Your task is to activate the split-step as soon as the ball leaves their fingers, then spring forward to catch the ball before it bounces a second time. You are training the spirit nervous system to respond to the stimulus, not to your own internal rhythm. Aim for the first foot to move within less than 180 milliseconds of releasing the ball.

Lesson two, learn how to skate on hard courts. Start with something comfortable, pulling old socks over your shoes is a good way, or find a more slippery area near the touchline. First just practice side lunges into a short slide, about one meter, keeping that 40 to 45 degree leg angle and keeping your body straight. When it feels natural, take off your socks and run five wide-angle forehand recoveries in real shoes, paying attention to the moment your foot transitions from gripping to sliding. That transformation is the whole skill. Get used to that feeling, and the fear of slipping on hard courts will disappear.

Chapter 4 — Blitz on a concrete court: why the first four shots decide the match

I often get asked this question by parents when watching their children play beautiful, twenty-beat shots from the middle of the court: "Isn't tennis like that?" The truth is no, at least not at the level of winning real matches. An analyst named Craig O'Shannessy — who later became personal strategist for Novak Djokovic — extracted more than five hundred thousand ball points from the Grand Slam tournaments, all four major tournaments Wimbledon, US Open, Roland Garros and Australian Open. What he found should have changed the way every tennis player, at every level, trains.

Nearly seventy percent of all points in professional tennis end in the first four shots. Not four minutes, but four strokes. About twenty percent more finished before the eighth stroke. That means only about one point out of ten really becomes the tug-of-war between the two ends of the court that people still imagine when thinking about "real tennis". Now look at how most players spend their practice time: cooperative cross-court shots from midcourt, comfortable back and forth, twenty strokes deep. That's training for a scenario that only happens one out of ten. Meanwhile, the four shots that determine most of the points are hardly practiced separately at all.

If you want to win more games, you don't need to be better at hitting the ball. You need to be dangerous in the opening exchange. The four shots are: serve, the return, the server's first base shot after the serve — what coaches call Serve+1 — and the returner's first base shot after, called Return+1. This chapter is about thinking around all four.

Serve+1: set up the third shot before even tossing the ball

Here's a mindset shift that changes everything: serve was never really about the ace. At the professional level, servers rarely expect to finish the point immediately with a serve. What they're really doing is setting up the third shot. Watch Carlos Alcaraz or Roger Federer at his peak serving wide to the deuce box. The goal is not to send the returner off the field, but to draw them a few meters off the singles line, far enough that any return is a weak ball, bobbing into the middle of the field. As soon as that return leaves the racket, the server runs around the backhand in search of his favorite weapon: the inside-out forehand.

That third hit — Serve+1 — is where the real damage happens. There are three ways to end it. The first way is the laser shot into the open court: the returner has just been pulled aside and hasn't had time to regain position, you just need to hit the space they left. Direct and extremely effective. The second method is more sophisticated, and to be honest, very easy to implement: the wrong leg trap. The deliverer is sprinting toward the middle to regain position, all weight in one direction. Instead of hitting into the open field, you hit behind them, into the same space they just left. Reversing direction at full speed requires real physical effort, and most tennis players simply can't keep up. You will see their balance completely collapse. The third way is the low-bounce short angle: instead of using power, you use heavy topspin to let the ball fall short and wide into the service box, pulling your opponent forward and out of the court.

The lesson for your own play is very specific: before you throw the ball up, know where you want your third shot to land. Serve is just a setup step. The third hit is the killing blow.

Return+1: why the middle of the field is your best friend

So if the server is trying to trap the third shot, what should you do on the second shot — the return itself? I see popular players repeatedly making the same costly mistake: faced with a fast serve, they try to hit a winner down the line with a low success rate. Most of the time the ball goes out or hits the highest part of the net. It's an ambitious shot that almost never pays off.

Players who understand tennis at a deeper level do something almost boring — and it helps them win more points. They aim about eighty percent of their returns with strong first serves deep in the middle of the court, into the corridor between the service lines. It doesn't sound flashy, but it's incredibly effective for three reasons. First, it takes away the serve's angles: a cross-court or sideline ball gives the opponent a wide range of attack direction, while a straight ball down the middle narrows that range, leaving them with no dangerous place to hit. Second, it disrupts their stance: the deep ball is aimed directly at the server so that they cannot step sideways into the extended forehand position intended for Serve+1, but are forced to step back awkwardly, hitting a neutral, defensive shot instead of the planned weapon. Third, this is simply the safest shot on the court: you cross the net at the lowest point, right in the middle, and have the entire length of the court for the ball to land on. Fewer unforced errors, more pressure on opponents. When you're not sure what to do with a difficult return, mid-court is almost never the wrong choice.

Wardlaw's direction of attack rules

A college coach named Paul Wardlaw coined one of the clearest and most useful mental frameworks in the game — a set of rules about which way to hit the ball based on which direction the ball is coming from. Once you internalize these rules, a large portion of self-sabotages will simply disappear.

Rule one: cross-court balls should return cross-court. When a ball comes diagonally, it is already moving past you in that direction. Changing direction to hit down the line means you have to fight the ball's natural momentum, hit earlier, and change trajectory suddenly — opting for a low success rate in any pressure situation. Cross-court return means you swing the racket following the ball's natural path, over the lowest part of the net, into the longest diagonal line on the court. That is the path of least resistance. Rule two: the ball down the line can go anywhere, because the ball does not oppose your swing, you are in control of the situation. Rule three, and I find this is the most commonly violated: never change direction with a ball that has passed behind you. If you're late and the ball has skimmed your hips before contact, trying to hit down the line will almost certainly result in a foul, usually with the racket flying. In that situation, just roll the ball across the court with a bit of topspin, reestablish the position, and survive to fight on the next ball. Three rules, learn them, believe them, and a whole bunch of self-defeating errors will disappear.

Not all points are the same

Tennis has a strange scoring system — you don't just have to win the most points, you have to win the right key points, in the right order, to win the game. That means some points are more important than others. A number worth pondering: winning a single point at 30-30 makes your probability of winning the game jump from about fifty percent to over eighty percent. Compared to a point at 40-0, it changes almost nothing — you already have a big advantage.

And this is exactly where movement players do the opposite. Under the pressure of 30-30 or a break point at 30-40, most instinctively shrink back, become passive, start pushing the ball and hope the opponent misses first. It's a completely understandable reflex — and also a wrong one. Playing passively at break points has been proven to significantly reduce your chance conversion rate. Watch the truly great players — Nadal, Serena, Djokovic — and you'll see them do the opposite under pressure. At the most important points, they focus on their favorite cards with absolute determination. Not reckless, but not hesitant. It's a habit worth building: when the point matters most, trust your best shot and go for it, instead of cowering and hoping.

Ugly wins: the art of breaking the beat by Brad Gilbert

Brad Gilbert, a former tennis player once ranked in the top 5 in the world, wrote a book called "Winning Ugly" that has become almost a bible for those who know they are not the most physically excellent person on the court. The central idea is simple, and one of my favorite philosophies: if your opponents love rhythm, don't give them rhythm, give them ugliness.

Imagine a big, strong hitter who can hit flat, fast, hip-level shots all game long with a comfortable rhythm. If you play at the same speed as them, you're playing at the same rhythm that makes them dangerous — you're doing their job for them. Instead, break it down. Launch a moonball: high ball, arc, heavy topspin, over the net more than a meter high, pushing the opponent completely back behind the baseline, out of the comfortable hitting area. Low slice: an underspin that bites deep, slides low, forcing a tall player to bend at every shot — physically uncomfortable and disrupting rhythm in a way pure speed never could. Mess up the tempo: use all the rest time between points, change the rhythm of the toss and serve, insert a small miss when they are waiting for a big shot. None of these shots are flashy, but they all wear away at an opponent who has built their entire play around a rhythm that you refuse to give them. You don't need to hit harder than someone to beat them. Sometimes the winning move is simply refusing to play by their rules.

Build a tennis player in ten years, not ten weeks

I want to back up a bit, because everything above is about winning points — but developing a tennis player is a much longer journey. Real, sustainable tennis ability is not built in an off-season. It's almost like a ten year process. Overall, the process goes through five stages. From six to nine years old, the priority is not tennis technique at all but general fitness: multi-sport, flexibility, balance, and above all, fun. Children who specialize too early and too narrowly can easily burn out or develop distorted, injury-prone movement patterns.

Between the ages of nine and twelve, the technical foundations begin to form — how the body connects to create spin and force. From twelve to fifteen years old, children go through a growth spurt, technically called Peak Height Velocity. This is the stage that fools many well-intentioned trainers: when limbs lengthen rapidly, sometimes by several centimeters in just a few months, the entire body's perception changes, and movements that were smooth six months ago suddenly look clumsy. I want to be clear because I've seen it repeatedly misjudged: this clumsiness isn't a loss of talent, it's biology. A good coach recognizes it for what it is, lightens the racquet weight if necessary, and focuses on stabilizing spirit core muscles until everything stabilizes. From the age of fifteen to eighteen is when the first attack mindset, these four strikes, really becomes the focus, along with structured strength training and building character under pressure. And eighteen and up is the fine-tuning for the professional game—the small margins, the science of recovery, the final touches that separate a good player from a great one.

Common tactical mistakes and how to correct them

There are a few traps I see over and over again on the field. The "plowman" trap — hitting back and forth without a plan — silently makes you lose the battle in the first four shots, where most of the ball's scoring is actually decided. Fix: choose your Serve+1 target before tossing the ball, every time, without exception. Trying to hit a winner down the line from a cross-court ball will result in a useless self-missing error every set; Believe the Wardlaw rule, cross field is cross field until there is an actual short ball to attack. Pushing the ball at break points reduces opportunity conversion rates; Instead, attack the second serve, step inside the baseline, and play your best shot with determination. And if you keep letting your opponent hit the ball hard at the rhythm they want, they'll settle into a comfortable rhythm and play their best tennis — breaking it up, with moonballs, low cuts, sudden changes of pace.

On-court drills: practice the first four shots

One drill I often use is the four-strike finisher game. Play tiebreak-style games up to ten points, with an added rule: if a ball goes beyond four shots without a winner or a forced error, both score no points. It sounds harsh, but it reprograms your brain to hunt for aggressive Serve+1 and Return+1 cards, instead of drifting into passive back-and-forth play. The second exercise is the mid-court target box challenge: mark a two meter by two meter square directly above the hash line in the middle of your opponent's baseline, then receive the first twenty serves, trying to get at least fourteen into that box. It builds exactly the depth and return consistency you need to seal your server's attack—and doing it under repeated pressure really helps it take root.

Chapter 5 — The high-altitude cannon: the 8-stage architecture of serve kinetics

I always tell my students that the serve is the only shot in the whole sport that you have complete control over. No opponents, no bounce, no luck — just you, the ball, and a square you're trying to hit. Yet this was also the most intense action that took place on the field. In less than a third of a second, a top pitcher's arm goes from being completely still to swinging faster than the propeller of a small plane, sending the ball at 225 km/h into a target no larger than a doorframe. This chapter is about how that actually happens, and how you can build it into your own gameplay.

Serving isn't one move — it's eight

I want to start by dispelling a thought that holds back many tennis players: serving is not a single swing. It is a series of eight distinct stages, and the two sports scientists who map it best — Dr. Mark Kovacs and Dr. Todd Ellenbecker — have grouped those eight stages into three larger groups, worth remembering because I will refer back to them throughout this chapter.

The first group is preparation. This is when you establish your stance, release the ball, put weight into your legs in a "trophy pose", and drop the racket behind your back into a deep curl position. The second group is explosive — the explosive part, where your legs push, your body rotates open, and the racket accelerates into contact. The last group is the safe descent: after contact with the ball, the arm is still carrying enormous momentum, and the last two stages are entirely devoted to releasing that energy safely without damaging the shoulder. What every coach eventually tells their students is this: these stages are not independent. They are a linked chain. If you cut off the power phase — where the legs curl in — then no matter how fast the arms are afterward, it won't be salvageable. You can't get the whip-whipping finish that everyone wants if you haven't turned the spring all the way before. So before talking about racket speed, we have to talk about the legs.

Everything starts from the feet, not from the hands

When researchers analyze each high-speed frame of players like Pete Sampras and Roger Federer, what catches their attention is not what happens in the shoulders or wrists, but how deeply and purposefully they put force into their legs before the racket begins to move forward.

Top servers lower their knees to about 110 to 115 degrees of flexion — much deeper than what feels natural the first time you try it. If you bend more shallowly, you simply won't be able to recruit your glutes, the largest and strongest muscle group, to push yourself up. If you bend much deeper, you get no additional benefit, just more pressure on the kneecap. There's a real balance here, and it's deeper than most movement players will ever reach. That sit turns into a real jump: when they kick down on the ground, they create a force equal to two and a half to three times their body weight, straight down, and the ground pushes back with equal force. That catapults them up and nearly sixty centimeters into the field, allowing the ball to make contact at a height of nearly three meters — much higher than when standing still and touching the ground.

Meanwhile, the upper body is building its own spring. As your legs gather pressure, assume the classic "trophy pose" — releasing arm reaching straight up toward the ball, shoulders tilted so that the shoulder on the fielding side is noticeably lower than the other shoulder (about 35 to 45 degrees), and the upper spine is bent back 20 to 25 degrees. That flexion stretches the front abs like you're drawing a bow. All that stored tension is about to be released in one direction: forward, into the ball. The legs pack, the hips and shoulders curl, the spine flexes — that's the spring you're about to release, and it happens before the racket really begins to accelerate.

The real driver of serve speed is not your wrist

Here's a question worth pondering: what actually takes the racket head from standing still to over 210 km/h in the split second before contact with the ball? If you grew up thinking the answer was "swag your wrists like a flyswatter," you're not alone — it's the most common misconception in the sport, and the best way to get tendinitis. High-speed video proves it quite clearly: active wrist flicking contributes almost nothing to racket speed, less than 5%.

The real mover is something most players never think about: shoulder internal rotation — the upper arm bone itself rotates rapidly around its longitudinal axis, like you're wringing a towel. At top first serves, this rotation alone contributes more than half of the total racquet head speed at the moment of contact. More than half, from just a swing that most players don't even know they need to do. During the drop phase, your upper arm bone is externally rotated to an extreme, almost uncomfortable-looking position—up to about 170 degrees of external rotation. That tension energizes the large internal rotators throughout the chest, back, and deep muscles under the scapula (subscapularis) like an elastic band stretched to its limit. Then as the legs push and the torso rotates open, those muscles release in unison, rotating the upper arm inward at a speed that can exceed 2,800 degrees per second. Imagine your arm as an axle, and the racket as a spoke protruding from that axle. That shaft doesn't swing, it rotates, powerfully, around its own length. That is what creates the powerful swing that sends the racket head through the ball.

The flick helps the racket face to be perpendicular to the ball

There is a hidden riddle in the Continental grip. You hold the racket almost like an ax, with the edge of the racket facing the ball — which raises an obvious question: how does that edge make contact with the ball so straight and perpendicular at speeds of more than 210 km/h?

The answer is a quick twist of the forearm — forearm pronation — that occurs in the last few milliseconds before the racquet strings touch the ball. The racquet moves up toward the ball along its edge throughout most of the swing, helping it cut cleanly through the air with minimal resistance. Then, in the last four or so milliseconds — too fast for conscious control — your forearm muscles rotate the forearm bones around each other at more than 2,000 degrees per second, turning the racket face from an angled position to a straight perpendicular position, just as it hits the ball. The ball itself is flattened to half its resting diameter against the string for a few milliseconds, before bouncing back. And that rotation doesn't stop right at the point of contact — the racquet continues to twist through, so immediately after impact, the string faces out toward the side fence, not up into the sky. That continued rotation is actually what protects your elbow; Suddenly stopping the racket at the point of contact is what causes elbow damage over time.

A mantra I often use with my students: think "edge up, edge out" — the leading edge of the bat on the way up, the trailing edge of the bat facing out toward the fence. Everything in between will work itself out if the rest of the sequence is properly energized.

The only toss that makes Federer difficult to read

Why is Roger Federer's serve so difficult to read, even for good returners like Nadal and Agassi? It's not deception in the sense of trickery, but consistency. Most players reveal their serve before even swinging the racket, because they toss the ball differently for each type: toss it forward for a straight serve, toss it to the right for a cutting spin serve, toss it behind the head for a topspin serve. A pretty good returner will recognize that almost immediately and start moving before the ball is even hit.

Federer hits every serve — straight, slice, topspin — to exactly the same spot: roughly "12:30" on the clock, about twenty centimeters inward of baseline, just above the ball's eye. Where does the difference between actual tosses fall? Less than two centimeters. He made no secret of the three different types of tosses. He has just one toss, and completely changes the type of serve by what the racquet does in the last millisecond before contact. That's a real goal worth aiming for — not "learn how to hide serve" but "no longer need to hide it in the first place", because the opponent really can't predict anything from the toss.

From that single toss position, here's what changes to create the three signature serves. Strong straight serve: push your legs all the way, fully pronate your forearms, hit straight through the center of the ball. This is a shot that requires almost zero margin of error — the ball only crosses the net about fifteen to eighteen centimeters before landing in the service box, part of the reason why it is a low-success, high-reward shot. topspin serve: instead of hitting straight through, you sweep up behind the ball, from bottom to top, creating a strong topspin that causes the ball to arc high above the net — sometimes more than a meter over the net — then rush down a steep slope and bounce up, sometimes nearly two meters high, straight into the returner's body or high backhand shoulder. This is the safest shot in terms of stability and also the most uncomfortable to pay. Cutter spin delivery: you slice around the outside of the ball instead of hitting through, creating strong sidespin, the ball curves noticeably in flight — more than a meter off the side is common — and slides low after the bounce, pulling the returner off the court, which is why it is a favorite weapon for wide-angle serves on the deuce box. Same toss, three completely different weapons, determined by what the racket does in the final moment.

Protect your shoulders during the descent phase

Everyone focuses on the acceleration portion of serve, but the phase I'm really most worried about as a coach is right after impact — the deceleration phase. Even when the ball leaves the racket at a speed of more than 160 km/h, your arm is still carrying huge forward momentum and rotational momentum. If nothing slows it down safely, the humeral head will be pushed forward into the anterior part of the shoulder socket, and that's how you tear the shoulder labrum or develop impingement syndrome.

The muscles that do this braking job — the small muscles located behind the shoulder blade, along with the posterior deltoids — must contract centrifugally, meaning they are actively lengthening while trying to slow the arm. They absorb a compressive force of almost your entire body weight, in less than a tenth of a second. It's hard work, and it's a big reason why servers who have never trained their back shoulders often have chronic shoulder problems, while their forehands and backhands remain perfectly healthy. The way to protect that joint is to let the arm complete its full stroke: let it sweep diagonally across the body and finish relaxed near the opposite hip, while the back leg kicks back for balance. Resisting that finish—stopping the arm mid-stroke, forcing it to a halt—is one of the quickest ways to lead to a serving-related shoulder injury.

Identify and fix common errors

There are a few errors I see over and over again. A weak "waiter's tray" pick-up shot — the elbow drops below shoulder height and the forearm pronates too early, before the racket can drop into position — is corrected by keeping the leading edge of the racket pointing skyward throughout the pick-up, the string side never pointing skyward during the pick-up. Lower back pain, especially with the topspin crossover, is often caused by bending from the lower back instead of applying force to the knees; Alternate with a true knee bend, lowering to 110–115 degrees and pushing your hips forward into the yard. Unstable tossing is caused by flicking the wrist or bending the elbow during the release — lock the releasing arm straight at the elbow and wrist, lifting it completely from the shoulder joint like a swinging gate on a hinge. And the straight delivery that often goes out (too long) is because the racket face is still slightly open at contact — the forearm swing is not closed — remind yourself to flick the thumb down and finish with the string facing the side fence, no longer facing forward.

Two exercises worth including in your practice session

The first exercise is throwing over the fence. Stand about four and a half meters away from an iron mesh fence, holding an old racket with the strings removed or a rugby ball. Get into a deep trophy position, let the racket drop behind your back into a deep external rotation position, then throw it hard and high over the fence. This lesson teaches the spirit system how to activate the internal rotation motor and unfold the entire sequence of movements without the psychological pressure of actually having to hit the tennis ball. This is the fastest way I know to feel the true meaning of "arm like an axle". The second is a kneeling serve: kneel with your right knee directly on baseline, completely removing your legs from the equation, then hit the second twenty shots into the service box using only upper body rotation, full racket release, and a final forearm flick. What the exercise demonstrates to the player, almost every time, is that a large amount of racket speed comes gently from the rotation and pronation of the forearm, not from using arm strength to push the ball. Most players are surprised by the force they create just from a kneeling position.

Chapter 6 — The iron gate: front-line blocking and left-hand geometry

"Receiving a serve is the hardest shot in tennis. You have less than half a second to read the ball's spin, predict where the ball will go, get your body in the right position, and put up a wall against something flying faster than a car on the highway." If serving is a cannon, then serving is an iron gate. You can't make it easy, but you can make it systematic — and once systematic, it turns from fear to weapon.

You don't have time to swing the racket

Let's start with the number that will completely change the way you think about serving: with a 125 mph first serve, you only have about 400 milliseconds from the moment the ball leaves your opponent's racket to the moment it hits your racket. That's less time than the blink of an eye. During that range, your eyes have to catch the toss and shoulder tilt, your brain has to predict which way the ball is going, your feet have to get you into the right position, and your racket has to be ready. There is no room for a wide, large arc. If you pull the racket behind your back like you would a normal rally forehand, you will always be late before a 120 mph serve — and when you are late, the ball will either fly out, or hit the edge of the racket frame and bounce around.

So the serve shot lives by a different rule than all your other shots: turn your body, don't pull your arms. Rotate your upper body about 45 degrees to load the racket, and keep your arms in front of your chest throughout. You're not gaining momentum, you're building a wall. Think of it less like a groundstroke and more like a boxer's stance: neat, ready, already in position before the punch comes.

Step on the ball, don't wait for the ball

Here's something worth paying attention to the next time you watch Djokovic or Agassi receive a serve: neither of them stands still waiting for the ball to arrive. As soon as the opponent tosses the ball up, the receiver takes a small step — about half a meter — forward, and split-steps into the court instead of remaining in place.

Why is it so important? Because a moving body absorbs and redirects force much better than a stationary body. When a 120 mph serve hits the racket face that's drifting forward, you get a natural, elastic rebound — the incoming ball speed did most of the work, and your forward momentum adds depth to the return almost for free. You don't need to swing hard to get the ball to go far; It was the collision that pushed the ball back deep into the back of the field. Those who stand still and wait often create short returns, "good bait" for the opponent, because there is no forward energy in the system to bring the ball back to the baseline. Step forward then jump — carry the momentum into the shot instead of waiting.

Build a wall: strong wrists win big swings

A 130 mph flat serve hits your racket strings with truly ferocious force. If your wrists are loose or limp at the moment of impact, the racket head will be thrown back and the return will be thrown all over the place — this is the most common reason players miss serving. The fix is ​​to lock your wrists just before the moment of contact, the same way you brace your arms if someone throws you a heavy medicine ball. In the final moments before the ball arrives, tighten your forearms — both the wrist flexors and the wrist extensors — so that the wrist itself cannot move at all. The racket face should be slightly open, tilted slightly back, and held steady as a rock.

After that you hardly swing the racket at all. The shot is a short putt — only about ten to fifteen centimeters following the ball, no more. You're not hitting the ball, you're blocking it and allowing your opponent's own speed to bounce back. This is why you'll hear coaches call a good serve "borrowing power" — you're not creating speed, you're redirecting it.

Second serve: time to attack

The above is all about surviving a strong first serve. The second serve is a completely different story, and if you handle it exactly the same then it's a missed opportunity. The average second serve at the professional level travels at 95–105 mph with lots of kick spin or cutting spin — markedly slower and with more bounce than the first serve. If you stand far back behind the baseline waiting for it as if waiting for the first serve, a heavy kick will bounce up to shoulder level, forcing you to return the ball weakly, defensively and floatingly. It's a gift to the opponent.

A better approach — and this is what every attacking receiver uses — is to step completely inside, about one to one and a half meters from the baseline, as soon as you see the first serve fail. From there, you catch the ball on the rise, at hip height instead of letting it bounce up to your shoulders. This does two things at once: it takes away the power of the kick by catching the ball before it has fully bounced, and it robs the opponent of recovery time, as the ball comes back to them much sooner than they expect. A server who is used to comfortably preparing for the next shot suddenly has to frantically maneuver — and their odds of winning the next shot plummet when you take away their time like that.

Confronting left-handers: why they make right-handers miserable

If you're right-handed, nothing rattles you more than facing a high-class left-handed opponent — think Rafael Nadal. This is not superstition. There's a real geometric reason why left-handed people are so upset. Their Serve wide cut, from the ad court, curves away from the right-hander and toward the double corridor — often a meter further than expected — pulling you off the court and opening up the rest of the court for the next shot. Their spin-heavy cross-court forehand naturally goes into your backhand, and with heavy topspin, it bounces above the shoulder, right at the spot where most right-handers are weakest technically — the high backhand. Once you've been pulled wide and out of position by the cross-court shot, they have an open line to play down-the-line for a neat score.

If you're a right-hander who wants to stay in the game against a left-handed opponent, there are three adjustments worth building into your gameplay. One is to shift your starting position on the ad court — a little wider and a little further forward than normal — so that you cut across the line of the ball before it has space to curve away from you. Second, refuse to play by their rules: instead of continuing the cross-court play they want, break the pattern by redirecting your backhand down the line into their backhand. Third, when you serve to a left-hander, aim your flat serves to the middle T instead of wide — this constricts their backhand and deprives them of the angle they're most confident in exploiting.

Common serving errors

In the eyes of the coach, some repeated errors deserve a name. The late return of the ball to the fence at the back of the court is often caused by you swinging the racket full swing like a groundstroke before a quick serve - rotate your upper body 45°, keep your hands in front of your chest, don't pull your arms back. Getting locked up by a serve to the body is caused by standing stiff-legged and trying to turn your body to swing the racket — jump back a few centimeters and lift your front shoulder to block the ball from leaving your body. The return that falls short, "giving" the ball to the opponent, comes from standing still during the split-step instead of moving forward. And a left-hander's inability to cut wide is often simply a matter of standing in a neutral position and letting the ball curve away — position yourself on the ad court earlier and catch the ball on the bounce, before it can curve.

Two exercises worth including in your workout

The first lesson is a flash from the service line. Have your partner stand at the service line instead of the baseline and serve flat shots at about 75% speed. Cutting the distance in half will drastically cut your reaction time — to less than a quarter of a second — forcing the body to abandon any remaining momentum habits and rely solely on torso rotation and a solid wrist block. It will feel uncomfortable at first. That discomfort is the purpose of the exercise. The second is to ambush the second serve: place two cones about one to one and a half meters inside the court from the baseline, and on every second serve your partner makes, step into that front box before the ball bounces and hit a strong, deep forehand into their backhand corner. This lesson cultivates the habit of seeing second serves as opportunities to attack, not just balls that need to be put back into play.

Chapter 7 — The modern forehand: five moves every top forehand has

Let's look at a video of a forehand from the wooden racket era, then compare it with Carlos Alcaraz. You won't see the same shot played on a better racket, but two completely different sports in terms of movement. In the old days, forehand was like a handshake: step in, swing sideways, touch the ball in front of you. The forehand is now a whiplash. The body is coiled like a spring, the hips explode first, and the racket is so late that by the time it hits the ball it is already flying extremely fast, the ball leaving the racket at more than three thousand spins per minute. I don't say that number to impress, it's simply what a good forehand actually does today, and once you understand why, you won't want to teach or play this shot the same way.

High-speed cameras have been pointed at professional forehands for decades, and whether you watch Federer, Djokovic or Sinner, the stroke breaks down into the same five movements, in the same order, every time. First, you rotate and accumulate force. The shoulders roll back away from the ball, and the hand not holding the racquet reaches out to maintain balance and keep track of the incoming ball. Then the racket drops, not because you press it, but it falls naturally, the racket head facing the court like a slingshot being stretched back. Then the legs and hips explode forward, and this is where the real power comes from: a strong kick into the ground followed by a quick rotation of the hips, which happens before the arms do anything. Next, the front hip brakes, a movement most players are never taught, and that sudden stop launches the arms and racket forward. Finally, the arm whips over and the wrist flips over, creating what's called a windshield wiper motion and heavy amounts of topspin to the ball. Those five movements are the core. The difference between players lies only in rhythm and form, not in essence.

Two different grips, two different forehands

How you grip the racket changes almost everything else in your swing, so it's worth understanding the two most common patterns at the top. The Eastern grip, the flatter and more classic style that Federer uses, allows you to touch the ball earlier and flatter, with your palm more behind the ball. It is suitable for people who read the ball early and hit the ball while it is still rising after bouncing. You have a penetrating shot, but less natural spin and a smaller margin of safety over the net.

The Semi-Western grip, the style used by most professional tennis players today, including Djokovic and Alcaraz, rotates the hand slightly inside the racket handle. That small change does something important: it causes the racket face to close in on itself as the racket falls, so when you swing up and through the ball, you're sweeping up behind the ball instead of hitting flat through it. It's the source of heavy topspin, and a large part of the reason modern tennis hits the ball hard while still keeping it in the court, because spin pulls the ball down.

There is a more extreme version, the Full Western grip, which some players prefer because they want maximum spin and hit the ball very high after the bounce. It's powerful, but it comes at a price: it puts more pressure on the wrist than the other two styles and has difficulty with low, fast-slipping balls, the kind that are common on grass or after a good slice. If you or your student has wrist pain and holds it that way, that's usually the first thing to change. In fact, if you're building your forehand from scratch, Semi-Western is where most modern coaches start, as it gives you virtually free spin and handles both low and high balls.

Straight arms or bent arms? Both are effective

If you've ever watched Federer and Djokovic play forehands side by side, you'll see that their arms look completely different at the moment of contact. Federer's arms are straight and locked. Djokovic's arms were bent strongly at the elbows, close to his body. Neither way is wrong, they are two different solutions to the same problem.

A straight forehand creates longer leverage. Think of a long wrench that turns bolts faster than a short one: for the same rotation speed, a longer arm means the head goes faster. The price to pay is that the straight arm needs to be positioned early, you have to get the arm into position long before touching the ball, because there is no way to bend it at the last minute.

A bent forehand, with elbows bent and wrists bent back, is more compact. Short, tight arms accelerate faster and are easier to adjust late, which is exactly what you need when the ball comes in strong and preparation time is tight. This is why it is often considered an easier version to teach and learn, especially for developing players who still practice early preparation. No shape is more correct than the other. If you coach yourself or teach a student, match the arm shape to the person's preparation rhythm: early preparers use long levers, late preparers need leaner arms.

The wrist does not flick the ball, it is pulled back and then bounces back on its own

This is the most harmful advice in tennis, and I'm almost sure you've heard it before: "flip your wrist at the ball." Ignore that advice. High-speed video clearly shows the wrist not actively swinging forward at any point during a good modern forehand. What actually happened was closer to the cracking of a whip than a push.

Hold the racket loosely, think like you're holding a bird, not a hammer. As the hips and shoulders flare forward forcefully, the heavy racquet head resists being jerked into sudden movements. So, as your body rushes forward, the racket head lags a beat behind, and that lag bends your wrist back, not because you mean it, but because inertia does it for you. Bending back stretches the tendons in the forearm like a stretched rubber band. And just like an elastic band, once stretched, it automatically releases stored energy, bouncing the racket through the ball without you actively kicking anything.

This is also the origin of the so-called classic X-factor, the tension you feel across your body when your hips have begun to rotate toward the ball but your shoulders are still rotating back. The distance between the hips and shoulders is the elastic energy being stored, waiting to be released, just like the wrist lag. Next time you go out on the court, try holding the racket at a tightness level of about 2 out of 10, the loosest but still controllable. Let the hips initiate the swing, and just let the wrists follow naturally. Players who "use their muscles" to push their wrists forward are almost always the ones who push or slap the ball out, because one wrist actively closes the racket face too early and too hard.

Windshield wipers and why the ball goes down

Have you ever wondered how a tennis player can hit a forehand that flies over the net four feet high and still lands inside the baseline? Looking at it, I thought the ball would fly out, but it didn't, thanks to the spin. In the final moments before and during impact, your forearm flips rapidly, the racket surface sweeping up and across the ball almost like a windshield wiper. That motion creates heavy topspin, and the topspin degree does a specific thing to the balloon: because the top of the balloon is rotating forward, it pulls on the surrounding air in a way that causes the balloon to pull down, adding to the gravity that is already doing its work. The faster the ball rotates, the harder it is pulled down.

That's the whole secret behind the modern heavy spin forehand. It allows players to swing hard and aim high above the net with confidence, as the spin will do the work of pulling the ball back down the court. And once the ball hits the court, it's the spin that causes the ball to rebound strongly after bouncing, which is exactly why balls with heavy spin are so annoying to bounce back.

Two exercises worth including in every workout

The first lesson is catching and holding the racket. After each forehand, finish by using the hand not holding the racket to grab the racket's neck and raise it high over the opposite shoulder. It sounds like a small detail, but it's effective: it prevents you from over-rotating and keeps your shoulders balanced throughout the swing.

The second exercise is to practice with a tension band. Tie a rope about four feet above the net, stretched across the middle of the court. Hit thirty heavy spin forehands from the back of the court, each one flying over the rope and landing within the baseline. You can't do this without a real upward sweep, which forces you to create the steep, high-spinning swing that this whole chapter is about, and there's no way to fake it.

Chapter 8 — Classic blade: one-handed backhand, scapular power and gliding slice

Watching a nice one-handed backhand shot at slow speed, you won't see it as a technical compromise. It looked like the best shot on the course. Federer, Wawrinka, Henin, none of them play "artistically" just to look good. What you're looking at is a full-body whip, built exactly like how a good boxer creates a hook, and it can produce 100 mph drives with a disguise on the slice that no two-handed backhand can match. This chapter is about how that whiplash actually works, and how to build it without destroying your elbows along the way.

Holding the racket is the first foundation

Everything starts from the position of the knuckles on the racket handle. With one-handed left, you need to grip the Eastern backhand, bringing the base of the index finger and the base of the hand up to lie flat on the racket handle. Coaches number the edges around the handle, and this is bevel number 1.

Why is this one bevel so important? Because it places a solid bone block right behind the racket face. When a heavy ball, even at 80 mph, lands on the racquet strings, that same bone prevents the racquet frame from rotating open or closed in your hand. With the wrong grip, too much of a forehand grip or too much of a Continental, you will feel the racquet shake at impact, no matter how good your swing is.

Turn your back, turn it all the way

This is the biggest technical difference compared to the two-handed backhand: the one-handed backhand requires a much deeper rotation. While a half turn of the backhand is enough, you need to roll your shoulders to about 115 to 125 degrees, almost turning your back to the net, while turning your hips much less, about 45 degrees.

The gap between shoulder rotation and hip rotation is where the real power comes from. It energizes the muscles along the upper back, the peroneus, the middle trapezius, and the posterior deltoid, like a stretched elastic band. You will not create a strong one-handed backhand with arm strength alone. You achieve that by loading this rotation properly, then letting it unwind on its own.

Catch the ball far in front, a non-negotiable principle

If there's one rule that differentiates between a healthy one-handed backhand and a painful one, it's this: the arm should be locked straight, and you should be at contact about 40 to 45 centimeters, about half a meter, in front of the front hip.

This isn't style preference, it's physics protecting your elbows. Think of it like leverage: when you address the ball in front with straight arms, the entire arm becomes a solid, unified strut from the shoulders all the way to the face of the racket, and the large back muscles absorb the impact. Hit the ball late, even just a few centimeters from the right position, and that lever collapses. The force that should be going to the back and shoulders now goes straight to a small tendon on the outside of the elbow, the same tendon that is often blamed for tennis elbow syndrome. Constantly late to the ball, and that's exactly how you tear it.

So if you remember just one thing from this whole chapter, remember this: in front, arms straight. Everything else is just a subtle adjustment.

The other arm is equally important

This is something most players never think about but every top one-handed backhand does automatically: watch the arm that's not hitting the ball. As the hitting arm swings forward and up through the ball, the other arm, say the left if you're right-handed, swings back hard, in exactly the opposite direction. At full extension, your arms should almost form a straight line across your chest, as if you were extending your wingspan.

It's not for beauty. That's what prevents your body from rotating open too early. If the upper body is free to continue rotating towards the net as in a forehand, the racket face will drag over the ball instead of straight through it, and you will miss the ball more often than you will hit it. By throwing your other hand back, you create an equivalent counter-rotating force that locks your body at nearly 90 degrees to the touchline at impact. That's what allows the hitting arm to move through the ball in a straight, precise line toward the target, instead of being pulled sideways.

If your one-handed backhand is flying wide, before checking your grip or swing path, see what your other arm is doing. Nine times out of ten, it's collapsing too early instead of kicking back hard.

The slice, the sharpest weapon in tennis

The one-handed slice is perhaps the most useful shot in tennis, both as a defense and as a precision surgical weapon. It uses a completely different grip, the Continental at bevel 2, and a completely different swing path.

How it works: you put the racket head high, about ear level, then slice down and forward past the bottom half of the ball at about a 45 degree angle, before bringing the racket back to level at impact. Done well, this shot produces about 2,800 to 3,200 revolutions per minute of backspin, on the ball.

That amount of backspin creates a useful effect in the air: it causes the ball to ride on a cushion of air pressure underneath, so instead of falling like a normal shot, it glides through the air. A well-hit defensive slice can hover for nearly three seconds, just what you need when you're pushed off the court and need time to regain your position.

But slice isn't just a defensive time-buyer. Hit it hard and low on a hard court, the amount of backspin clings to the court on the bounce, keeping the ball gliding about 14 degrees below the bounce angle, the ball stays close to the court instead of bouncing high. Your opponent is forced to duck and lift the ball, which is much harder to attack than a ball that bounces right within their comfort range.

How Federer solved the problem with Nadal

For many years, Nadal had one of the most effective strategies in tennis history when facing Federer: creating heavy topspin, about 3,800 revolutions per minute, repeatedly hitting Federer's left shot cross-court at shoulder height. A one-handed backhand has serious difficulty with a ball that keeps climbing up into that area, with no clean way to get through it.

At the 2017 Australian Open final, at age 35 and just returning from knee surgery, Federer appeared with an answer, and it wasn't just a change in mentality. Three specific changes. He switched to a larger racquet, the 97-square-inch Pro Staff, which gave him a larger sweet spot and more stability when hitting off-center, exactly the kind of shot a tall, heavy ball would produce. He no longer stood back to wait for the topspin to fall into his comfortable hitting zone, instead stepping about a meter inside the line and hitting the ball as soon as it bounced, about 20 centimeters after hitting the ground, before it had time to climb past his waist. And instead of the arc-defensive slice he had relied on for years, he began hitting drives flat and hard, both cross-court and down the line, robbing Nadal of rhythm instead of giving him rhythm.

None of those three changes will work without the other two. Stepping inside is only effective if the racquet can withstand off-center hits, and hitting the ball early is only beneficial if you penetrate flat through it instead of trying to arc back a heavy topspin. The hidden lesson in that match is this: sometimes correcting a technical weakness isn't about honing the same shot harder, but about changing where and when you make contact.

Two exercises worth doing

Post on the fence. Stand sideways next to a yard fence, front shoulder almost touching the fence. Make 20 slow left swings, making sure the hitting arm moves forward along the fence while the other arm swings back into a full wingspan, and that your head doesn't budge even a bit throughout the swing. This is the cheapest way to feel the backswing of the wingspan without worrying about hitting a real ball.

Post target slice glide. Place two empty cans of balls on their sides about a meter away from the service line. Hit 25 low, aggressive slices so that the ball crosses the net less than 15 centimeters away and glides through the gap between the two cans without knocking them over. This drills a low, penetrating slice, not the arc-safe version that most players default to, and gives you immediate feedback on whether you're cutting down through the ball or just pushing it away.

Chapter 9 — Non-dominant hand motors: ambidextrous left, side brakes and cruise speed

I hear this question a lot: "Why is my two-handed backhand so weak and pushed, even though I'm swinging with all my might?" And almost every time the answer surprises people. It's not due to weak hand strength, nor is it due to the tightness of the grip. The problem is that you are using the wrong hand to hit this shot.

This is something coaches have misunderstood for decades. We often teach students to "pull the ball with their right hand", meaning their dominant hand does the main job. But when researchers attached sensors to the racquet handles of Djokovic, Agassi and Sinner, the data told a different story. The two-handed backhand isn't actually a "left" in the true sense, it's a powerful forehand backhand, with the dominant hand only playing an accompanying balancing role.

The upper arm is the engine, not the lower arm

Picture this: your non-dominant hand, located above the racquet handle, provides the majority of the pressure, upward stroke and forward thrust, more than 70% of the total force of the shot. The dominant hand, which is on the bottom, is actually just holding things steady. It is the anchor and guide, not the main force generator.

So if you're right-handed and can't power your left shot, I can almost guess the reason before I see you hit a single shot: your left arm is "sleeping". As soon as you start thinking of this stroke as a left forehand, rather than a "left" that the right hand is pulling through, your left pectoral, left shoulder, and left hip will wake up and work together. That's when you start to see the ball move at a gentle speed, the kind of force that crosses the net with a safe margin, instead of just trying hard and hoping.

How do the two hands work together on the racket handle?

The stability of the entire stroke depends on how the two hands are placed together on the handle, and they are doing two very different jobs.

Your lower hand, your dominant hand, holds the Continental grip. Its task is to act as a motionless fulcrum, counteracting the tendency of the racket to rotate backwards after contact with the ball. It does not create force, it maintains the battlefield.

With your upper hand, your non-dominant hand, hold the Eastern forehand grip, placing it next to your lower hand, with no gaps. This is the hand that actually generates the power, the palm facing the target as you swing past the ball, and it is this hand that creates the topspin and thrust across the ball.

If your two-handed backstroke keeps coming out too wide or too long, nine times out of ten, it's because your hands are separated on the racket handle. Check to see if the heel of the upper hand is resting close to the thumb joint of the lower hand, it is that close distance that keeps the whole structure strong.

Low lifts: shoulder somersaults

Watch Djokovic or Sinner handle a ball that sinks to knee level, and you'll see they never bend their wrists or scoop the ball up with their elbows. Instead, they tilt the entire shoulder axis, lowering the front shoulder and raising the back shoulder, about 35 to 45 degrees. As they release the force of their rotation, the back shoulder almost somersaults up and over the front shoulder, like a side somersault.

This adjustment does a lot of things. It helps you lift a low, sinking ball without bending over or scooping the ball with your hands, and it produces heavy topspin, around 2,800 revolutions per minute, with enough distance over the net, often more than a meter, to let you swing freely without fear of catching the net.

If you find yourself bending at the waist to get under a low ball, that's your cue: drop your front shoulder into position and push your back shoulder up over it, instead of bending at the hips.

Why does the two-handed backhand prevail in a sliding situation?

This is where the two-handed backhand really surpasses the one-handed backhand, the ability to hit with real power even when you're stretched out and sliding.

Picture Djokovic rushing three meters out to his left corner. He put his outside leg down at about a 45-degree angle and slid more than a meter, absorbing a force equivalent to 3.2 times his body weight through his thighs and buttocks as he descended. That's a significant eccentric load to control, and that's why leg technique and leg strength are as important as arm technique here.

Since both hands are on the racket, the racket frame resists rotation much better than holding one hand, about 45% more torsional stability, a large part of the reason he can hit the ball cleanly even when sliding or flying without the racket surface shaking. And because his hips remain square to the court throughout, he doesn't need to turn around to get back into position. He just pushed off from his sliding outside leg, and was back near the center of the baseline in less than half a second, under 450 milliseconds if you want a specific number.

The Sinner cruises at a gentle 85 miles per hour

If you want to see the upper limit of this shot, check out Jannik Sinner. His two-handed backhand isn't just fast, it's the fastest and most consistent baseline shot on the pro scene, maintaining speeds of 82 to 85 miles per hour during sustained shots, and he does it without looking like he's exerting any effort.

There are three factors that contribute to this. First, his grip on the racket is extremely light, about 2.5 on a scale of 10. That relaxation allows the racket head to naturally drop below the ball thanks to gravity and inertia, instead of creating racket head speed by exerting force. Second, his swing back is very compact, the hands never move back from the plane of the spine, all the load comes from a rotation of about 50 degrees of the body, not a big swing back with the hands. Third, he contacts the ball with the racket surface almost flat, about 85 degrees, which is what creates the depth and penetrating power of the shot, the kind that pushes the opponent three meters behind the baseline and always has to defend.

Two exercises to build this technique

Forehand exercises only use your non-dominant hand. Completely release your dominant hand from the racket, holding the racket only with your non-dominant hand with the Eastern forehand grip. Hit the ball consistently from baseline for ten minutes, trying to create real topspin and depth. This is not a trick, this is how you awaken the motor pathways that make your left arm the prime mover instead of the passenger.

The post slides and brakes into the target. Place a cone in the deep corner along the touchline. Sprint sideways, slide into an open position on the outside leg, brake through the front hip, and hit a two-handed backhand along the way at the target. This exercise trains correct foot form and hip brakes to help you hit with power even when stretched far and out of balance.

Chapter 10 — Breathtaking close combat: volley straight punches, soft hands, and the art of transitions

The net is the most honest place on the tennis court. There is no time to think, no room for a wide swing, and no tolerance for a hesitant hand. I often tell my students that at the back of the court, you are still allowed to make mistakes, but at the net, the ball judges you in a split second. So this chapter does not teach you "how to play volley" in a theoretical sense. It teaches you how to stand in front of a ball flying at over a hundred kilometers per hour and still keep a cool head.

Don't swing the racket, block the ball

The biggest mistake I see with players practicing volley is that they treat it like a miniature forehand. The ball came quickly, instinct took control, and the racket swung back as if they were standing at the back of the court. At net, that instinct punishes you immediately. Imagine: you stand about nine meters away from your opponent, the ball flies at more than 130 km/h. You just need to pull the racket back sixty centimeters to "gain momentum", then by the time the momentum swing is complete, the ball will have flown past the place you intended to hit long ago. You are pressed, touch the ball behind your back, and hit it out or into the net.

The fix is ​​not to swing the racket more skillfully. But not swinging the racket at all. Good volley players always raise the racket early, turn their shoulders and hold the racket face in front of their chest before the ball arrives, like you are holding up a shield rather than using the momentum of a baseball bat. Then, instead of swinging, they just step forward to catch the ball and let their body weight do the main work. The arm was almost still, just a short, firm punch, about ten centimeters, exactly like the action of stamping a stamp on an envelope. The image I want you to keep in mind is simple: volley is not a swing, it is a step and a block. The legs provide power by pushing weight into the ball. The hand's only job is to keep the racket surface steady and create a clean contact at the point of contact. Get this idea, half of your volley problems will disappear after just a few practice sessions.

Soft hands: drain energy from the ball

Now comes the opposing skill, and it's almost magical when you first learn it. Look at Roger Federer or John McEnroe doing a small volley that just skims the net and then dies on the spot. The ball had just flown at more than 130 km/h, yet it left their racket with almost no force left, falling gently. The secret is that they relax, just a little, right at the decisive moment.

In the fraction of a second before the ball arrives, a player with good feel will loosen his grip on the racket, from a firm grip to an almost light one, like holding a small bird without wanting to startle it. When the ball hits the strings, that soft grip allows the racket head to move slightly back a few inches instead of standing stiffly. That small concession absorbs most of the ball's energy instead of bouncing it back. At the same time, the player gently glides the racket underneath the ball, adding a little backspin. The combination of a soft, absorbent grip, and a subtle backspin stroke is what causes the ball to die as soon as it hits the court instead of bouncing on.

The lesson I always repeat is: hard hands create speed, soft hands destroy speed. If your volley small ball keeps flying over the service line, the error usually lies not in the path of the racket but in the grip. Relax just before contact, as if you were trying not to break an egg, and let the racket absorb the shock instead of resisting it.

Lower your body without breaking technique

Why do so many low volleys end up in the grid? Because when the ball drops below the edge of the net, most players make the same mistake: bending at the waist and letting the racket head drop as if swinging a golf club. That one move ruins the shot in three ways at once, collapsing the wrist angle, opening the racket face abnormally, and taking your eye off the ball's flight just when you need it most.

A better way is to lower yourself with your legs, not your spine. As the ball comes down, lower your back knee to the ground, sort of like a lunge, bringing it just a few inches off the ground, while keeping your upper body upright. This creates a subtle yet powerful effect: because the spine remains straight instead of bent forward, your eyes remain level with the ball flight, and your balance and depth perception remain sharp when needed. At the same time, keep the racket head always higher than your wrist, never letting it drop down. The power to lift the ball should come from the legs straightening again, not from the wrists flicking up. Think "squat and then lift", not "scoop the ball up". Bend your knees, don't bend your back. Lift with your feet, not your wrists. That's the entire rule, and it fixes more lower volley errors than any other technical tweak.

Survive shootouts at the net

Sometimes, especially in doubles, you find yourself in a purely reflexive confrontation: both players close to the net, the ball flying back and forth from a distance of only a few meters, faster than the ability to think consciously. This is the "shootout" at the net, and it rewards preparation, not talent.

There are two things that separate the survivors from the overwhelmed. One, keep your elbows always in front of your body, about twenty centimeters in front of your chest, neither close to your ribs nor behind. If your elbows drift behind your body, the ball will press against your body before you can react, and you won't have any space to block. Two, don't change the way you hold the racket. At this speed there is no time to switch from forehand to backhand grip and back again. Keep a neutral, versatile grip, and just rotate the racquet around your elbow fairly steadily, like a small shield that rotates left and then right. Let your hands react instinctively, don't try to "aim" each shot. These plays look chaotic in the moment, but whoever stays neat, keeps their elbows forward, and resists the temptation to overthink will almost always win.

Quick diagnostic panel

If your volley is having problems, here's a quick way to find the cause. Swinging the racket or hitting the ball is often an old habit from hitting the ground, you still pull the racket back before touching the ball. I call it the back-to-the-wall exercise: stand about five inches away from a fence and practice volley without letting your racket touch the fence, which physically prevents momentum from gaining momentum. Volley flying too high is usually caused by your hands being too stiff when handling the ball on your shoulder, you are pushing the racket up instead of hitting it through. With a high float ball, don't block, attack: step in and hit the ball down with topspin instead of trying to place it gently. Low Volley falls into the net by bending at the waist with straight legs, allowing the racket head to fall below the wrist. The knee bend exercise I just mentioned above will fix it. Hitting the racket with fast shots is often because you glance at your opponent right before contact, your head moves before the ball. The gaze locking exercise is to keep your eyes fixed on the point of contact until the ball has long left the string.

Two exercises brought to the field

The first lesson is a gunfight exercise to train reflexes. Stand with a partner about three meters apart, both in the service area, facing the net. Volley back and forth as fast as possible, don't let the ball bounce, always keep your elbows forward. Try to maintain a streak of thirty consecutive strokes. This exercise trains your hands and reflexes to work faster than your mind, exactly the skills you need in a real gunfight. The second exercise is a sensory-targeted exercise. Place an empty basket of balls about two feet away from the net on the other side of the court. Have your partner practice passing strong and fast cross-court shots, then your task is to make small volley drops that fall into, or within thirty centimeters of, the basket. This exercise builds soft hands and ball feel to turn a strong blow coming from your opponent into a delicate drop that cannot be returned. Master the four concepts: the step-and-block punch, the soft-handed drop shot, the low-ball knee bend, and the forward elbow position in shooting, and the net will no longer be a place you shy away from. It will be where you end up scoring.

Chapter 11 — Conquering the sky: spin attack and smash

There is a moment in every point when the ball leaves the ground and everything changes completely. Below baseline, tennis is a game of angles and power. When the ball flies high, it's a completely different sport, about timing, about deceiving your opponent, and about having the courage to jump when your instincts are screaming to back off. I like to call this aerial part the "sky war", because whoever controls the altitude usually controls the score. This chapter is about the weapons that will help you win that fight: the offensive topspin lob, the defensive high lob, and the smash to finish the point in the air.

Lob topspin attacks

Most players think of the lob as a last resort, the shot you make when you're behind the baseline and have no options left. The attacking topspin lob is the exact opposite. It is the weapon you actively choose, aiming at a player who has net play and thinks he has won for sure.

The usual situation is this: your opponent net play is after a strong approach shot, and you are about to hit a passing shot, or at least he thinks so. You take a normal, firm position, exactly like the position used for a forehand down the line. He read the intention, stood firmly, lowered his body, preparing to block the low volley. At that moment you take away his advantage. Instead of hitting through the ball, you lower the racquet head under the ball for the last split second and sweep uphill, sort of like a clean, powerful version of the forehand topspin you still hit, only pointing up toward the sky instead of forward. The ball leaves the racket with very heavy topspin and passes high enough over the net to be beyond the reach of volley's hand. For a moment, it looked like the ball would go out, but then the spin kicked in, the ball dived hard and landed deep near the baseline instead of drifting out to the fence. That dive is the secret of the entire shot, it makes the opponent unable to do anything but turn around and watch the ball fall.

The two deciding factors are deception and racket sweep angle. If the stance or momentum reveals the intention to lob too early, volley's hand will be able to retreat and catch the ball. If the swing is too flat, more like a push than a sweep, the ball won't have enough spin to come back and fly out. Practice this uphill sweep until it is as natural as your normal topspin.

Moonball high defensive lob

Not every lob is meant to attack. Sometimes you're just trying to survive. Imagine: your opponent hits a sharp angled shot that pulls you completely outside the doubles line. You're tense, off balance, and far from ready. Trying to hit a winning shot now is asking for trouble, because you don't have enough balance or ability to cover the court to bear the consequences. What you need is time.

That's what a high defensive lob provides. Open the racket face and lift the ball into a soaring arc, as high as possible. A ball that reaches a top of about six or seven meters will hover in the air for nearly three seconds before falling. Three seconds may not sound like much until you're the one sprinting back to the center of the court, but that's enough to regain your balance, catch your breath, and reposition yourself for the next shot. I want to emphasize: this is not an embarrassing shot. Many of the world's best defensive players rely on it constantly. It's not a concession, it's a real tool.

Pull-pull smash: ends the aerial point

If the lob is about patience and deception, the smash is about explosion. And no one did it better than Pete Sampras, who turned the scissor-kick into one of the sport's most iconic shots.

The instinct of most movement players when they see a lob ball flying over their heads is to retreat: run backwards, chest still facing the net, trying to follow the ball. This method is not effective. You lose your balance, misjudge the depth, and often miss the edge of the racket. The solution is almost the opposite of instinct: as soon as you see the lob ball fly over your head, turn your hips sideways and turn your back partially towards the net. Then step back with quick, crossed steps, similar to the steps you would use to chase a wide ball, rather than walking backwards.

While tracking the ball, raise the hand that is not holding the racket and point directly at the ball. Not just for beauty. Keeping your eyes fixed on one point, your fingertips, helps your inner ears and eyes agree on your position in space, which is important when you're about to leave the ground. Put your weight on your back foot and jump to catch the ball at the top of your reach, in front of your body, instead of letting the ball fall close to your ear. This is the part that makes Sampras' smash stronger than the average player: in the air, he kicks his front leg forward and his back leg back, like a pair of closing scissors. That counter-rotation movement isn't just stylish, it provides support for the core and shoulders to grip while suspended, allowing the arms to swing past the ball at tremendous rotational speed. The result is a smash that falls like a sledgehammer, at a speed exceeding 160 km/h, often falling straight into the stands before the opponent can turn around. You don't need timing like Sampras to benefit: even a simple version of the hip-hinge-then-jump sequence will improve your smash more than almost any other single technique change.

Backhand smash and tweener

Occasionally a lob drifts past your non-dominant shoulder, too far to wrap your body into a normal forehand smash. Trying to force yourself to play forehand in this situation usually ends badly: you reach over your body, lose your balance, and have no significant power left. A better choice is the backhand smash. Switch the grip to the Eastern backhand grip just like you used for the one-handed backhand and slice. Turn your back almost completely toward the net, allowing your shoulders to roll deeply, essentially gaining momentum like a discus thrower. Then push your elbow up and pop your forearm out at the highest point, sending the ball cross-court into the service box. This won't be your greatest weapon, but it's the shot every serious player should have in their bag, for those days when the lob catches you on the wrong side.

The tweener, hitting the ball back between the legs, was once just a show to get applause, like Yannick Noah did. Today, players like Federer and Alcaraz use it as a real ball-saving option when running at full speed away from the net, chasing a lob that has no other way to reach it. The mechanism is simpler than it looks: you run past the ball, letting it fall behind you instead of turning around and hitting it normally. Once the ball has dropped close to shin height, you flick the racket forward between your legs with a quick flick of the wrist, sending the ball over the net, often with enough backspin and force to prevent your opponent's net play hand from turning in time. Not a shot to build a play around, but when you're already beaten and running in the opposite direction, it's better than losing the point directly.

The above errors are not common

Stumbling or wobbling when backing up to a deep tire is often because you are running back with your chest facing the net instead of turning your body. Correct it by turning your hips sideways at the first step, then step back with a diagonal step as if chasing a wide ball. Hitting the net is often caused by letting the ball fall too low and hitting it behind your head. Point your bare hands at the ball and jump from your back foot to catch the ball in front of your body. The topspin lob that flies out is due to a flat swing instead of a sweep; lower the racket head below the ball and sweep upward, like a strong topspin forehand. The backhand smash hits the edge of the racket because you hold the Continental grip with the racket face open; Switch completely to Eastern's backhand grip before swinging to have a solid fulcrum behind the ball.

Exercises on the field

The first exercise is a jump-pull-pull sequence. Have your partner practice standing at the service line and repeatedly toss high lobs toward your deep baseline. For each fruit, rotate your hips sideways, take three quick cross-steps back, jump from your back foot, and try an aerial pull-up. The goal is to hit ten consecutive smashes deep enough to fly over the fence if there is no barrier. The second lesson is to deceive with passing shot. Place a goal, a bucket or goalpost, at the deep baseline. Hit the ball back and forth normally from the back of the court. When encountering a pre-determined short ball, position yourself as if you are about to hit a strong passing shot along the touchline, then at the last moment switch to a lob topspin. The goal is to get the ball to fall within half a meter of the target, so you practice both deception and accuracy at the same time.

Chapter 12 — Destruction with velvet: the art of the owl in disguise

I want you to stop thinking that a drop shot is a "nice" shot. Not at all. It was cruelty wrapped in politeness. Look closely at what Alcaraz or Federer actually do with this shot: they cause an opponent running back at full speed to suddenly brake, reverse their body weight, which has been fully concentrated in the opposite direction, and then rush forward dozens of feet only to see the ball die just inches above the edge of the net. It was not a light blow. It's a trap, and those who fall into it often lose both points and spirit crystals.

Lessons from Alcaraz

What makes Alcaraz's little shot so scary is not its pure technique but its disguise, and it's more perfect than many people think. Amateur players often "sell out" their shot before even touching the ball. They slow down, change grip early, stand up straight, and push the ball gently forward. Any opponent will realize that in half a second and immediately rush to punish.

Alcaraz does the opposite. He rotates his shoulders and puts his weight on his back leg just like he's about to hit a high-speed forehand, fully curled up, fully committed, holding nothing back. That fierce preparation is not just for show, it is what tricks the opponent's brain. When you see someone in such a turning position, your own motor system begins to imitate, activating your legs to prepare to turn and run back before you can even consciously decide. Coaches call this the mirror neuron effect: you're back before the ball is actually hit, because the spirit system sees the "big swing coming" and reacts instinctively. Then, just before the moment of contact, just the last thirty milliseconds, Alcaraz did what almost no one else could do at that speed: he quietly killed the shot. His arm brakes, his grip softens, and instead of pushing through the ball, he gently cuts underneath it. By the time the opponent's brain can understand what happened, they have taken a few steps in the wrong direction, and it takes nearly a third of a second just to realize the mistake, before even starting to correct it. On the tennis court, it's the line between being on time and not being on time.

What really happens in your hands

Let's talk about grip strength, because this is really the technical secret, and it's simpler than you think. When hitting a normal baseline drive, you squeeze your grip quite tightly, about a 7 or 8 on a scale of 10. A tight grip helps transfer energy to the ball effectively. And that's exactly what you don't want in a small shot. In the final moments before contact, you let your grip soften, down to a size of 2 out of 10, barely touching the racket. Why it's important: when the ball hits a loosely held racket, the racket's frame will actually bounce a bit, recoil a few inches in your hand. That bounce absorbs most of the ball's energy right at the palm and handle, instead of shooting it back at the opponent. You don't slow the ball down by pushing lightly, you let the racket itself absorb the impact.

At the same time, you slice very slightly under the ball, creating a very strong amount of backspin. That's what causes the ball to stop when it bounces instead of sliding forward. Backspin bites the surface of the court upon landing and causes the ball to either die in place, or even spin back into the net. That bounce turns a short ball into one that is impossible to handle. The whole sequence of movements boils down to this: a fierce build-up that looks realistic, then soft hands and cuts under the ball at the last moment, then a strong backspin that kills the ball on the bounce. The camouflage brings surprise, while the handle and spin bring the ball dead.

When should you actually pull the trigger?

A small miss at the wrong time is just a free gift to your opponent. Before deciding, I tell my students to check three things. One, the opponent is standing quite far behind the baseline, at least a few meters, the farther back they are, the more they have to run. Two, you are standing inside your own baseline, having stepped forward to handle a short ball. Trying to hit a small shot while standing far behind the baseline will cause the ball to stay in the air for too long, allowing any decent opponent to catch up. Three, the opponent's weight is moving sideways or backward, they are on the wrong foot, the momentum is in the wrong direction, and now it must be reversed.

When all three come together, you're not just hitting a soft shot, you're exploiting the entire length of the court, forcing your opponent to cover about twelve meters from front to back in just one point. Even if they somehow make it in time before the second bounce, they have to dive, knee close to the court, passively scooping up the ball. That clears the way perfectly for you with an easy volley or passing shot on the next ball.

Read the pitch

Not every course rewards small putts the same, and it's worth training yourself to recognize the difference. Clay courts are the king of junior court surfaces. The loose surface grips the ball and cancels out its momentum, so a ball with strong backspin will freeze in place. If you see your opponent standing about fifteen feet away from the baseline on clay, that's an invitation. The grass field behaves differently, the ball slides low and fast instead of bouncing, just a few inches above the turf. It's not as deadly as on clay, but the low slide combined with the fear of slipping creates a unique kind of hesitation in your opponent, and that hesitation buys you more time. The hard court is in the middle. Here, the more effective way to play is often the diagonal cut, cutting the ball short and wide into the service box, towards the touchline, so that you not only force your opponent to run up but also pull them out of the field horizontally.

The most common errors I encounter

The number one mistake is selling the shot too early. If you slow down or change your grip a second or two before touching the ball, you give your opponent warning. Correct it by preparing with the same intense momentum as a real drive, and only change at the last moment, not before. The second mistake is pushing the ball high: if you still squeeze your hands tightly and push the ball forward with your arms, the ball will fly long or bounce high and short, which is the result your opponent wants. Correct it by softening your hands to a 2 out of 10 feel and letting the racket absorb the force instead of using your own force. Mistake three is hitting the ball into the net, usually because you cut straight down on the ball without creating an arc. Give yourself a real safe distance, aim about thirty to forty-five centimeters through the net. It sounds like a lot, but when you see how much distance professional tennis players use once the feeling of touching the ball is correct, you will see that it is very reasonable. The fourth error is hitting while standing too far behind the baseline, the ball is in the air too long and gives the opponent enough time to recover. Simple rule: only play small when your foot is inside your baseline.

Exercises to build your swing

The hula hoop game is very useful. Place a hula hoop directly behind the net, in your partner's serving box. Have your partner practice passing the ball short, in the middle of the field. Your task is to gain intense momentum each time like a real forehand, brake and soften your hand at the last moment, and then release the ball into the hoop. Aim for 8 out of 10 times the ball falls into the hoop without showing any signs of it, without slowing down, without changing grip early. The second lesson is to skip small and then lob the ball consecutively. Hit a small disguised shot to pull your partner up close to the net, then immediately follow it up with a topspin lob that attacks over their head, deep into the back of the court. This exercise is intentionally very harsh, it teaches you to fully exploit the entire length of the court, both front and back, in one spot. Practice these two exercises long enough, and the small drop will go from being a game of chance to a weapon you can rely on when you need it.

Chapter 13 — The Invulnerable Athlete: Tendon Health, Joint Protection, and Recovery

I coached picturesque bats at the age of seventeen, but by twenty-five I couldn't raise my arms above my shoulders. Also coached people who were still plowing three intense sets at the age of fifty. The difference is almost never in talent. It lies in whether they understand what their bodies really need to survive years of hitting the ball hard, over and over again, on a hard court that never holds back.

Your body is not a machine. It's living tissue that adapts to load, or degrades under that load, depending on how you treat it. This chapter is about the difference between those two outcomes, and about how to stay on the field when most of your peers have retired due to pain.

Tennis elbow pain isn't what you think

For decades, doctors called it "tendinitis." Tennis elbow, patellar tendon pain, all common overuse injuries in racket sports. The word "inflammation" makes everyone think that the tendon is inflamed, like a sprained ankle, and only needs rest and anti-inflammatory medication to heal. Now we know that's wrong, and it completely changes the way you treat it.

When your elbow tendon weakens from years of gripping and hitting the ball, it doesn't become inflamed. It degenerates. Think of the tendons as a bundle of ropes, all running neatly parallel to withstand tension. Under chronic load, the bundle frays, the fibers lose their parallel arrangement, disorganized blood vessels grow into the nearly avascular tissue, and the entire structure becomes weaker and stiffer. Doctors call this tendinosis, not tendinitis, and the distinction is more than just a matter of words.

Here's why it's important: if you take ibuprofen every time your elbow hurts, thinking you're soothing the inflammation, you're actually doing yourself a disservice. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs paralyze the very cell signals that tendons need to regenerate themselves. You're hiding the pain while silently hindering your own repair process.

So what actually cures it? Load, right type, right dose. The tendon only heals by being asked to do the same thing that hurt it, in a careful and controlled manner. This is where the FlexBar comes into play. If you've never used one, buy one. It's a flexible rubber bar, and the exercise, often called the Tyler Twist after the physical therapist who popularized it, requires you to twist the bar with an eccentric contraction of the forearm muscles: you control the bar as it slowly untwists, resisting force all the way, four seconds at a time, three sets of fifteen reps, every day.

That slow stretching under tension is the signal that tendon cells need. It activates fibroblasts within the tendon and promotes genes that produce new, organized collagen. Stick with it for six to eight weeks and you will feel the difference, not because the pain disappears overnight, but because the tissue has actually regenerated.

Shoulder problems go unnoticed until they are serious

If there's one type of injury I wish every serious tennis player understood before it happened, this is it. It's called GIRD, which stands for Glenohumeral Internal Rotation Deficit, and it's the biggest predictor of rotator cuff and labrum tears in overhead athletes.

It forms like this. Every time you serve, the shoulder not only throws the ball with force, it also has to decelerate an arm that is moving at explosive speed, and that deceleration load is placed behind the shoulder capsule, over and over again thousands of times each season. Over time, that tissue thickens and contracts. When that happens, the humeral head is pulled slightly upward and backward, away from its ideal position. And when the joint socket and head are no longer in the right place, each serve begins to compress the rotator cuff tendon and rub against the marginal cartilage, the rim of cartilage that holds the shoulder joint together.

What is difficult to realize is that this does not manifest itself as pain immediately. It manifests itself through the gradual loss of internal rotation in the batting shoulder. You can reach farther behind your back with your non-playing hand than with your serving hand, and you may not even realize it. Number to remember: total range of motion, external rotation plus internal rotation, should be kept relatively equal between both shoulders. If internal rotation in the ball shoulder is reduced more than 20 degrees compared to the other shoulder, or if the total combined range is reduced more than 5 degrees, you are in the danger zone for a labral meniscus tear.

The fix is ​​incredibly simple and only takes three minutes a day. It's called a "sleeper stretch". Lie on your side with your hitting arm, shoulder bent 90 degrees, elbow bent, and gently use your other hand to press your forearm into the floor. Hold for thirty seconds, three rounds, every day, ideally right after working out. That gentle, prolonged stretch keeps the back of the shoulder capsule from contracting in the first place. It is the cheapest insurance policy that exists against a labral meniscus tear.

Why do many young tennis players have to have hip surgery?

This surprised many people. Hip meniscus tears used to be very rare in tennis. Now it appears in players under the age of twenty-five, and the culprit is not age, but the modern open forehand on hard courts.

Think about what happens when you put power into an extended standing forehand and your front foot lands straight ahead, perpendicular to the goal line. The hips are forced into extreme internal rotation to get into that position. Then when you turn decisively to hit the ball, the bone protrusion at the top of the femur, which some people naturally have a more pronounced protrusion, hits the hip socket rim with real force, every time you hit that shot. Do this thousands of times in your career and you're slowly wearing away the cartilage that's supposed to last a lifetime.

The fix doesn't cost a penny. When you get into position for the open forehand, turn your front leg out, about 40 to 45 degrees, instead of straight forward. That slight rotation brings the hip head back into the center of the socket instead of pressing it against the rim of the joint. Same shot, same force, same camouflage, just a joint that doesn't gradually destroy itself with each swing. If you coach children, this is something worth hammering into their footsteps now, before habits and trauma have formed.

Train around injury without losing strength

This is a situation every serious tennis player eventually encounters: something hurts, heavy weight training is impossible because it puts too much pressure on the joints, but you still need to keep your muscles strong so you don't lose what you've built. This is where the blood flow restriction exercise method, referred to as BFR (Blood Flow Restriction), comes into play.

The idea sounded strange at first. You wrap a pressure cuff around your upper arm or leg, inflate it enough to partially restrict blood flow, and then lift very light weights, about 20 to 30 percent of the maximum weight you can lift at one time. Not heavy at all. But because the muscle is working under conditions of restricted blood flow, it fatigues very quickly and accumulates metabolic products just like during a really hard set, even though the load on the joint remains minimal.

The body responds to that metabolic stress by releasing a massive burst of growth hormone and other recovery signals, promoting muscle growth and helping tendon collagen regenerate, with almost none of the joint wear and tear that heavy weight training causes. If you are recovering from an injury, this is something worth asking your physiotherapist directly, as it has become a mainstay in professional tennis players' recovery programmes.

Sleep is not optional

Federer is said to have slept ten to twelve hours a day for most of his career. That's not laziness or luxury. It was one of the smartest performance decisions he ever made, and most players completely underestimate it.

Deep sleep is not a time of passive rest. That's when the body does the real work of recovery. During the deepest stages of sleep, the pituitary gland releases most of the day's growth hormone, which is responsible for repairing microscopic muscle damage from exercise and refilling glycogen stores burned on the field. Not getting enough sleep means you're undercutting the greatest recovery tool available, after all, no other supplement or recovery device can compare.

The brain also benefits. While you sleep, the brain goes through a sort of cleaning cycle overnight, clearing away the metabolic waste that accumulates from a day of intense focus and reflexes. That's part of the reason why a well-rested player reacts faster and sees the ball better than a sleep-deprived player, actually having a cleaner nervous system, not just a feeling of freshness. If you're serious about tennis, treat your sleep schedule with the same discipline as your training schedule.

An eight-minute warm-up before each game

You don't need a fancy routine, you need a regular routine. This one takes just eight minutes and covers the most vulnerable joints: resistance band pull-ups for the rotator cuff, fifteen times with a light band to wake up the small stabilizing muscles around the scapula; Stretch the muscles in the sleeping position for thirty seconds on each arm to expand the range of motion of the shoulder; horizontal stepping with a resistance band twenty steps in each direction to activate the lateral hip muscles to absorb the lateral load; and hold a Spanish squat for forty-five seconds at a 90-degree knee bend to wake up the quads and soothe irritation around the patellar tendon.

Do this exercise before playing, not after. The body reacts to warming up differently depending on whether the tissues are prepared or not, and eight minutes is a small price to pay for a career that doesn't end prematurely.

Chapter 14 — The inner fortress: bravery spirit, tiebreakers, and stoic stillness

Tennis is a very strange type of fighting. No coach can step on the field with you. No teammates will replace you when you are exhausted. There's no clock to wait out, no wall to hide from, just seventy-eight feet of empty air between you and another human trying to take the match from you. And here's what I want you to think about: a winner is not someone who is never afraid. That is someone who has learned how to turn that fear into sanity.

Truth 54%

I want to start with a number that will change the way most players think about this sport, once it really takes hold. Novak Djokovic wins about 54.5% of the points he plays in his career. Rafael Nadal 54.2%. Roger Federer 54.1%. Let that sink in: the three greatest tennis players of this era, over thousands of matches and championships, won just over half the points they played. Djokovic, even on a dominant Grand Slam championship run, still lost about 4.6 out of every 10 points along the way.

Now think about how an amateur tennis player reacts when he misses a serve or misses an easy shot. Throwing his hands in the air, mumbling curses, then four games in a row were given away in a slow collapse. That's the perfectionist illusion at work: the belief, often unspoken, that a good player shouldn't miss any shots.

The best tennis players in the world have made peace with another truth, and I call it the 54% rule. You will miss, often, and sometimes at the worst possible time. You will get a wrong refereeing decision. The ball will hit the edge of the net and fall through into a winner you don't deserve. None of this is a sign that something is wrong. It's simply tennis. Players who handle this best treat each point as a separate, self-contained world. The last point has disappeared. There is only this breath, this toss, this ball.

Djokovic's fortress: calm the alarm system

At the 2019 Wimbledon final, Federer was only two points away from winning the championship, 8-7, 40-15, serving, with fifteen thousand spectators on Center Court screaming his name. There could hardly be a more hostile moment to be on the other side of the net. Djokovic's heart rate did not spike. His eyes were not absentmindedly looking at the crowd. He watched Federer's toss, stepped in, and unleashed a forehand passing shot along the unreachable string. He went on to win the championship.

How can a human do that? Much of it lies in keeping the brain's alarm system, the amygdala, from taking control. Under extreme pressure, the amygdala can take over: hands shake, breathing becomes shallow and heavy in the chest, and the conscious mind begins to chatter in doubt just when you need it to be quiet the most. What Djokovic and players like him have forged is a way to stop that before it starts. The tool is almost embarrassingly simple: the breath.

Specifically, the breathing pattern is 1:2 ratio, inhaling for about three seconds, exhaling for about six seconds. That longer exhale stimulates the spirit vagus nerve, the body's natural brake pedal for the spirit meridian system, and it can lower the heart rate by about 15 beats per minute in just a few seconds. When the alarm system calms down, the analytical part of the brain also calms down, and the body is free to execute the shot it has been trained to do thousands of times. It's the state top athletes describe as "the ball looks bigger" or "everything slows down." Not a miracle. It is a spirit system that no longer fights against itself.

The next time your chest tightens before a critical point, take one slow breath, with the exhale twice as long as the inhale. Don't rush into the score right away. Let the exhalation do its work first.

Nadal's ritual: armor made of repetition

Watching Nadal between the points, to a normal eye, looks like someone with OCD. Water bottles lined up. Wiper. Touch the back of your pants, touch both shoulders, tuck your hair behind both ears, touch your nose, bounce the ball the same number of times before serving. That's not superstition. It is one of the smartest designs of the spirit system in this subject.

The logic here is: each of those small actions is exactly the same, point after point, no matter what just happened. Missing an easy volley in 0-0 set one? Still the same ritual. Serve to win Roland Garros? Same ritual, same order, same rhythm. By keeping the physical dance completely constant, Nadal strips the moment of its emotional weight. His body couldn't distinguish between a normal score and a champion score, because it was doing exactly the same thing. That familiarity brings a sense of calm. Ritual becomes a kind of armor, a solid support to return to when everything else in that moment is chaos.

You don't need Nadal's exact sequence of actions. What you need is a small, repeatable routine of your own, something you do the same way at each point, to give your mind something to do instead of worrying about the score.

Talk to yourself like a coach

Pay attention to your inner voice next time you're under pressure. For most players, especially when things start to go downhill, it sounds like: "I can't believe I just missed a shot. I'm falling apart. My backhand was terrible today." It's self-talk in the first person, and there's real research behind why it's harmful. Psychologist Ethan Kross at the University of Michigan has shown that talking to yourself in the first person when stressed tends to amplify the emotional response instead of dampening it. You remain stuck inside that emotion instead of creating any distance from it.

The fix is ​​incredibly simple but effective: talk to yourself in the third person, by name. Like: "Okay, Henry, light feet, slightly bent knees, eyes on the toss. Hit the inside-out forehand and go all in on it." When you say your name like that, you create just enough psychological distance to stop being the panicked victim of the moment, and become the coach who calmly stands next to you, giving clear instructions. That shift alone tends to stabilize fine motor control, the small, precise muscle work the forearm needs for a clean stroke, and that's exactly what gets tightened up first when anxiety takes over.

Sixteen seconds by Jim Loehr

Sports psychologist Jim Loehr spent years studying the top 100 tennis players in the world, and what he found surprised many people: the gap between a top 10 champion and a talented but only decent player lies primarily in what happens during a point match. It's about what happens in the sixteen seconds between points, the part of the game that no one really pays attention to.

Loehr divides that period into four stages, and it's worth embracing them by feel. In the first few seconds after the point ends, your job is simply to react well physically, walking with your head high and shoulders straight, no matter what just happened. That posture is not for decoration, it actually has the opposite effect on how you feel. In the next stretch, you allow yourself to cool down, take a long exhalation, perhaps readjust the racquet strings, giving your heart rate space to decrease. Then there's a short period of time to refocus your strategy: decide, specifically, where you want to hit the first shot of the next point and with what kind of spin. And in the last few seconds before serving or receiving, you settle into whatever little physical ritual anchors you, then lock eyes with the ball.

Players who manage this rhythm well can bring their heart rate from around 170 beats per minute down to 130 before the next point begins. It's the difference between entering the next destination exhausted and confused, versus entering with a clear head. Sixteen seconds between points is not downtime. That's where the real battle is decided.

The stoic's arena

Long before anyone was stringing a racket, the Stoic philosopher Epictetus wrote something that resonates perfectly with the tennis court: there are things that are within our power, and there are things that are not. Our own judgment, actions, efforts, these are ours. Everything else, how our body appears in that moment, what others think of us, events that are beyond our choice, are not entirely in our hands.

On the tennis court, that line is easy to draw. Out of your control: the wind blowing across the field, a bad refereeing decision, a lucky winner hitting the edge of the net from your opponent, a hostile crowd. Within your control: the effort you put into the next ball, your breathing and body language, your game plan, how much effort you put into the shot.

The best athletes practice something akin to amor fati, loving their fate, or at least accepting it. They don't fight the unlucky bounce, they see it as just another piece of information and move on. That stillness in the midst of chaos is not coldness. It is freedom, freedom from wasting energy on things that were never yours to change. In a game-deciding tiebreak, this stillness is even more valuable, because it's what keeps you playing according to your plan instead of huddling and waiting for your opponent to make a mistake.

Chapter 15 — Micromachines: what really happens inside your joints

I'm often asked why some players can absorb tremendous impact, a heavy topspin to the wrist, a full-speed dive into a lunge position, a 130 mph serve tearing through the forearm, and then continue playing, pain-free, match after match. The answer does not lie in willpower. It lies in the body structure. Underneath every shot you make, there's a whole layer of technique in your joints, connective membranes and cartilage that's doing more work than your muscles will ever give you credit for. Let's open that layer and see.

The hidden shoulder lift

Reach your arms straight up to serve at maximum extension, you will think the humerus simply rotates in the joint. Not so, at least not alone. If the shoulder joint had to do all the lifting on its own, the rotator cuff tendons would almost immediately get caught on the raised bone above, the acromion. It's one of the most common injuries in tennis, and the body has an incredibly well-designed solution to avoid it.

Think of your shoulder girdle as a small elevator system with three moving parts working together in sequence, rather than a single joint doing all the lifting. Your collarbone rotates backward around its own axis, almost like a vault, which lifts the roof of your shoulder and moves it out of the way. At the same time, the scapula tilts and slides along the ribcage, mainly by the serratus anterior, a muscle that radiates along the ribs. And only then does the ball-bearing joint, the joint most people think of when they say "shoulder," do its real lifting.

This is the important part of the workout: for every two degrees the upper arm moves, the shoulder blades need to rotate about one degree to keep up. Coaches call this the 2:1 cadence. When this ratio is broken, usually because the serratus anterior is weak or "asleep", the scapula can't keep up with the rhythm, the shoulder blades can't move out of the way, and each overhead stroke you make will start grinding the tendon against the bone. That's why most shoulder injury prevention at the professional level focuses not on the shoulder joint, but on awakening the muscle group that controls the scapula.

The system shares the shock of the forearm

Now visualize the moment of ball contact in a strong flat serve. Your forearm is pronate, rotating from palm up to palm down, faster than nearly any other movement the human body makes. That's a huge amount of torque going through two thin bones, the radius and ulna, and if either bone has to absorb it all on its own, you run the risk of a permanent fatigue fracture.

Nature's solution is a tough fibrous membrane called the interosseous membrane, which stretches diagonally between two bones like a drum. When the ball hits the string and the shock travels up the radius, the membrane immediately stretches and transfers about half of the load to the ulna. Instead of one bone taking the brunt of the impact, both bones share, and the elbow joint on the other end is never overloaded in one direction.

This is also why elbow pain often stems from technique rather than bad luck. If you pronate with a bent, stuck elbow, instead of letting the arm fully extend into the contact area, you're forcing that fascia to transfer the load through a joint that's not in the right position to receive it. Correct the arm extension and the shock sharing system will work the way it was designed.

Wrist safety latch

Here's a number that should make you respect your wrist more: during a heavy topspin forehand, the deceleration force the wrist experiences at the moment of impact can exceed 45 times gravity. It was an instant, violent stop for a joint made of eight small, loosely connected bones. If left completely unsupported, this type of concussion will cause two of those bones, the scaphoid and the lunate, to misalign and grind against each other, over time leading to a really serious wrist injury.

What saves you is a small but tough ligament that holds those two bones together, and more importantly, what you do about 30 milliseconds before contact with the ball. Top tennis players tighten their wrist flexors and extensors at the same time just before the ball hits, essentially clamping the entire wrist bone cluster into a solid mass. A hard mass will disperse the shock over a wide area. A loose pocket of bone is not. This is one of the easiest things to coach in this entire chapter: a soft, loose wrist at the moment of contact is not just a technical fault, but a structural weakness.

The feet change roles twice in one step

Your foot has to be two completely different tools for the same very brief moment, and it does that every time you move on the field. As soon as the heel or toe touches the ground, whether from a split-step or diving into a corner, the foot needs to "soften" immediately. The ankle rotates slightly inward (pronation), the midfoot joints unlock, and the entire structure turns into a cushion, absorbing an impact force equivalent to about three and a half times the body weight. Then less than a tenth of a second later, it has to do the exact opposite: lock up, become solid, and turn into a spring that you bounce off to forcefully change direction.

This transition from shock absorber to launcher is primarily driven by the plantar fascia, a tough band of tissue that runs along the sole of the foot, working with a muscle on the outside of the calf. It's called the windlass mechanism, and you can feel it directly: tiptoe and stretch your big toe up, you'll feel the arch of your foot automatically tighten. That is the spring being energized. Tennis players with stiff, inflexible big toes will completely lose access to this mechanism, and it is a bigger cause of chronic foot pain than many people think.

Articular cartilage: the best lubricant you'll never have to buy

The final part of the machine, and perhaps the most impressive. The cartilage lining your knee and hip joints is thinner than a credit card, yet it can withstand the contact pressure of a five-set match, enough pressure to crush most modern engineering materials. How does it do that?

Articular cartilage is mostly water, about 80%, held within a mesh of collagen and other structural fibers. Here's the key: when a sudden, strong force hits a joint, the water has nowhere to go immediately. It is trapped and pressurized within the fibrous mesh, and for a brief moment it acts as a hydraulic cushion, carrying most of the load instead of allowing the solid tissue underneath to be crushed. Additionally, your joints are lubricated by natural compounds that make the cartilage surfaces in contact with each other significantly more slippery than ice on ice. This is a feat of natural engineering, and it is also a limited resource, which is why volume management is as important as technique, throughout a long career.

How to read out microscopic errors

When something in this system goes wrong, it manifests itself in a specific, easily recognizable pattern. Sharp pain in the front and upper shoulders when hitting the ball overhead usually means the serratus anterior muscle isn't activating, so the shoulder blades aren't rotating enough to move out of the way. Practicing the serratus wall slide with a resistance band as a warm-up will teach the scapula to do its job in a 2:1 rhythm.

Wrist pain on the little finger side of the forearm after serving is often due to pronate with the elbow bent, stuck, rather than fully straightened. Practice feeling full extension into the contact area before allowing your forearm to rotate. Pain or throbbing in the inside of the knee when braking suddenly is a sign of the knee collapsing inwards (valgus) when moving sideways, corrected by keeping the kneecap in line with the second toe each time you decelerate. And heel pain or stiff arches that can't be stretched often come from a stiff big toe joint, causing the foot's spring mechanism to never be activated, so exercise your big toe to stretch to its full range every day.

Two lessons to memorize into your body

The first song is serratus wall-slide punch. Wrap a light resistance band around your wrists and stand facing a wall with your forearms resting vertically against the wall. Slide your arms up until they are fully overhead, while maintaining tension on the band and actively pushing your shoulder blades forward around your ribcage, rather than just letting them hang passively. This is the best way to teach the scapula to do its job, so that the rotator cuff doesn't have to take the brunt of the impact that the scapula should be sharing.

The second song is windlass first-toe pogo hop. Stand barefoot on the baseline. Stand on your tiptoes and actively lift your big toe as high as possible. You will feel your arch stretch immediately. Keeping that tension, perform twenty small, quick jumps, alternating between jumping straight and jumping to the sides. You're teaching your foot to find a springy, firm state on demand, which is exactly what you need in the brief moment between landing and rebounding during a real play.

Chapter 16 — Mind synchronization: formation pair, poach and play as one

I have coached many singles players, but when they step onto the doubles court, they immediately lose their way. They still hit the ball well, still move well, but what's not right is the coordination between two people, two individuals standing on the same side of the field, not a team. Here's what I want you to understand before reading any further: doubles is not singles with an extra partner. It's a completely different sport, just coincidentally sharing the same field and the same ball. The skill that separates a good doubles team from an excellent doubles team lies not in racket technique, but in synchronization. The two bodies moved as if tied together, reading each other's thoughts without speaking, filling the same space at the same moment. That is the topic of this chapter.

Golden rule: together net play

If I could only teach you one thing about doubles, I would choose this: the team that gets both players inside the service line, advancing net play, wins the majority of points. The statistics are very clear, teams with both players crossing the service line win about 72% of the points. As long as one person stays in the back of the field, that number drops straight below 50%. If you're both stuck at the back of the field, you only have about 26% left, which is considered a loss before the point starts.

So why doesn't everyone rush net play every time? Mostly because of fear. Being passed or having a tire go over your head feels very humiliating, but standing at the back of the field seems safe. But it is safety that is the problem. Doubles are decided in the air, at the net, where you can turn the corner and finish the point with just one shot. Every service game, every return shot, every rally ball you hit should have a question behind it: does this shot get both of us closer to the net?

Imagine a rope tied around both of your waists, not a real rope, but a spirit crystal rope, about three meters long, that always keeps you connected to your teammates no matter what's going on. When teammates are pulled to the sideline to cover the corridor, that rope must pull you into the middle of the T to cover the hole they just left. When your teammates see a short ball and step in to attack, you should also step in at the same time, without looking, without anyone reminding you. The moment that rope goes slack, when you're more than three meters apart, you've opened up a space right in the middle of the field, and a good opponent will find it immediately.

When the delivery person is eating you

Every team will encounter a returner who hits the crosscourt very well, the ball is low and strong, and your netminder cannot touch the racket no matter where he stands. The standard double Formation doesn't have many solutions for this situation. That's when two special formations appear.

I-formation is based on the element of deception. Instead of standing in the usual position, your netminder sits low above the center line, with his head lower than the edge of the net, obscuring the returner's familiar view of the server. Before the ball is thrown, the two people agree on a plan with a secret signal behind the person on the net, for example, a pointing finger means "I will move to the deuce side", holding hands means "I will go to the ad side", and spreading the hand means "I will pretend, stand still". As soon as the server touches the ball, the netminder jumps out of that low sitting position in the correct direction, blocking the return path high above the net to finish the point before the server can understand what just happened.

The value here lies almost entirely in psychological factors. You don't win the point by having a better shot, you win because you take away the returner's familiar visual landmark, forcing them to guess. A server who is used to seeing two fixed dots on the court at a time suddenly no longer knows where the two of you will stand, and having to guess under pressure often leads to missed shots.

Formation Australian works differently. Here, the server and netminder stand on the same side of the court instead of facing each other. This completely blocks the returner's preferred crosscourt, forcing them to play down the line, over the highest part of the net, into the narrowest part of the court, right where you've already defended in the hallway. If your opponent's forehand crosscourt return is beating you, formation Australian is often the neatest fix without changing a single shot.

Poach properly: move forward, not sideways

Most movement players when trying to poach fail for the same reason, they run sideways, parallel to the net, chasing the ball's flight. That feeling seems natural but is completely opposite. Running sideways causes you to stay the same distance from the net throughout the movement, meaning you block the ball late and low, often below the edge of the net, where you have neither angle nor power.

Good bouncers do it differently: they cut forward, at a diagonal angle of about 45 degrees toward the net rope, just as the server touches the ball. Just that one change, going diagonally forward instead of drifting sideways, does two things. First, it shortens the distance to the ball much faster, because now you are attacking the ball's flight instead of chasing it. Second, and more importantly, it allows you to touch the ball much higher, usually a foot above the edge of the net, near the top of the ball's trajectory, instead of having to struggle with the ball at shin level. From that high position, you're no longer blocking a volley to keep the ball in court, you're smashing the ball at the feet of the opponent's netminder, and there's almost nothing they can do. If you only remember one thing about poach, remember this: moving forward always beats moving sideways.

Handling the middle of the field: whose ball is it?

Ask any couple what causes them to lose the most free points, and most end up admitting the same thing, a ball hurtles down the middle of the court, they both look at each other, they both hesitate, and the ball drops without anyone touching it. This is not a technical problem. This is a communication error, and can be completely fixed with a single rule.

Whoever has the forehand facing the middle of the field receives every ball that goes down the middle. End. Don't argue, don't hesitate, don't politely yield to teammates. If your forehand is pointing toward the middle string, that's your ball, and you should say "mine" loud, early, and often, so there's never a moment of doubt. This is why left-handed and right-handed couples, when both keep their forehands facing the middle, as the Bryan brothers did for many years, create a wall that is extremely difficult to penetrate. There are no loopholes for opponents to exploit, as there is never any confusion as to who is getting the ball.

Besides, good teams often stand slightly askew instead of forming a horizontal line. The person on the crosscourt side takes a step back, and the person on the side near the touchline presses closer to the net. That slight V shape naturally funnels the ball towards your most dangerous player at the net, rather than leaving both players equally exposed.

Common doubles mistakes and how to fix them

Constantly getting hit over the head is often due to standing right next to the edge of the net, knees locked, and not having time to react. Correct it by keeping a distance of about 2.5 to 3 meters from the net instead of following closely, always ready to turn around to follow the smash ball. The ball is dead in the middle of the field without anyone catching it because there is no unified rule about who receives the ball in the middle, corrected by the middle forehand rule, and shouting loudly and early. Poach repeatedly missed the net by running horizontally and blocking the ball in a low position, below the edge of the net, corrected by cutting diagonally forward and touching the ball at the highest possible point.

Then there is a mistake that few people talk about but it poisons the whole team: the body language of teammates is dragging the whole team down. Rolling your eyes and sighing after every missed volley silently poisons the team relationship. Develop a simple ritual, high-five or racquet after each point, win or lose, and adhere to it absolutely. A brief touch of the racket after a losing point is more valuable than any words of encouragement spoken out loud.

End of set: 10-point tie-break series

When the third set was decided by a 10-point tie-break, much of the match's tactical sophistication was compressed into just a few shots, and small advantages became extremely important. The biggest leverage is the early mini-break. If you win your first return point and then hold both of your serve points, you've built a 3-0 advantage, and teams that get to 3-0 in a 10-point tie-break usually win in the end about three out of four times. That means your focus in the opening points shouldn't be on showmanship, but on discipline: keep a high first-serve-to-court ratio, avoid double faults, and aim for your T rather than risk hitting the line when you're not necessarily in good form that day.

Proactively implementing formation early is also worthwhile. Using I-formation in the first two return points, before the opponent can adjust, often gives better results than saving the trick for later. And when the tie-break series tightens at 8-8 or 9-8, this is when passive teams start playing to not lose, waiting for their opponents to make mistakes instead of creating their own opportunities. Championship teams do the opposite. They call poach, they net play, and they take the points themselves instead of hoping their opponents give them to them. Doubles rewards courage more than any other part of tennis. Learn to trust your teammates, work together net play, and receive the ball in midfield without hesitation, and you'll win more in these tense moments.
