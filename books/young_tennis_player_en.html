Section 1

The Young Tennis Player presents a simple and successful way to learn and enjoy the sport of tennis. Questions asked by young people from around the world inspired the authors to write this book, which explains a simple

method of learning sound fundamentals of the game. These basics can be understood quickly and students can soon enjoy playing tennis. Players can build from a solid foundation to play at any desired level.

John Newcombe, as a three time winner of Wimbledon, and Clarence Mabry, a world famous coach, have worked together to produce a simple

arid complete system for learning tennis — a system that includes technique, practice and competition for all levels of play. The system is based on the player understanding what level he has reached and how to advance to the

next level. Technique is taught by improving the ability to judge the ball, control the racket and to move accurately about the court. Tactics and strategy are functions of analyzing the zones of the court, playing conditions, the styles of your opponent and, of course, your style of play: All practice plans simulate playing conditions so there is no drudgery in practice

— just fun and improvement.

Each chapter illustrates John Newcombe hitting the shot under discussion. His shots are analyzed by Clarence Mabry with an emphasis on

the elements that are required for all good shots. The pictures, which show

the proper techniques, are followed by some common faults, their causes and corrections. This information will allow you to correct your mistakes.

After you learn to play, the refinement chapters are designed to improve your game which is based on a fundamental system. Understanding spins is

introduced with methods to add these shots to your game. You can learn how to hit all spins and when to use each type.

Physical conditioning starts with your first appearance on the courts and continues through special purpose exercises for the professional athlete.

Diet suggestions, pre-match meals and timing are explained in a special section of the book.

Tennis practice sessions should be concentrated, but fun. About one half of the practice time should be spent on conditioning, drills and simulated playing situations, and the other half used for playing.

The Young Tennis Player is also a reference book. Tips on tournament schedules, playing procedures, selection of coaches, and college scholarships will help the reader enjoy the benefits of tennis in all parts of the world.

Players who are really interested in competitive tennis can find the secret

to a world champion’s success. A dedication to physical conditioning, prac-

tice and becoming a better student of the game (learning when you lose) is a part of competitive development.

It is worth making a special effort to give your son or daughter an opportunity to learn the game of tennis at an early age. Tennis will build a

healthier body and mind while demanding self-discipline and offer a lifetime of pleasure. Your child will probably play tennis for social reasons, or as conditioning for other sports. They might enjoy competitive tennis which

has a future leading to a college education or professional tennis. Whatever

the reason might be, it is certain your child will benefit from the experience. All children can benefit from understanding the game and its traditions.

When you take your children to the courts, explain the simple rules of etiquette that are recognized universally. Good manners are just as

important as good play. You will probably notice some club or court rules that need an explanation to enable your child to use the courts when the

family is not available. Most facilities have a pro shop area or receptionist to register for court use.

Watch the players get on and off the courts. Explain the reasons for hurrying between points and games to ‘cross over’ a neighbour’s court. It is usually best to cross courts at the fence line but this will change according to

convenience. Common sense is the rule. Most important, move as rapidly

and as inconspicuously as possible.

Now notice the players playing. If a ball strays on to the next court, players wait for their neighbours to complete the point and return the ball. The ball is returned on one bounce. ‘Thank you’ is the only response. When

the ball rolls on to your court, it is proper to stop play and return the ball.

Players play a ‘let’, i.e. play the point again when interrupted.

You are probably not certain about the dimensions and proper terminology of the court. To refresh your memory, the playing surface from fence to fence is 120 feet long and 60 feet wide. A singles court is 78 feet long and 27 feet in width. Doubles is played with two alleys on either side of the singles court. Each alley is 4'/ feet wide. The doubles court then covers

an area of 78 feet by 36 feet. A net is placed in the centre and is 31 feet high

at the net posts and only 3 feet in the middle. The middle is lowered by using a band called the centre strap.

Other lines that need explanation are the service lines. A centre service line is located down the middle of the court and extends 21 feet from the net

on both sides. A service line, located 21 feet from the net, is parallel to the

net. The service line goes across the singles court and forms the service courts or boxes. These service boxes are called the left court and right court,

or sometimes referred to as the ‘deuce’ (right court) or ‘ads’ (left court). The

same service areas are used for both singles and doubles.

To play a match the players attempt to win points as follows: A server puts the ball in play by standing behind his baseline and on the right side of

the court. The serve is hit over the net into the service box diagonally

opposite the server. The server has two chances to get the ball in the service box. If the first is good, the point has started. If it fails to land in the proper box, the server gets a second chance. If both serves miss, it is a point for the opponent. This is called a double fault.

Other points are won against a player who hits the ball out-of-bounds; that is, outside the singles court if playing singles, or the doubles court if playing with a partner, or through his failure to hit the ball first bounce. It is

permissible to hit the ball full toss or after one bounce only.

You might save some arguments if you explain that a ball landing on the line is in play, or ‘good.’ Tennis players ‘call’ (i.e. state whether the ball was

‘in’ or ‘out’) all balls on their side of the court so they must be fair.

The server alternates right and left side after each point until he wins or

loses his serve. A simple scoring system for games is used whereby the winner is the first player to win four points. The traditional method gives the player 15 for the first point, 30 for the second, 40 for the third and game for

the fourth unless the score is 40 each, which is called a deuce. Then one player must win two consecutive points to win the game. The point after deuce is called ‘advantage’. If the server wins an advantage point it is known

as ‘van in’ (advantage in). If the receiver wins one it is ‘van out’ (advantage out). They will catch on to the scoring quickly once they participate. Naturally, the server serves for a complete game and then the receiver becomes the server. They continue to alternate serving games until one

player wins six games with a two-game lead. If the score reaches five-all they continue until a two-game lead has been won by a player. This group of six

games is called a set. A tie breaker is played if the players reach six or eight

games all. The player due to serve next serves the first point and the players then serve two consecutive points in turn. The game — and set — is won by the first player to score seven points: but if the score reaches six points each,

one player must gain a two-point lead to win. Numerical scoring (rather than any extended use of the 15, 30, 40 system) is used throughout the tiebreak. A set will take thirty to forty minutes to play and a match is usually

the best of three sets. Of course, the official rules can be found in the Rules

Handbook or Yearbook supplied by local tennis associations.

Now you and your child are probably getting restless and ready to hit

some tennis balls. Remember that these rules of etiquette and rules of the

game are the same all over the world. It is your responsibility as a parent to insist on good sportsmanship and fair play.

The final tip for parents is to make certain that your children learn to play the game correctly and get sound tuition. After they learn to play and have fun on the tennis court, you should always encourage, but not push for achievement.

Enjoying tennis involves hitting the ball correctly. First you learn to get the racket on the ball and then to get the ball over the net. It is like learning to ride a bicycle — once you learn the basics, you will never forget them. If you

take a little time at the start to learn properly, improvement becomes a simple process. All you have to know is what stage of development you have

reached.

Selecting a racket depends on the player’s size and strength. To expect a small child to wield a full-size racket is not only unrealistic, it would hamper

his ability and confidence. An older child, however, may not need a junior-

size racket. I would strongly advise buying a wood frame for novice players; wood affords greater control than metal frames. Also, a racket strung with

nylon is less expensive than one strung with gut, yet is perfectly satisfactory for beginners.

You should also learn the names of the parts of the racket. The handle is

the portion which you grip, or hold. Further up is the throat. Above that are

the strings (the face); the frame around them is the head.

You hit the ball out of bounds You may be hitting the ball too late, or else you’re hitting under the ball. Hit

it earlier, making sure that your swing is level and your grip is keeping the racket face perpendicular to the ground.

You hit the ball into the net You may be hitting the ball too early. Remember that to return the ball deep (i.e. far into) your opponent’s court requires you to hit it between 3 and 5

feet over the net, and you should be striving for depth at this early stage.

Another reason why your shot may be hitting the net is that you are swinging down on the ball. Keep that swing level and check to see that the racket face is perpendicular to the ground.

Many people fail to realize that just aiming a shot is a method of control. If your forehand shots consistently hit the net, try aiming higher, just as you should aim lower if your shots consistently go out.

You miss forehand shots Swinging at and missing the bail is a consequence of improper judgement. The ball may be bouncing too close to your body or too far away. That leads

to rushing your shots, and the jerky mistimed swing is the cause of your

missing the ball. Bear in mind that the ‘hitting zone’ is between your waist

and your forward foot as you are standing sideways to the net.

Your forehand lacks power Check your grip. Unless you are holding the racket firmly, your shots cannot be expected to have any force.

Make sure that you are transferring your weight correctly. In addition to hitting the ball out or into the net, improper weight transfer will deprive your forehand of power. A correct follow-through is especially important: your weight should follow your racket until the ball clears the net, so make sure that your follow-through is not rushed.

The zones of the court.

Drills and practice-sessions must be fun and productive. Ingredients for proper practice include imagination, enthusiasm and a knowledge of tennis.

A knowledge of the court itself is important. Tennis shots are produced in three zones of the court, and the technique of hitting varies in each location. The deep or backcourt area is labelled the manoeuvring zone. Few points are won outright from this area within 12 feet of the baseline because the smart player plays to manoeuvre his opponent out of position with safe shots.

Practise consistency and direction, and try to clear the net from 3 to 5 feet by hitting ‘up and through’ the ball from the manoeuvring zone. Shots hit from this area are forehands and backhands, cross-court (diagonally), down the line (straight in front of you), lobs, serves, and returns of serve.

When an opponent is manoeuvred out of position, the player should expect or anticipate that the return will be played from mid-court. Midcourt is the second area, labelled the approach zone. This 15 feet area is called the approach zone because you should plan to approach the net from

it. Remember that only one shot should be played in this zone as you move forward to the net position. Top players are constantly improving their abilities in the approach zone because it controls their ability to attack confidently and with safety. Shots played from this zone include the

approach groundstroke, approach volley, overhead, drop-shot, and half

volley.

The 12 feet area closest to the net is the put-away zone. You should win most of your points in this zone, especially if you have done your job in the two previous zones and earned your way to the net. Practise quick footwork,

a strong grip, and attacking the ball. Shots that are hit before the bounce

include deep volleys (hit into the opponent’s manoeuvring zone), angle volleys (into the approach zone), drop volleys (into the put-away zone) and

easy overheads (hit anywhere).

Proper practice is to drill in each area separately and then put together shot sequences that require moving through the three zones as they are

played in points. When playing the zones of the court, you should play a ball in all three zones to get a winning pattern.

Imagination and a wall can be your best practice partner. The biggest hindrance to improvement is the excuse of not having a practice partner, so don’t let that hold you back. Working against a wall will offer repetition. It will improve your ability to keep the ball in play while grooving the rhythm

of your shots. Caution: do not rush your timing when working on this phase of your game. Listen to the three sounds of the shots — the ball hitting the wall, the ground and the racket — and strive to make these sounds as uniform as possible.

Serving games against the wall is one of the best means of practising your serving accuracy. Serve at targets on the wall and play games against the wall, giving extra credit for hitting the designated target.

Shot combinations and points can be played against a wall. Serves, groundstrokes, and even volleys and overheads, can be used in typical playing combinations.

All players strive to become all-court players. These all-court players can play the ball comfortably in all three zones of the court and hit the ball into all three zones of the opponent’s court. Great players find that offence, defence and patience are all required to be an all-court player. Confidence in this style of game is the foundation of the great champions.

The game of singles always reminds me of two gladiators facing each other. Most times after completing a shot you will return as quickly as possible to behind the baseline at the centre.

The service starts off a point, so make sure you take time to prepare. Too many players walk up to the line and serve with no forethought or preparation. Did you ever see a cricket bowler walk up to the wicket and bowl before setting himself?

There are many ways to serve a tennis ball at your opponent: with slice, flat or kick, wide of the forehand or backhand, at the body on both sides, or with a continual change of pace.

You should be attempting to get around seventy per cent of first serves into play. Don’t go for the big first serve all the time as this usually has a low percentage of success. I like the saying that a player is only as good as his second serve. Fortunately I had a very good one. I often see youngsters working on their service but hardly ever on the second serve. As I stated in

the chapter on serve, I worked mainly on my serve until age eleven, and it became my major weapon. Many a time after a victory I blessed the time my coach spent on my serve.

Although the serve is important, nothing can be more discouraging than

to have your opponent constantly returning your serve well. So it makes

sense to work on your service return. There’s the return against the serve and volley player which must be low and firm and then the baseliner who

serves and stays back. This return should be higher over the net to achieve a

deep drive. The deeper the drive the more chance you have of gaining the

initiative. If the serve is short and slow, don’t hesitate to chip it low with a slice to your opponent’s feet, and move to the net position. The idea is to

Manoeuvre your opponent out of position so that he is doing something he doesn’t like. Assess your opponent’s strengths and weaknesses during the

first few games. If he has a weak backhand, then attack it. Don’t just hit everything to the backhand though. By playing wide to the forehand, you

will open up the backhand side.

Once the ball is returned, the fun really starts. Knowing your own strengths, weaknesses and limitations is all important. It’s called percentage tennis or limiting the number of unforced errors. In the 1977 Wimbledon final, Borg beat Connors 6-4 in the fifth set. Borg made sixteen errors to Connors’ seventy. Connors hit more winners but couldn’t catch up that

huge deficit.

There are three ways to move around a tennis court — sideways, up and back, and up and down (by bending your knees).

Study your opponent’s mobility in the same way as you would study his strokes. Nothing is tougher or more frustrating than an opponent who is like

a rock in his baseline play. Baseliners usually run very well from side to side but not well when forced to go forward and back. The short angle or drop-

shot, when used at the right time, can really break up their game. Similarly, the net rusher does not always run as well from side to side; quite often he will run well to one side but a little more awkwardly to the other. This flaw

can usually be picked up by seeing which is the weaker stroke, forehand or backhand. Against the net rusher, you should also use a varied defence to

test his mobility. Drives, dinks (soft touch shots), chips and lobs should be constantly varied.

As you grow up you will gradually get to know more about your own strengths and limitations. At twelve years of age I had a very solid baseline

game, good enough to make me number one in Australia for my age-group. The decision was made by my coach and myself to start expanding my game

to a more aggressive approach. This meant that I now had to become a serve and volley player. Although I still won most tournaments, the change of style brought a few losses that may not have otherwise happened.

Volleys and drives would sometimes keep missing the lines, causing a 6-4, 7-5 loss. Reinforced by my coach’s confidence, I silenced friendly critics by saying, ‘If 1 am ever going to win Wimbledon, then those shots will start to fall in.’

It was at age fifteen that the mixture of baseline and net attack started to work well. Some of the experiences I had up to that age are worth recording

here.

My mother repeated a statement to me around this time that I have always remembered: ‘You should always learn more from a match you have lost.’

From that time on, my habit upon losing has always been to analyze the loss immediately following the match. It’s important that this be an honest evaluation. For example, it’s easy to say: ‘I lost my serve because I doublefaulted twice. If I hadn’t done that I could have won.’ In reality the double faults were probably caused because of the mental pressure put on you by

your opponent’s good returns. Accepting that answer means you have to

admit to yourself that deep down you actually failed.

A perfect example that comes to mind here is my 1969 U.S. Open semifinal clash with Tony Roche. After three hours the score stood at 6-6 in fifth

set. At 30-30 on my serve I double-faulted twice to lose the serve and consequently the match. Tony and I have played many great matches but

this is the only time I can remember double-faulting myself out of the match. On this occasion Tony’s great returns and the pressure of the occasion forced me to over-serve. It was a costly mistake but a valuable

lesson.

Learning how to finish off a match when you’ve built up a winning lead is

a must if you are going to become a champion. At eleven I led a thirteenyear-old 5-0 in the first round of an Under-Fourteen State tournament. It was a one-set match and I lost 7-5. I never figured out how I lost but the

necessity to be able to finish off a match was driven home.

Sixteen years later I almost got caught the same way. This time it was on

the world’s greatest court, Centre Court, Wimbledon. It was the quarter finals against Tom Okker with me leading two sets to one, 5-0 and my serve. Twenty minutes later the score stood 5-5. I realized that my biggest problem

at this stage was mental. The whole momentum of the match had swung in a very short time. By all rights I should have been in the locker-room congratulating myself. With Tom’s serve to come it was essential that I break serve. Taking a few extra seconds to collect my thoughts and get a

clear head, I proceeded to break serve and then close out the match 7-5.

If I had got angry with myself by saying, ‘How could I have let him back in, what an idiot I am,’ or similar thoughts, in all probability I would have lost the match. Instead I forced myself to think ahead and not back. My only backward thoughts were what had been winning for me before and how I could re-apply those tactics. That’s an example of an invaluable experience

to remember.

Perhaps the most dramatic experience I had as a growing tennis player

was at age nineteen when I was selected to play the Davis Cup finals for Australia against the United States. At that time, 1963, the Davis Cup, along with Wimbledon, were the biggest events of the tennis year.

It’s important to note here that it was my first Davis Cup match. My peers twelve months before had been Rod Laver, Roy Emerson and Neale Fraser. Two weeks before the tie my selection on the team was announced. Although I was in the final team of four, I had no real thoughts that I might

be selected for play. Four days before the match Captain Harry Hopman

As ‘Hop’ didn’t say anything else, it was completely to my surprise when my name was announced in the customary draw just twentyfour hours before the first match. Two things helped me get out of a potentially embarrassing situation. First, most captains or coaches under these conditions would be telling you

I found out a lot about myself in this match. The bad start was due to prematch nerves and not settling down quickly enough. I let the occasion carry

me away instead of concentrating solely on my opponent. Most importantly, however, I found out that under extreme pressure I was able to respond and

shrug off the disastrous first two sets.

If you look at the history of all champions you’ll find that they have this quality. Rod Laver did it numerous times in big matches, and at some stage during four of the Wimbledons that he won, Bjorn Borg was down 2-1 sets.

In my Wimbledon final against Smith and in the U.S. Open against Kodes I trailed two sets to one before winning. It all tracks back to that mental ‘zone’

I was talking about.

Everything is done purely on reflex action. The only object really obvious

to you is your opponent. No doubts about what course you are on enter your mind and you seem able to anticipate every play. I rate my best ‘zone’ as the

fifth set in my 1970 Wimbledon final against Ken Rosewall. I led two sets to

one and 3-1 in the fourth when Ken reeled off five straight games, hardly

losing a point. I pulled myself together to run out the fifth set 6-1. Two points remain vividly in my mind. My first service game, trailing love-15, I

aced Ken on a second serve. At 3-1 and 30-40 on his serve I anticipated he would try a first serve at my body on the backhand side. As he threw the ball up, I moved to the left to hit a forehand winner on return. Running around a

second serve is an accepted tactic, but one never usually tries to anticipate

the direction of a first serve. In both cases I never questioned or doubted what I was doing. It wasn’t pre-planned, just a reflex action.

I can’t offer any easy method here on how to get yourself into this mental ‘zone’. It comes through experience, getting to know yourself and how you

react to pressure.

Don’t expect to be able to stay in this ‘zone’ throughout a whole match. I have rarely seen it last longer than one hour, normally for about thirty minutes. A bad mistake can be made by trying to start off a match playing all out. If you are expecting a tough match, then pace yourself from the beginning. Build yourself and your game into the match. Compare a three

set game to a 3 mile race — if you tried to sprint all the way, you’d have no chance of a strong finish.

Being able to change your tactics and style of play half way through a match can be of great help, no matter how good you are. In 1968 and 1969 when I was ranked in the top four players, I spent hours watching Laver and

Rosewall (I was twenty-five and they thirty-one and thirty-six) play. Rosewall was a little more predictable, but Laver constantly amazed me how

he would get out of a disadvantageous situation by a sudden change of tactics. Rod could not only change his tactics; his standard of play would increase at the same time. It was almost as if he always held a card up his sleeve and knew just the moment to pull it out. He would often start off a match by driving off both sides on return of serve. If his touch was bad that day, then he would revert to chipping the returns and trying to keep the ball in play. When he felt his touch returning, he’d suddenly start attacking the ball again. If the passing shots weren’t working then he’d lob and chip until the touch returned.

The lesson here is that if you are making too many unforced errors by going for your shots, don’t be frightened to just keep the ball in play for a while. Even if you are losing by doing this, your touch will return more

quickly the more balls you hit. Many a time Rod was down two sets, but he

had confidence that his power game would come together, so he wasn’t frightened to wait. Playing im the wind can be one of the most exasperating experiences. The big secret is to play with the wind instead of against it. By that, I mean to use it to your advantage. Lobbing effectively in the wind can

really break up a net attack. When lobbing with the wind behind you, hit high and short, allowing the wind to carry the ball deep. Into the wind, hit lower and harder, trying more for a winning lob. A swirling wind is the

toughest to compete in. The big thing is to realize you are going to make

mistakes because of the wind. Don’t worry about them. Most of all, don’t

show your opponent any bad reactions if you miss.

Not showing your opponent negative reactions is all important in becoming a winner. I love it when my opponent becomes upset at a

linesman or himself. That is the moment to raise your game and apply some additional pressure. Some people today claim that the circuits are so tough that you have to be bad-tempered and outwardly aggressive to win. Don’t believe it. In 1970, in a tournament in Chicago, I beat Nikki Pilic, Fred

Stolle, Andres Gimeno, Ken Rosewall and Arthur Ashe to win the event. The last four had been ranked in the world’s top four players, while Pilic was a French and Wimbledon semi-finalist several times. That example was similar to the strength of our tournaments then. None of the guys swore at

his opponents or yelled obscenities at linesmen. Yet I can assure you that all were every bit as tough to beat as today’s stars.

Nothing can be more puzzling to your opponent than your smiling when you are obviously under pressure. I found if I couldn’t force out a smile I was

getting too tense. Sometimes it is tough to smile because there’s nothing funny happening and the situation is getting more and more tense. Look at your opponent and imagine he is a cow, a clown, or something that tickles your fancy. A quick smile, a deep breath and you’ll be back in business.

Surfaces A word here about different types of surfaces and how to play on them. There

are three basic types — hard, clay and grass. The U.S. Open is now played

on hard court. The composition is generally an asphalt or bitumen base covered with three layers of an acrylic paint, usually coloured green or blue.

The bounce here is medium to fast, depending on how long the court has

been laid. The texture of a newly laid surface will be rougher, causing the ball to pop slightly and bounce higher than it will after the surface smoothes

off with use. As the court becomes smoother, so the bounce will be faster and lower. If this is happening, your swing preparation will need to be quicker; a shorter backswing and bending the knees becomes very

important.

Clay courts are the slowest, so you have time for a longer and fuller swing. With the slower bounce, there is more time to put spin on the ball, and subsequently many of the best clay-court players use a lot of topspin.

Grass tends to be the fastest surface. The bounce is lower and the ball tends to skid off the grass, especially when hit with slice. Topspin artists find grass tough because they don’t have the time to hit their long looping strokes; low bouncing balls make a good knee bend essential, and a long backswing on your preparation will lead to all sorts of trouble. (Talking about altering your backswing for the surface may sound odd, but it is something you should learn and practise as your game develops.)

Putting spin on the ball is something that every young player should

practise. That doesn’t mean that you have to become a topspin artist; the more complete player is one who can hit flat, topspin, or slice off both

forehand and backhand sides.

In a rally with someone who is using excessive spin, remember that the answer to topspin is slice and the answer to slice is topspin. This will keep

the ball on the same rotation. It doesn’t mean, however, that you have to stick to this rule. Apply it if you are in doubt, caught on the wrong foot, or

against any shot that has you in trouble.

The best way to improve your singles game is to play with better players. Then, after the match, analyze why you were beaten. It will probably be

because of your control of the ball. The better player is able to keep much

greater depth on his shots, which allows him to control the play and put and keep his opponent on the defensive.

The one-on-one situation in singles can make for a lot of fun. Don’t fight yourself and never be afraid to lose. Remember, you’ll become a better

player faster by learning new tactics as well as new strokes.”

The art of doubles is knowing where to stand, where to move, and how to

hit a few basic shots.

The serve in doubles must be placed deep into the court and at about three-quarters pace to ensure a good first service. Most of the serves will be

hit to your opponent’s backhand and the rule is to get at least eighty per cent of your first serves in play so your opponents must return them defensively.

Returning the serve is also very basic. The easiest return is back in the

same direction as the server or cross-court. You shouldn’t have trouble returning serve if you plan to keep the ball in play, as opposed to going for

the ‘winning’ shot.

The next shot is the key to winning constantly. Your first volley or the second shot hit by the serving team should be placed between the opponents

or down the middle — a deep shot if one or both are at the baseline or a Jow

soft shot if both are at the net. A second shot will allow your team to establish a strong position at net.

Even though you have learned the fundamentals of tennis technique and strategy, you can never expect to become a really good player without the

help of a qualified instructor or coach. That holds true at all levels; touring professionals have their coaches or advisers who can objectively analyse their games and correct any shortcomings.

Finding a first coach should be attempted in a logical and realistic fashion, and the following five steps are the way to do it.

1. Shop around the clubs, recreation centres and schools. Locate a coach who has large beginner groups, a novice tournament schedule, and an outing

for the entire group about four times each year.

2. Notice the atmosphere of happy coaches and students. All students should be encouraged to practise and to receive frequent recognition for achievement.

3. Look for punctual and organized groups and a teaching-method that is simple, flexible and active. 4. Your first coach should emphasize perfection on the fundamentals of the serve, consistency on groundstrokes, a conditioning routine and, naturally, plenty of laughs. More advanced coaches or professionals must have a proven record. The new coach must have playing experience, coaching experience and a capacity to refine your game and style of play. The coach will be your

trainer, administrator and, most important, close friend. Many decisions in your life will be made on and around the tennis court and your coach will

give you needed confidence.

A parent’s role in the development of the young tennis player is always difficult. Both Clarence and I were fortunate to have the perfect tennis parents. They always encouraged us but never pushed. Their personal and financial sacrifices were made without strings attached. Our coaches were carefully selected and they were allowed to work with a minimum of interference.

Once you get on the professional circuit you will have almost enough knowledge and experience to act as your own coach. An occasional trip back

to the old coach will help to regain confidence and to keep you on the proper trail since rough edges will appear when you enter competition. Some of the players that require a cheerleader or valet as a touring coach are just looking

for an escape if they have a bad year. Tennis is an individual sport for the players to enjoy and top players are capable of making their own decisions.
