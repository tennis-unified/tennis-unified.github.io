Listen and learn is all I can say!

This is a great opportunity to see the future of tennis.

This book is a study of recommended tennis practice using the Science of

Biomechanics. It developed over many years of practical study and coaching.

Biomechanics is not a robotic science but a scientific way of examining natural movement and correcting it towards a particular purpose, such as good tennis practice. The study also provides corrective action, so as to avoid injury.

The purpose of this book is to provide coaches and players with technical information which is usually too scientific for coaches and athletes, in sports science publications. Conversely, it is not unusual to hear that many coaches and players find traditional coaching books quite lame. I hope you find the book informative, thought provoking, relatively easy to understand and user friendly on the tennis court.

This is the first volume in a series of books on various sports. Other volumes in the series are currently in publication and will be available soon. There will also be a volume on work posture and injuries.

Always remember that no coaching at all is far better than bad coaching. My :

study invariably shows that the best actions of the championship players are those that have developed naturally, without formal coaching.

First, study the section that deals directly with the basic movement principles.

Much of this section uses Pat Cash and Andre Agassi as models and case studies.

Following this, case studies of various players re-emphasise the good and notso-good practices and outline the potential for injury and restrictive movement.

There will be later editions, where I will include analyses of some emerging players, among others. Please email any suggestions for players you would like included in later volumes. (Any pictures and videos that you would be happy to part with would be gratefully received into my collection.)

The Appendix, which determines the development of models, introduces a little mathematics. If mathematics is not your forte, then skim through this section. You will still pick up the sense of the principles of movement from the case studies.

Biomechanically, the forehand is the easiest to begin with, so the forehand is described extensively and the other tennis shots are described with fewer examples. Otherwise I would be repeating myself, especially with regard to the principles. Therefore, in many ways, the other tennis shots are described as being variations on the forehand shot. The principles are fundamentally the same throughout all shots in tennis and all actions in other sports.

The position of the feet defines the relationship between the player and the field of play. When the body is too ‘front-on’ to the target, it makes contact easier and slightly compromises the preparation. If you are too side-on, then you compromise the contact and favour the preparation. If you had to choose between being too side-on or too front-on, being too front-on is the safer side of perfect. If you tried to wash the dishes or shake someone’s hand while standing very side-on, you would feel fairly silly and ineffective. I have no idea where the ‘side-on’ idea came from but I suspect that somebody used it in a textbook, and others just copied.

Even the great players’ forehands (pre -1970) were all front-on: Bill Tilden,

Bobby Riggs, Althea Gibson, Margaret Court, Billie Jean King, Rod Laver,

Tony Roche and Evonne Goolagong Cawley. (Rod Laver had the most complete array of all shots. He was the most natural player, technically, in the game, ever.) I also would include Tony Roche, Jim Courier, Pete Sampras, Ivan Lendl,

Bjorn Borg and Marat Safin in this elite group of forehands. In more recent decades, Agassi produced one of the best forehands that we have ever seen. His position is fantastic.

You can find a suitable position if you set up some targets and rally forehands with your friend. If your position is wrong, your direction will be erratic. If you find the good position then your forehand direction will be approximately in line with the target.

In the photograph below, you can see how Margaret Court had a fantastic, natural, foot position. It would certainly be expected that she would have the left hand too low, because in those days, players carried the second ball in their other hand.

The backhand position is slightly more side-on than the forehand, because of the difference in the backswings. The feet need to accommodate the fact that the hitting arm needs to come across the body. The double-handed backhand requires a more front-on position than the single-handed backhand, as it is more similar to a forehand.

The position on the forehand volley, smash and serve follow the same angles as the forehand. The backhand and backhand volley follow the same angles, and the backhand slice and forehand slice are, effectively, backhand volleys and forehand volleys, respectively. The smash is effectively a serve.

The balance principle describes the relationship between the body and the height of the target. The balance should be perpendicular to the intended height of the action.

Using your targets in practice, you can find your ideal balance by observing whether you are short or long of your target. Pat and Andre Agassi’s balance is superb. They do not hit with excessive topspin and/or high over the net, so they only need to be leaning back slightly, as you can see in the photos below.

The balance on the serve is ‘down’ because you are driving the ball down into the court. The same balance is needed for the slice shots and volleys, and because the slice lifts the ball the downward action is needed. Very high volleys are more like serves and they are hit flatter. Unfortunately one is often forced into a backward balance, on smashes, and more downward action is required by the upper body.

Homeostasis is the ability of a living organism’s system to adjust its internal environment, in order to maintain a stable equilibrium of balance, shape, physiology, temperature and economy of movement dynamics. This is the body’s protective instinct. The words that come to my mind when thinking about homeostasis are...tidy, efficient, minimal, secure and humble.

Michael Jackson’s clever dance style used a minimum amount of space. This had the effect of being very illustrative, focused and concentrated — and all with so little effort.

The ‘shaking hands’ shape of the waist-high forehand is the most biomechanically sound shot in tennis. When technique is natural it appears to be almost invisible. There is effortlessness, as if the players are not trying. They often appear to be ‘floating in space’. When there are idiosyncratic flaws, there is high visibility and a view of extra effort, as if the players are ‘over-trying’.

I believe that people with posture, obesity and injury problems are vulnerable to cancer. From a homeostatic point of view, at the micro-cellular level, it would seem feasible for the cells to divide, unnaturally, to push the space into opening out. It then follows that a tumour could appear in this region. The entirety of our human form requires the care for billions of individual cells, which already deal with their own problems. Pushing these cells into situations of stress may cause them to rebel.

There is really no right or wrong way to describe the planes and axis of rotation.

Sometimes in medicine, anatomists use different terms from those used by biomechanists, for example. Some just use the labels X, Y and Z. The point is to be consistent and to know what you are talking about.

For the purpose of this book, rotation around the transverse axis will be referred to as ‘lateral’. Imagine a spear going through your head and down through your body to your feet. The only way that you can rotate around this axis, without the axis itself moving, is around it, like an ice-skater spinning in one spot. Looking at the image below (Fig. 3), the transverse axis is perpendicular to the transverse plane and it is formed by the intersection of the coronal and the sagittal planes.

The sagittal axis is the axis perpendicular to the sagittal plane, i.e. the sagittal axis is formed by the intersection of the coronal and the transverse planes.

Imagine a spear going through your left side and coming out your right side, through your waist. Around this axis, your head can spin to the ground while your feet spin to the sky. Because this is a forward/backward rotation, | prefer to call this ‘longitudinal’ rotation.

The coronal (or frontal) axis is the axis perpendicular to the coronal plane, i.e.

the coronal axis is formed by the intersection of the sagittal and the transverse planes. Imagine a spear through your stomach and back. You can spin left over right and right over left around this axis. And because you remain facing the

‘front’, I like to call this ‘frontal’ rotation.

During the Agassi forehand preparation, the body rotates laterally clockwise

(when viewed from above). During the hitting of the forehand, the body rotates smoothly anti-clockwise. Agassi performs this movement very well, apart from the slight bump in the axis from the ‘knee jerk’ to the right and overuse of the left arm, which would restrict the rotation from operating perfectly, back to contact.

The longitudinal rotation on the forehand should be a ‘falling back’ motion as the ball is lifted over the net with topspin, except for the slice forehand which is rotated down to assist the downward intention of ‘slice’ spin. The flat forehand has only a subtle backward motion as the ball is lifted marginally. Agassi performs the longitudinal rotation to perfection apart from a slight bend in the axis from the forward left knee excess.

The frontal rotation on a right-handed forehand is from left to right. Agassi performs this rotation extremely well, apart from the slight over-downward knee action which moves the axis excessively down-and-up, producing a little bump in the rotation.

Using the X, Y and Z axes to represent the transverse, sagittal and coronal axes respectively, we can give X, Y and Z coordinates to represent height, width and breadth, and we can also develop mathematical formulas for these movements.

We can use any units of measurement from the centre: millimetres, centimetres, metres, inches or feet.

If we represent the central point of the body, behind the ‘belly-button’ or navel, to be zero on all axes, then we may say that at ‘time 1’ (tl), Agassi’s left knee could be -60 units in height (along the X axis), 6 units in width (along the Y axis) and 6 units in breadth (along the Z axis). At ‘time 2’ (t2), the centre of the left knee moves to the coordinates 70: 7: -3 (X/ Y/ Z). As the leg straightens and_ the height from the ‘belly-button’ increases from 60 to 70 in a negative direction

(negative being ‘down’ and positive being ‘up’ from the centre of the body), the knee is going back (-Z) and to the left (+Y). At ‘time 3’ (t3), you may have the coordinates -65: 8: -4.

With some mathematical adjustment, you may develop a formula to describe the knee position at any given point in time as: aT = bX + cY + dZ. With the axes themselves actually moving, we may wish to factor this movement in as well.

With a formula for the model, you can evaluate how far, in each direction, a certain player’s knee is out of position.

Rotation is maximised in all movement skills by sharing the muscle contractions. Strangely enough, ‘more is less’. So if there is not equal share, more is achieved through under-achieving, as the muscles at least have the benefit of interacting, summating, combining and overlapping, like ‘waves of muscle fibres’ flowing together. Under-achieving with the left hand would help the knee to find a more comfortable position in which to rotate freely.

The traditional ‘waiting position’ has some question marks over it. By default, the ‘waiting position’ implies that the left hand, for right-handed forehands, follows the racquet in preparation. You can imagine that shaking somebody’s hand, while keeping the hands together, brings about a ‘cramped’ shape for that action. The left chest area is forced into an area that ‘complements’ the right chest. When you shake hands naturally, you would place your left arm into a balanced and mirror image position. With respect to the forehand, this pressure of the left arm moving to the right, out of a homeostatic design, can force the right side rotation into ‘overdoing it’.

You may also try to run to the ball with your left hand on the racquet; in doing so, you would experience much more difficulty than if you were to run to the ball without placing your left hand on the racquet. Also, in this case, you can allow the rotation of your chest to be governed, more naturally, by your rightarm backswing. The grip can fall easily into the right position without the help of the non-hitting hand. Rod Laver had no real coaching before he was discovered by Australian super coach, Charlie Hollis. Laver had no ‘waiting position’, and consequently he moved well and could not over-rotate. (See the following photos of the left-handed Rod Laver.)

In an ideal world, the muscle fibres overlap in a pushing and pulling wave-like action; a summation of parts. The order of flow should be as follows:

abdominals, upper thorax, upper legs; buttocks, upper arms, lower legs, lower arms. You can observe this when you climb a tree. As you go up to the next branch, you can observe which body parts move first. Agassi’s sequence is interfered with slightly at the left leg-knee region.

A stylish dancer will seem to be moving smoothly, as if every body part is connected. A less stylish dancer will appear jerky. Their legs and arms will seem to be making an extra effort, dragging the body around as if disconnected. With the centre of the body being thrown around, they will be off balance.

This can be easily explained by a handshake, which naturally occurs at an arm angle of approximately 135 . This angle occurs through all actions at all body segments in all sports; a potential number for the meaning of life. These angles also occur through the entire animal kingdom. If the arm is outstretched, you can feel the strain in the shoulder deltoid. If the arm is too ‘bent’ then the lower arm is strained. You would also be standing neither too far to the left, nor too far to the right, of your ‘handshakee’.

Body parts should have enough space but also should be sufficiently compact.

Parts are entering into the zone as other parts move out. You should not attempt to put all the body parts into the same area at same time. This phenomenon is very evident in a too ‘side-on’ forehand and all other flawed shots.

Agassi’s head should be starting to leave the zone at contact, but it gets ‘stuck’ in the zone, and makes the contact area slightly too ‘cramped’. Serving to his body, on the forehand, will give his opponents some rewards.

The contact zone on the backhand is more in front and closer to the body, left to right, compared with the forehand. The double-handed backhand is hit more behind than the single-handed stroke, and further away from the body, left to right, but closer than the forehand. The biggest mistakes are to hit the forehand too close and too far in front, and to hit the backhand too far away from the centre of the body.

This principle describes the variations from the ‘flat’ stroke, which in turn describes the intention of the action. This principle has much to do with the angle of the racquet face and the grip being used. Agassi uses a ‘semi-western’ grip which is ideal for ‘rib-high’ shots. Ideally, the player should move to a more ‘western’ grip for higher shots and to a more ‘eastern’ or ‘continental’ grip for lower forehands. Agassi mostly uses just enough ‘topspin’ to help bring the ball down into the court. He utilises the principle that ‘the shortest distance between two points is a straight line’. When applying ‘topspin’, and maintaining a rhythmic circular motion, the racquet face should start off closed, open at contact and then close after contact. Agassi does this to perfection.

Sometimes when professional tennis players need to run for a wide and low forehand, they need to swing up on the outside of the ball in order to lift the ball up over the net with control. The racquet head will finish on the same side of the body as the contact. The spin serve follows exactly the same principle, except that the players are driving the spin action down, not up. These are two similar actions except that they are ‘upside-down’ and ‘back-to front’ images of themselves. Professional players sometimes use a slight forehand grip on the ;

serve to produce an ‘inside-out’ serve. This serve is hard to keep down but if it goes in, it has considerable power and slides off the court; this shot is also a close cousin to the high ‘inside-out’ forehand or backhand.

Boris Becker sometimes used this serve to ‘skid’ a powerful serve out wide on the ‘ad’ court. Pat Cash uses it as a surprise tactic. The returner has trouble picking it unless they notice the grip the server is using.

The ‘kick’ serve is a myth. There are only flat and spin serves. Your serve will become annihilated if you endeavour to hit up on the back of the ball with a horizontal racquet. You will never be able to get the ball to hit the court quickly, achieve any angle, or establish any movement through the air or off the bounce.

This table is very general, as there are infinite variations in height and an infinite number of grips, and there are many variations. The table can also be looked at in terms of a clock face, where the shots opposite each other on the clock face are usually opposite in design, yet they are made using a similar grip. Now, if you make your feet become your head, and turn yourself upside-down, you are getting close to a spin serve action. Furthermore, if you make a few modifications to the original shape, you can now run back to the back fence and play a blind trick shot between your legs back into the court. So, using the 12 o’clock and 6 o’clock positions (on the clock face), with a few bends here and there, we can see that the volley between the legs, the serve, and the shot between the legs made while facing the other way are all in the same family of shots. Position: The angle of the feet can also be used to describe the relationship between the player and the court. The Cilic forehand has a better relationship with the sideline to his right than with the court. Hitting cross-court with accuracy and pace will be very difficult.

Balance: There is a conflict between the balance of the lower body and upper body. If the lower body takes over, the ball will go into the net; if the upper body takes over, the ball will go long. Cilic must manage these conflicting balances to achieve a successful height over the net. This means that every time he hits a forehand, he will experience the anxiety associated with uncertainty.

On very important points, when the pressure is on, it would be wise to hit to his forehand. Anxiety adds to the muscle tension of vulnerable areas.

Homeostasis: Homeostasis may be considered in terms of ‘space’. For example, you may stand at a cliff and extend one leg out over the ‘drop’. The leg feels unprotected from the body as it extends away from the body centre, and the body itself feels in danger. Extend this thought to a parking space.

You could say that Cilic’s right hip has parked too far away from the curb and the left hip is too far into the traffic. For the body-segment muscles to feel comfortable, efficient and protected, the space needs to be neither too small, nor too big, while retaining symmetry. We are given an ideal space in which to perform various movements. For example, when you climb a tree, you must use your trunk to move your arms up with it, and then bring your legs into the ‘new’ space.

Axis: All the axes are compromised.

There are statistical mathematical methods to determine the probability of the formula being an accurate assumption. When scientists are estimating the credibility of their experiments, they generally accept that a probability of 90% or more is accurate.

In the formula for John there may be 20 variables, including d (what colour shirt he had on yesterday) and e (how much time was spent eating breakfast). The Growing Legend that is Brad Langevad: Sports Coaching and New

Athletics, swimming and all sporting contests evolve — new methods and techniques replace past orthodoxies and so performance improves dramatically.

Over the last few years sports coaching methods have focused on:

* Elimination of unnecessary movement

* Greater strength and fitness

* Avoidance of costly injuries.

Technology has accelerated this evolution, not only through new equipment, better clothing and improved playing surface design but also in the use of video” and performance software to analyse and study movement. In most sports, coaches and players who want to be at the top of their profession tend to be hungry for knowledge and have embraced these new methods. Those that have done so have succeeded. On the other hand, some coaches and some entire sports fail to move with the times, partly through ignorance and partly because established coaches, clubs and sporting associations that have thrived under these comfortable orthodoxies are reluctant to change. They resist change and fall behind, producing mediocre and injury-prone athletes.

Tennis is Brad’s primary passion and he played the professional tennis circuit in his youth. Despite the best coaching, a couple of his strokes kept letting him down, and this also led to painful injuries. Brad began to question the way tennis was being taught at senior coaching levels and within professional coaching associations, and the way injuries were being managed.

After completing a science degree, Brad began to explore the use of biostatistics, physics and computer technology to examine the movements of the body and apply it to tennis students. It became clear that conflicting body movements restrict a player’s ability to act fluently and caused injury. Golfers, cricketers and other sportspeople all suffered.

To test his theories, over several years Brad analysed videos of hundreds of athletes, from top professionals to everyday sportsmen. Brad found that the top athletes had a great deal in common, whereas the weaker performers varied greatly. Despite the idiosyncrasies that made the successful actions appear different, they varied only marginally. They were all following the same basic biomechanical principles naturally, and often in defiance of their coach’s methods. To complicate the issue, world champions had fine actions and weaker actions. By giving statistical numbers for actions and adding them, Brad could roughly get the world rankings, with slight variations, explained by intelligence, tactics and other aspects. He found, through interviews, that the more successful actions had been less coached and that the ‘Agassi forehand’, for example, had slipped through the ‘instructor’s trap’ and had developed efficiency naturally, whereas the ‘weaker Agassi serve’ had been engulfed by the instructor’s dogma.

Brad realised that he was not defining actions to justify science; instead, he was using science to explain nature as art.

Brad’s findings were to define the coaching philosophy he has used for the last 25 years. He was biomechanically defining each perfect action; even basic walking, sitting and sleeping. Computer models were developed to compare clients. Brad was helping office workers, drivers, labourers and hairdressers.

STEP IN AND TAKE THE BALLON - THE RISE

When you step in as you hit the ball, you are combining two actions. Moving up to a short ball involves the same smooth transition as moving sideways or backwards; moving to, hitting and moving away are linked as a smooth chain of events without over-overlapping. Try serving while jumping on a trampoline or hitting a forehand while sprinting forward.

There is actually a point where the ball is neither rising nor falling; the easiest time to hit the ball. Top players find this point often. But the safest alternative to hitting at the perfect point is to hit the ball when it starts to drop. Hitting at the perfect point has the appearance of hitting on the rise, but it is not, and it will destroy your control if you try‘to do this. The reason why a half-volley is an easy shot is because at the point that the ball hits the ground, it is still.

Good players let the ball drop when they can’t hit it at the ideal point. Tennis is not about rushing the opponent; rather, it is about putting the ball into the place most feared, by way of the shortest distance, and that is often behind opponents when they make the mistake of speeding to the open court.

You can hit the ball the length of a football field if you want. Tennis has a confined space and the placement of the ball involves a specific speed, not the maximum speed. Distance and accuracy is more important than velocity.

Furthermore, trying to hit the ball too hard increases the back-swing to breaking-point and the body cannot recover to a symmetrical contact. Don’t confuse tennis with javelin throwing. McEnroe made a career of hitting the ball flat and soft with angles and deep skidders. The drop-shots and drop-volleys he practised made him the world’s best player. Ask Pat Cash!

Hewitt and Agassi both take the pace off the ball when the short angles are on offer. Take a leaf out of Sampras’ book of shots; the low flat ball hit more directly to the target and low over the net is more effective than belting a ball high with massive amounts of topspin to keep it in, unless you are trying to bounce the ball over an 8 year old. Generally speaking, the higher ranked players hit the ball softer and more directly. More brain than brawn is needed.

Tennis is no different, in terms of forehands and backhands and other strokes. Players will have less difficulty with changing a forehand that is unreliable and causing injury than stopping themselves from hitting down the line instead of going crosscourt, or wearing a blue shirt instead of a white one.
