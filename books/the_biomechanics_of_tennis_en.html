This book is a study of recommended tennis practice using the Science of

Biomechanics. It developed over many years of practical study and coaching.

Biomechanics is not a robotic science but a scientific way of examining natural

movement and correcting it towards a particular purpose, such as good tennis

practice. The study also provides corrective action, so as to avoid injury.

The purpose of this book is to provide coaches and players with technical

information which is usually too scientific for coaches and athletes, in sports

science publications. Conversely, it is not unusual to hear that many coaches

and players find traditional coaching books quite lame. I hope you find the book

informative, thought provoking, relatively easy to understand and user friendly on

the tennis court.

This is the first volume in a series of books on various sports. Other volumes in

the series are currently in publication and will be available soon. There will also

be a volume on work posture and injuries.

Biomechanically, the forehand is the easiest to begin with, so the forehand

is described extensively and the other tennis shots are described with fewer

examples. Otherwise I would be repeating myself, especially with regard to

the principles. Therefore, in many ways, the other tennis shots are described

as being variations on the forehand shot. The principles are fundamentally the

same throughout all shots in tennis and all actions in other sports.

The position of the feet defines the relationship between the player and the

field of play. When the body is too ‘front-on’ to the target, it makes contact

easier and slightly compromises the preparation. If you are too side-on, then

you compromise the contact and favour the preparation. If you had to choose

between being too side-on or too front-on, being too front-on is the safer side of

perfect. If you tried to wash the dishes or shake someone’s hand while standing

very side-on, you would feel fairly silly and ineffective. I have no idea where

the ‘side-on’ idea came from but I suspect that somebody used it in a textbook,

and others just copied.

Even the great players’ forehands (pre -1970) were all front-on: Bill Tilden,

Bobby Riggs, Althea Gibson, Margaret Court, Billie Jean King, Rod Laver,

Tony Roche and Evonne Goolagong Cawley. (Rod Laver had the most complete

array of all shots. He was the most natural player, technically, in the game,

ever.) I also would include Tony Roche, Jim Courier, Pete Sampras, Ivan Lendl,

Bjorn Borg and Marat Safin in this elite group of forehands. In more recent

decades, Agassi produced one of the best forehands that we have ever seen. His position is fantastic.

You can find a suitable position if you set up some targets and rally forehands

with your friend. If your position is wrong, your direction will be erratic. If you

find the good position then your forehand direction will be approximately in

line with the target.

In the photograph below, you can see how Margaret Court had a fantastic,

The backhand position is slightly more side-on than the forehand, because of

the difference in the backswings. The feet need to accommodate the fact that

the hitting arm needs to come across the body. The double-handed backhand

requires a more front-on position than the single-handed backhand, as it is more

similar to a forehand.

The position on the forehand volley, smash and serve follow the same angles as

the forehand. The backhand and backhand volley follow the same angles, and

the backhand slice and forehand slice are, effectively, backhand volleys and

forehand volleys, respectively. The smash is effectively a serve.

The balance principle describes the relationship between the body and the

height of the target. The balance should be perpendicular to the intended height

of the action.

Using your targets in practice, you can find your ideal balance by observing

whether you are short or long of your target. Pat and Andre Agassi’s balance is

superb. They do not hit with excessive topspin and/or high over the net, so they

only need to be leaning back slightly, as you can see in the photos below.

The balance on the serve is ‘down’ because you are driving the ball down

into the court. The same balance is needed for the slice shots and volleys, and

because the slice lifts the ball the downward action is needed. Very high volleys

are more like serves and they are hit flatter. Unfortunately one is often forced

into a backward balance, on smashes, and more downward action is required by

the upper body.

Homeostasis is the ability of a living organism’s system to adjust its internal

environment, in order to maintain a stable equilibrium of balance, shape,

physiology, temperature and economy of movement dynamics. This is the

body’s protective instinct. The words that come to my mind when thinking

about homeostasis are...tidy, efficient, minimal, secure and humble.

Michael Jackson’s clever dance style used a minimum amount of space. This

had the effect of being very illustrative, focused and concentrated — and all with

so little effort.

The ‘shaking hands’ shape of the waist-high forehand is the most

biomechanically sound shot in tennis. When technique is natural it appears to

be almost invisible. There is effortlessness, as if the players are not trying. They

often appear to be ‘floating in space’. When there are idiosyncratic flaws, there

is high visibility and a view of extra effort, as if the players are ‘over-trying’.

There is really no right or wrong way to describe the planes and axis of rotation.

Sometimes in medicine, anatomists use different terms from those used by

biomechanists, for example. Some just use the labels X, Y and Z. The point is to

be consistent and to know what you are talking about.

For the purpose of this book, rotation around the transverse axis will be referred

to as ‘lateral’. Imagine a spear going through your head and down through your

body to your feet. The only way that you can rotate around this axis, without the

axis itself moving, is around it, like an ice-skater spinning in one spot. Looking

at the image below (Fig. 3), the transverse axis is perpendicular to the transverse

plane and it is formed by the intersection of the coronal and the sagittal planes.

The sagittal axis is the axis perpendicular to the sagittal plane, i.e. the sagittal

axis is formed by the intersection of the coronal and the transverse planes.

Imagine a spear going through your left side and coming out your right side,

through your waist. Around this axis, your head can spin to the ground while

your feet spin to the sky. Because this is a forward/backward rotation, | prefer to

call this ‘longitudinal’ rotation.

The coronal (or frontal) axis is the axis perpendicular to the coronal plane, i.e.

the coronal axis is formed by the intersection of the sagittal and the transverse

planes. Imagine a spear through your stomach and back. You can spin left over

right and right over left around this axis. And because you remain facing the

‘front’, I like to call this ‘frontal’ rotation.

During the Agassi forehand preparation, the body rotates laterally clockwise

(when viewed from above). During the hitting of the forehand, the body rotates

smoothly anti-clockwise. Agassi performs this movement very well, apart from

the slight bump in the axis from the ‘knee jerk’ to the right and overuse of the

left arm, which would restrict the rotation from operating perfectly, back to

contact.

The longitudinal rotation on the forehand should be a ‘falling back’ motion as

the ball is lifted over the net with topspin, except for the slice forehand which is

rotated down to assist the downward intention of ‘slice’ spin. The flat forehand

has only a subtle backward motion as the ball is lifted marginally. Agassi

performs the longitudinal rotation to perfection apart from a slight bend in the

axis from the forward left knee excess.

The frontal rotation on a right-handed forehand is from left to right. Agassi

performs this rotation extremely well, apart from the slight over-downward

knee action which moves the axis excessively down-and-up, producing a little

bump in the rotation.

Using the X, Y and Z axes to represent the transverse, sagittal and coronal axes

respectively, we can give X, Y and Z coordinates to represent height, width and

breadth, and we can also develop mathematical formulas for these movements.

We can use any units of measurement from the centre: millimetres, centimetres,

metres, inches or feet.

If we represent the central point of the body, behind the ‘belly-button’ or navel,

to be zero on all axes, then we may say that at ‘time 1’ (tl), Agassi’s left knee

could be -60 units in height (along the X axis), 6 units in width (along the Y

axis) and 6 units in breadth (along the Z axis). At ‘time 2’ (t2), the centre of the

left knee moves to the coordinates 70: 7: -3 (X/ Y/ Z). As the leg straightens and_

the height from the ‘belly-button’ increases from 60 to 70 in a negative direction

(negative being ‘down’ and positive being ‘up’ from the centre of the body), the

knee is going back (-Z) and to the left (+Y). At ‘time 3’ (t3), you may have the

coordinates -65: 8: -4.

With some mathematical adjustment, you may develop a formula to describe the

knee position at any given point in time as: aT = bX + cY + dZ. With the axes

themselves actually moving, we may wish to factor this movement in as well.

With a formula for the model, you can evaluate how far, in each direction, a

certain player’s knee is out of position.

The traditional ‘waiting position’ has some question marks over it. By default,

the ‘waiting position’ implies that the left hand, for right-handed forehands,

follows the racquet in preparation. You can imagine that shaking somebody’s

hand, while keeping the hands together, brings about a ‘cramped’ shape for that

action. The left chest area is forced into an area that ‘complements’ the right

chest. When you shake hands naturally, you would place your left arm into a

balanced and mirror image position. With respect to the forehand, this pressure

of the left arm moving to the right, out of a homeostatic design, can force the

right side rotation into ‘overdoing it’.

You may also try to run to the ball with your left hand on the racquet; in doing

so, you would experience much more difficulty than if you were to run to the

ball without placing your left hand on the racquet. Also, in this case, you can

allow the rotation of your chest to be governed, more naturally, by your right-

arm backswing. The grip can fall easily into the right position without the

help of the non-hitting hand. Rod Laver had no real coaching before he was

discovered by Australian super coach, Charlie Hollis. Laver had no ‘waiting

position’, and consequently he moved well and could not over-rotate. (See the

following photos of the left-handed Rod Laver.)

In an ideal world, the muscle fibres overlap in a pushing and pulling wave-

like action; a summation of parts. The order of flow should be as follows:

abdominals, upper thorax, upper legs; buttocks, upper arms, lower legs, lower

arms. You can observe this when you climb a tree. As you go up to the next

A stylish dancer will seem to be moving smoothly, as if every body part is

connected. A less stylish dancer will appear jerky. Their legs and arms will seem

to be making an extra effort, dragging the body around as if disconnected. With

the centre of the body being thrown around, they will be off balance.

This can be easily explained by a handshake, which naturally occurs at an arm

angle of approximately 135 °. This angle occurs through all actions at all body

segments in all sports; a potential number for the meaning of life. These angles

also occur through the entire animal kingdom. If the arm is outstretched, you

can feel the strain in the shoulder deltoid. If the arm is too ‘bent’ then the lower

arm is strained. You would also be standing neither too far to the left, nor too far

to the right, of your ‘handshakee’.

Body parts should have enough space but also should be sufficiently compact.

Parts are entering into the zone as other parts move out. You should not attempt

to put all the body parts into the same area at same time. This phenomenon is

very evident in a too ‘side-on’ forehand and all other flawed shots.

Agassi’s head should be starting to leave the zone at contact, but it gets ‘stuck’

in the zone, and makes the contact area slightly too ‘cramped’. Serving to his

body, on the forehand, will give his opponents some rewards.

The contact zone on the backhand is more in front and closer to the body, left

to right, compared with the forehand. The double-handed backhand is hit more

behind than the single-handed stroke, and further away from the body, left to

right, but closer than the forehand. The biggest mistakes are to hit the forehand

too close and too far in front, and to hit the backhand too far away from the

centre of the body.

Sometimes when professional tennis players need to run for a wide and low

forehand, they need to swing up on the outside of the ball in order to lift the

ball up over the net with control. The racquet head will finish on the same side

of the body as the contact. The spin serve follows exactly the same principle,

except that the players are driving the spin action down, not up. These are two

similar actions except that they are ‘upside-down’ and ‘back-to front’ images of themselves. Professional players sometimes use a slight forehand grip on the ;

serve to produce an ‘inside-out’ serve. This serve is hard to keep down but if

it goes in, it has considerable power and slides off the court; this shot is also a

close cousin to the high ‘inside-out’ forehand or backhand.

Boris Becker sometimes used this serve to ‘skid’ a powerful serve out wide

on the ‘ad’ court. Pat Cash uses it as a surprise tactic. The returner has trouble

picking it unless they notice the grip the server is using.

The table can also be looked at in terms of a clock face, where the shots

opposite each other on the clock face are usually opposite in design, yet they

are made using a similar grip. This is why a player with an ‘eastern’ forehand

volley grip can have success with high forehand volleys and low backhand

You can have some fun with this concept. If you try a forehand volley between _

your head, and turn yourself upside-down, you are getting close to a spin serve

action. Furthermore, if you make a few modifications to the original shape, you

can now run back to the back fence and play a blind trick shot between your

legs back into the court. So, using the 12 o’clock and 6 o’clock positions (on the

hitting at the perfect point is to hit the ball when it starts to drop. Hitting at the

perfect point has the appearance of hitting on the rise, but it is not, and it will

destroy your control if you try‘to do this. The reason why a half-volley is an

easy shot is because at the point that the ball hits the ground, it is still.

most feared, by way of the shortest distance, and that is often behind opponents
