Florence. He found himself living modestly in the countryside with no available tennis court. Quickly focusing his analytical and creative mind on this lack, he created a new version of the game, one needing only a net across a floor with painted lines, which he implemented on a section of

flat lawn in a convenient meadow.

Alas, the nearby villagers and peasants sharing his own middle age were not susceptible to his importunings that they adjust their lifestyles to accommodate tennis. He accordingly assembled and trained a coterie of youthful players. They picked up the game quickly but, being a

generation younger, were more energetic and speedier than Machiavelli and attained greater accuracy and power

in their strokes. Thus it became necessary for Machiavelli to develop the technique of outfoxing his physically more adept opponents and, further, to introduce the game of doubles so as to diminish the court area which he himself

would have to defend.

Machiavelli's remarkable Prince, II covers every aspect of the modern game of tennis—serving, return of serve,

ground stroke placement, volleys, lobs, net play, poaching,

getting along with a partner, and the employment of ruses and covert influences on the play of the opponent, from warm-up through to the calling of close shots. Even the sly

injection of conversation during the game to impair the play of rivals was brilliantly conceived and presented by

the master.

When Machiavelli was exiled from Florence, the belong-

ings he took with him included a few regulation-size rackets. These he generously assigned to his opponents. For himself he fashioned a new racket that, mainly because of its increased size, he knew others would judge crude and clumsy. Machiavelli explained that it had been easier for him to fabricate it than a racket of smaller, conventional dimensions. He referred to his racket as “The Prince,”

using this affectionate nickname in what his playing companions regarded as a good-natured and witty belittling of his racket’s power by mischievously misapplying to it the

title of a powerful head of state. Actually, as he well knew, the larger racket gave him an advantage.

In this translation I have sought a literal conversion to English. Although in places Machiavelli words have been expressed in Americanese for easy clarity, his meaning has been strictly preserved. *

agree at the outset that the size of the tennis court has been chosen, as has the height of the net, so as to

But if the court area is just right for outstanding tennis athletes, then it follows that it is too spacious for players who, by reason of lesser athletic talents and, perhaps, deterioration of abilities with age, are lacking in energy,

fast court coverage, quick body reaction, and powerful

strokes. Tennis players, as they slow down, must smart up.

Doubles, not singles, is then the proper game. Even

though to maximize the pleasures of doubles, narrow alleys have been added to each side of the singles court, a doubles player has less territory to defend. Equally important, one’s doubles partner is expected to assume responsibility for about one-half of all the returns of the opponents offerings. While the partner is thus engaged in holding open the possibility of winning the point, one has the time

to assess the situation, move to what appears the most likely site for responding well to the probable next ball, and even consider where to place the return should the opportunity present itself. Moreover, during the moments when the partner is covering for the side, one can catch

one’s breath, snatch a few seconds of rest, and so manage to

remain vigorous throughout the match.

That doubles is to be preferred for all players except those especially skillful and speedy is made evident when singles play is observed between two mediocre competi-

tors. Owing to their constant need to run with maximum expenditure of available energy to reach useful return

positions, and their usually failing to arrive there until the last possible moment, their strokes are most often executed poorly. They rarely capture enough time to position their

bodies and legs properly and bring their rackets back fully. The ball is accordingly struck in a somewhat haphazard

manner. Assuming it surmounts the net, it may land unpredictably almost anywhere in the opposite court or out of it. In view of the awkwardness of their motions, the

stances and swings of such players as they connect with the balls are seldom worth noting by opponents seeking to

predict where the ball’s likely trajectory will take it. Accordingly, these opponents have precisely the same difficulty I have just described and are equally hard put to

arrive at ready positions from which satisfactory returns can be effected.

Net play, one of the most fascinating and potentially exciting aspects of tennis, is a weak dimension of any

singles match between average players. To play net, you have to be there first. But in singles, either to serve or to

receive a serve you must be positioned in the back court. You are, accordingly, in exactly the wrong part of the court for performing any type of strategy or stroke at the net. You

must go through the extra step of shifting locations. Even if the opponents send you soft balls, easily returned well by you to bad locations for them, you still have to hurry to be

properly in place for your anticipated smash from the net of

the expected, smashable next ball from the other side.

At best you have a run to perform. At worst you need to combine that run with arranging yourself at its end for a

successful net shot. In doubles, in contrast, two of the players can start from net positions even as the ball is

served to commence the game. Net play is an active, strong ingredient of doubles from the very first, even in a match among ordinary players.

Admittedly, a tennis match between two ordinary singles players may on occasion display some laudatory exchanges. On the whole, however, it is not inspiring to watch. It provides both players with substantial exercise but only modest satisfaction, a poorly balanced mixture for what is intended as sport, in contrast with work or domes-

tic quarrels.

Doubles, not singles, is especially the sensible choice of

game for players who are average physically but possess above average reasoning ability and knowledge of human

nature. Doubles offers them more opportunity for the

injection of strategy and psychology. The moves during a doubles match, altering as they do the relative geometry of location of four players, are much more amenable to the

employment of trickery and deception than the moves between only two players. Sending balls so they will land between the doubles partners and favoring the weaker of

the two are but two of many ploys that can be used to confound, confuse, and roughen the workings of the play-

ers on the other side of the net. Another example is to lob over the opposing net player's head. This will cause his or her partner to have to decide whether to shift over from the other half of their court to get behind the net player and try to handle the lob. The net player meanwhile must decide whether to attempt a quick retreat from the net so

as to intercept that lob or else rush to the other half of the net to cover the open area abandoned by the partner, who

is perhaps also seeking to make the return.

Doubles partners must cooperate and be compatible. Oftentimes their chance of success in attaining that happy

situation is marginal. It then becomes possible for a crafty opponent to place returns so as to magnify such weaknesses

in teamwork as may exist. For instance, it is common for players to possess bad habits that lead to frequent loss of points but to be totally unaware of their undesirable pat-

terns of play. Equally commonly, their partners may judge the cause of the penalizing errors to be very evident and believe that a ready cure exists in simply abandoning the bad habit, a cure the error-prone player refuses to con-

sider, making for a potentially nasty difference between

partners that the other team can build upon.

net and the back baseline and to try to play all balls coming

She has seen an outstanding player do just that with

winning results, and she insists on emulating that perform-

If their opponents direct almost all their shots at Ostinatas feet, two things will happen. These opponents will usually make the point, and they will also frustrate Testarta, making her more furious with each shot. When a ball

goes to Testarta, she will overdrive it because of her emotional disturbance.

Or consider the case of Lodovico Boccaccio and his son Giambattista. They are both excellent athletes and exceptional singles players in tennis. The son is just beginning to defeat the father in singles. As a doubles combination they do not do enough thinking about how to cooperate and

share so as to form a good team. Send a ball between them, each one a fast and aggressive player, and Lodovico will go for the ball, feeling that his senior position makes the

responsibility his, while Giambattista will move in for the

return, confident his hit will be stronger. They sometimes collide or strike each other. They always interfere with one

another so neither can return well.

As a final example, we take note of my friend Castiglione, who is perhaps the fastest moving tennis player I know. He does well in singles because of his speed, but he

is completely oblivious to the possibility of incorporating strategy. He has a powerful forehand and a relatively powerless backhand. In singles he runs around his backhand and takes most balls on the forehand. With his

quickness, he gets by in singles.

But Castiglione is an annoying doubles partner. When he avoids using his backhand and favors his forehand, he has to move too often into his partner’s territory, crowding

the partner and leaving an open area on his other side. Crafty doubles opponents will exploit this pattern.

Analytical tenniseers, those with the ability to add a mental component of the play to the physical, will have an advantage in doubles over players whose interests and aptitudes lie entirely with the physical side of the game.

For the cerebral player, doubles should be dominant. But this forces us at the outset to discuss a basic problem peculiar to doubles, namely, the selection of partners. Let

You and they should be on speaking terms throughout the match, exchanging occasional comments between points and sometimes even during the play. For example,

On the other hand, open criticism of a partner’s play on the spot immediately after its occurrence is seldom appreciated no matter how constructive and, in principle, justified.

To those convinced they possess such superior analytical abilities as to demand that they accept the responsibility to the tennis-playing society of formulating recommenda-

Just as one should never lecture to partners or blame them vocally when points are lost, highly detailed discussion of strategy should be minimized. Unless one expects

to play virtually exclusively with the same partner for many years, it is prudent to assume that sharing wiles and cunning tactics with a partner of the moment may result

inadvertently in sharing it also with an opponent when in

some later match the former ally becomes the present rival. Furthermore, as will be appreciated later, when devious acts and dirty tricks are described in this text, it is well that they remain secret from opponents and partners

Obviously, no specific item of strategy in doubles deserves a higher priority than selecting the strongest avail-

able partner in the overall. There is a double reward here.

Not only will that player’s strength aid your side, but his or her absence from the other side, and a replacement there by a lesser player, will lower the opposing team’s average

quality of play. But there is more to it than that.

Nobody is perfect, so you cannot have a perfect partner.

But having done as well as you can in selecting and recruiting that partner, you should know his or her gifts and handicaps well and be thoroughly aware of your own

strong points and limitations. Then you can build a strategy for your team to maximize the positives and minimize the negatives. Having your own play properly sized up should lead you to choose a partner whose play will overlap with yours, one's strong points covering the other's weaknesses.

Bruno, for example, is a fine partner for me. He has no fast shots to build on but is very steady. He is sure not to

net or overdrive a ball of usual, reasonable speed. He will send it reliably, time after time, to a sensible place on the other side. He cannot slam the ball for a sure point or cut

into the ball to give it a spin and a confusing bounce, but he can lob accurately. He provides me with much freedom

and confidence to move about, planning deceitful stances and clever placements and putting away shots. I know my

strategy will never be spoiled by Bruno's moving irrationally or missing easy balls while I am setting up to take the point.

You should start with ground strokes from your back court. After adequately examining the opponent’s backhand and forehand imbalances, you should deliberately hit

to exhort them to dash forward, so as to handle your ball after only one bounce. In warm-up any number of bounces

is acceptable. No, yours is to be a more subtle objective, and carrying it out requires some observational talent and

expertise.

Consider, for example, that every good athlete, when stationed well back, will exhibit an automatic, quick response to a short return. Even as the ball leaves your racket, you should watch your opponent's eyes and body intently. That person, if of exceptional athletic ability, will be seen to tighten muscles and lunge forward instantly.

Since it is the warm-up period, the opponent may quickly control further motion, relax, and decide merely to wait for

the ball to arrive after an additional bounce or two. If you

Some players have difficulty dropping a ball just over the net from a position back in the court. May I suggest a line of thought most helpful in executing such a stroke? Imag-

ine that at some point along the net a lightweight target (perhaps a pigeon made of thin wood) is perched, balanced precariously, ready to fall over if merely touched. It is your job to direct a ball at that target—not to knock it off forcibly

and certainly not to send it reeling into the opposite court

at high velocity but merely to tip it over. If you hold this picture in mind you will automatically adjust your stance

and swing perfectly to effect a drop shot.

If you are not way back in your court when you contemplate this short shot, you may find it very annoying to your opponents if you toss a spinning ball over the net. Here your focus of attention must be on cutting into and under

the ball to make it spin. You may deliver it well over the net, and it will still fail to go on back to your opponents if you catch them too far from the net. The spin here is the

important ingredient, and you can observe during warm-

up if your competitors, even if they get up to the ball to try

to handle it, find it hard to adjust for the confusing motion of your spinning ball after its bounce, the certain result of

your cutting into the ball.

It is difficult to cut a ball and impart a high rate of spin to

it while also hitting it with all your might. Achieving such

strong strokes regularly would stamp you as an extraordi-

nary, not merely ordinary, player. But it is not too tough to achieve a spin when returning an easy ball that is quite low when you contact it. Here again a particular mental image

is helpful. Think of the ball approaching you as having a northern and a southern hemisphere. In the middle of the southern hemisphere sits the continent of Africa. Pretend you do not like it where it is. You would like to wipe it with your racket, when the ball arrives, so as to drive Africa

south, actually down into the South Pole. This means you do not swing at the ball with a big stroke to direct it forward, your arm well back as you begin the swing. You do

not hold your racket so the string face is vertical, so you can

smack the ball directly with the full face of the racket. Nor do you hit the ball with the racket face horizontal, wiping the South Pole itself with your strings. Instead, you tilt the racket about 45 degrees so your point of contact is right on Africa, and you continue into and under the ball, thus cutting into it and turning Africa south.

Practice this play first on a short, slow ball from your opponents. Move up and send Africa downward. Your ball will pass over the net, drop, and then bounce peculiarly.

Your enemy on the other side may either curse or compli-

You should claim then that your cutting return was the accidental result of your almost missing the ball and happening to catch it way off from the useful area of your strings. During the course of the game, you need make no comment after giving the ball a successful spin. Simply register the point each time.

The fundamental strategy for players whose physical prowess does not enable them to surmount their opponents play is to concentrate on minimizing errors. This rule applies throughout the match. It is especially to be emphasized at the very beginning of play, with the serve.

Serving is the only time during tennis matches in which players have the opportunity to lose the point entirely by

themselves, with no contribution to the action from opponents or partners—especially because the service area into which the ball must be placed is so limited in size and

because the net stands mischievously right in the way of

that little square.

It is extremely tempting to seek to deliver a served ball

so hard and fast that it cannot be returned. Top players attain that objective with reasonable frequency, sometimes

even against opponents of remarkable skill and speed of reaction. The ordinary player, however, should discard

such an ambitious goal or attempt to reach it rarely and then with caution and reservations. Thoughtful analysis of

an average player's real chances of “acing” the opponent usually yields discouraging results. Trying for an ace that will make the point immediately may much more often lose

it instead, for two reasons. The first is that the serve may become a fault too frequently, the ball either striking the

net or else overshooting the small area in which it should land. To serve only to hear monotonously repetitive calls of “fault!” is truly service beyond the call of duty. The second reason is that even though the effort may result in a good “in” ball, it may fall short of attaining the real speed of an ace and will merely bounce high. An elevated post-bounce ball helps the receiver to execute a forceful return that will

immediately present trouble to the server.

An ace attempt which is successful only once in ten times makes too small a contribution when it works. Since only

Granted these circumstances, what does all this have to do with strategy and psychology? Are not problems or opportunities stemming from close calls nonexistent since we expect absolute honesty on the part of all callers of boundary shots? In reply, we agree first that we must

adhere strictly to the rule that all in or out calls must be handled with scrupulous integrity. Cheating should never even be contemplated. (As a practical matter, cheating in

making close calls can rarely be profitable. Cheating inevitably will be discovered if it is practiced substantially, the only way it can be effective.) By employing appropriate

guile without cheating, the calling of close shots can produce more favorable results for players who master the

knack than a laissez-faire attitude about the function would achieve.

The basis for this assertion rests in the fundamental difficulty of judging the bounce point when it is close to the

line. This difficulty is so severe that the average player, although having the absolutely sincere intention of always making a correct and honest call, can succeed in doing so only about half the time. The ball is as likely to be out when perceived as in, or really in when thought to be out. We are

speaking here of the truly close shot, not those manifestly one way or the other but merely not seen to be either for a certainty by a player with cataracts. In this latter instance,

the fifty-fifty average may still apply, but the operational width of the line, the pertinent arena of close calls, simply becomes enlarged to match the fuzziness of the vision.

If half of the close calls are called against you, albeit inadvertently, innocently, and uprightly, you need a set of tactics that will cause a result more conducive to building up the score of the match in your favor. How can this objective be attained?

An idea that is bound to come to mind immediately is somehow to alter your own calls when an opponent's ball lands near a line. Let us immediately veto that approach. You should continue to call them as you see them, trying as

always before to aspire to honesty and accuracy. It is

important instead to recognize that one steady, certain characteristic of human nature is always present to affect

the calls. The instinct for self-preservation is inherent and deep in all members of Homo sapiens. The sensing signals that this instinct generates make you see the opposition’s liners as out balls if any slight possibility exists of arriving at

that interpretation. Automatically, with no planning or other effort on your part, your calls of your oppositions very close, not truly discernible shots will appear to you to

be out more often than in.

Your opponents are like you, virtuous but also human. When your returns land in the unfocusable visual noise

regions along the lines in their court, they will be called out more often than in. It is here that, in principle, a crop

of calls by your opponents in your favor is available for your

reaping. You must create in the minds of your rivals a bias in the direction of calling the close calls in. To accomplish this we suggest several steps.

You should take the first step early in the match. You must watch carefully for the first ball happening to arrive in your court that is fairly close to a line but is clearly out. It

must be far enough out that the opponents also will surely have seen it as out. But—though this may at first seem an

incredibly nonsensical action—you must call it in. Yes, you

should give away the point! Noting that you are doing this, your opponents may even challenge your call out of earnest

sportsmanship. But you must be adamant.

“I saw it clearly,” you should insist firmly. “Your ball definitely caught the line. There’s no reason for playing it over. Let’s go ahead with the game.”

You must perform this step twice. By so doing, before the first set has progressed very far you will have planted three important impressions irrevocably in your rivals brains: (1) you cannot see well; (2) your honesty is impeccable; (3) what you cannot see you will call in their favor out

of sportsmanship. Your image will thereby immediately be established as so singularly admirable that the effect will be

a preshaming of your opponents. Their subconsciousnesses will come to believe that someone such as you deserves

special courtesies and gestures. The twenty or thirty close

shots you deposit in their court during the rest of the match will all be seen and called by them as in, whereas, without

the spell your behavior has cast, your balls falling into the

narrow blurry zones would have been called out.

Meanwhile, in your court, the balls arriving in the semiunseeable line areas will require that your own observation system be engaged in conventional close-call judging du-

ties. You will be totally honest, as usual. But your subcon-

scious will be influenced by the poignant memory of your having deliberately given away two points to the enemy.

Subconscious minds are tough, stubborn, and highly protective negotiators. They always seek compensations for

generosity that they will independently judge to be beyond the call. Without your conscious mind even being aware of

it, or compromising integrity in any way, you will see liners

as out more often than you otherwise would. In view of the

respect and awe with which you will have come to be regarded because of your early performance on calls, your opponents will entertain no suspicions or doubts about your honesty. Moreover, being farther away from the bounce point, they will know they could not have seen with any greater clarity than you precisely where the ball

actually touched the ground. Have no fear. They will never

think to contest your calls.

You can help to maintain the proper impression of your integrity and visual capabilities by taking certain additional

steps. For instance, the following common event will occur early in the first set. You will hit a ball out, so far out that your opponents will feel absolutely no need to call that it was out. They will assume you or anyone with normal

eyesight will readily have perceived the bounce as occurring well outside the critical boundary lines. When this happens, you must be sure to ask, in a most gentle voice,

“Excuse me, but was my shot in or out?”

This will startle the players on the other side for more than one reason, all useful to your objective. First, they will be surprised to find you seemingly unaware of the

optically obvious. Second, they will believe from your

question’s wording and tone that you were not debating the call in the slightest but simply wanting to know what it was. Third, they will be reinforced in their impression that you

are half-blind and so you might have believed that the ball

fell in and that they called it out incorrectly, and even,

heaven forbid, that they were cheating. * The result will be a mental memo to themselves to be certain in the future to announce every out ball as an out

ball, loud and clear, no matter how far out it lands. And,

most important, they will be biased to be very careful that their judgment is right on all liners—which translates to their calling them in balls whenever any doubt whatever exists in their minds, as it always does on close calls.

A closely fought match typically will include enough close calls to decide most games and sets. Shifting half the close calls your way can alter a five to seven set score

against you to a six to amore score in your favor. The approach we have described to influence the calls requires that you make only a modest effort the first few times to make certain you perform the steps smoothly. After that you will be able to employ the technique perfectly with hardly a thought. If winning is important to you, that effort will pay off.

It is not that the match necessarily turns into a battle of the sexes, although it may well do that. Yet mixed doubles is a common form of tennis. It is doubtless here to stay. It will last as long as there are two

sexes making up the human race and the race plays tennis. Accordingly, it is important for us to take up some guidelines that apply to the entirely meritorious desire we

should have to win most sets and to make wile and guile properly useful in reaching that objective.

At the outset, let us admit that upon rare occasions a male and female partner may be able to play a tennis match with their sexual differences not appearing to exert even a

remote influence on how they play and how they get along. They are simply two tennis players whose cooperative

operation depends totally on their skills, both physical and

mental. They are not perfect partners because neither is a perfect tennis player and because they are not perfectly

matched to achieve a maximum probability of winning every point. Their relationship, good or bad, as tennisplaying partners, however, has nothing to do with their

sexes. When such a situation occurs it may be considered a minor miracle. The occasions are so infrequent, so seldom to be encountered by the reader in a lifetime of tennis playing, that they are not worth further discussion here.

We limit our commentary to the far more common and hence more important pairing in which the male-female

interplay is indeed a problem and should be dealt with so

as to attain greatly improved results on the court. We shall divide our presentation on the devilments and mischievous

exploitations of the coupling of the sexes on the court into two parts. First, we shall consider matters inherent in the different expectancies of behavior of the two sexes. Then we shall take up an array of potentials that arise from the

fact that very often two individuals find themselves part-

ners in mixed doubles only because they are partners in one or another aspect of life off the court.

A given female and a given male of the species Homo sapiens may be found accurately ranked either way as to the relative superiority of their skills on the tennis court.

On the average, perhaps, men tennis players are stronger than women players. A typical man may be able to hit the ball harder than a typical woman. Men, again statistically,

tend to be larger than women and have longer legs. So, although it does not absolutely follow, most men players

may be able to run faster than most women players. But power in the stroke and speed in running constitute only

two of at least a dozen major criteria defining a good tennis player. Also to be taken into account are timing and

accuracy, form and agility, a sense of the ball’s trajectory,

consistency, stamina, concentration, training, experience,

repetitive practice, sharpness of eye, and a host of mental characteristics, such as the capability to research and antic-

ipate the opponents actions and habits. A player who hits the ball harder may hit that ball out most of the time. A player who runs faster often may run to the wrong place, or fail to start running early enough owing to a lack of

observational ability, or overrun an oncoming ball.

Objective analysis suggests that we have no way of knowing a priori, if all we are told are the sexes of a group of players, how to rank them as tennis players. Still less are we able to list them in order of their superiority as tennis

partners in doubles.

Yet our culture, the result of centuries, nay millennia, of habit building, causes most men to assume they are better tennis players than most women. Furthermore, many, if not most, women will go along with this same initial assumption. Given a man and a woman without previous mutual court experience who are commencing play as

mixed doubles partners, it can be expected that the man usually will presume he is superior physically on the tennis court (and some of the women will grant that to be likely) until the actual play might show the opposite to be true.

Most men and women will suggest that the man should serve first. They think he has a better chance of winning his serve than does his female partner. But this is an assumption that should not be made based alone on the sexes of the two partners. It may be that the man is particularly good up at the net and the first game is more likely to be won if the woman is allowed to serve first by virtue of the

man’s skillful net play. Or, it may be that the man, though serving somewhat harder, unfortunately serves doubles too often, especially in the first game, before he has warmed

up adequately. Then, instead of accepting the strong probability that the first game will be lost if the man serves, the

woman should be the first server. This is more likely to be

true if, though her service may not have the power of the

standard ace serve, she is accurate and can place the ball near the corners or along the lines of the service rectangle, either of which will help to cause the receiver of the serve

to return poorly and set up for her male partner at the net to intercept and score the point.

The man often will plan to cover substantially more territory than his woman partner. When balls arrive between them, he will feel he should take the initiative in making the return. Moreover, he will feel compelled too often to show he is a man by hitting the ball hard, whether

serving or performing back-court drives or strikes from the net. Similarly, he may feel it unmanly to allow a high ball to

overhead smash shot, even if the ball goes high for him and his timing does not permit him to include that stroke in his bill of fare.

the woman with whom he is teamed accordingly decides to avoid the net and share the back court with him even when he serves, he may feel insulted. Finally, since it is common for an ordinary player's forehand strokes to be superior to backhand strokes, it is common for the woman, with no

discussion, to be automatically placed in the forehand court, the man taking the backhand as the assumed

Cultural prejudices about the proper roles of the sexes work in numerous diverse ways. Many women bring to the

court an affected desire to be ultrafeminine and wish their

male partners to be ultramasculine. They expect the man to take the initiative, to poach and encroach and dominate the play on their side. They are disappointed if he does not rise to handle what they consider to be his true mission. By their actions they put burdensome pressure on the male player to play his tennis in the most male-superior fashion possible. They continually and aggressively solicit com-

opponent—all such importunings based on the idea that

the male partner is the superior tennis player and should act as the captain.

Suppose, however, that the female is steadier, smarter, faster, anticipates better, and has a broader repertoire of

strokes, while the male is erratic, sloppy, slow, misses easy balls or nets them, and overhits. A mismatch occurs if

either one of the partners allows the customs developed over thousands of years of civilization to determine his or her attitude toward the partner of the opposite sex.

Cultural biases can be the basis not only of the actions described but of severe reactions to them. There are females who will go out of their way to buck the culture, who will insist on taking over more of the court, refuse

If you are a female, select a man who will support you to the maximum, of course. But if you are a well above average player, do not allow yourself to pair up with a male who is totally impressed with his physical endowments and

In their married life either of the two mates may be the strong one, the two having developed a stable pattern in which the weak leans on the strong. Perhaps the two have

knocks. Perhaps the male is the strong partner in their

marriage. But what if, on the tennis court, she is the stronger athlete? This could be the case for numerous reasons—her talent, his lack of it, her speed, his sluggishness, her daily practice, his infrequent playing, her excellent physical shape and stamina, his terrible out-of-shape condition making him unsuitable for severe physical activ-

Of course, in their marriage the man might be the dependent one, looking for strength and leadership from his wife, while on the tennis court he may be far superior to her in every department of play. The point is that the

pattern of relationship in marriage, whatever it may be, is

too often moved over into the court, where it is inapplicable. Not surprisingly, all shortcomings, defects, incompatibilities (some fatal and others merely limiting happiness)

that exist in a marriage, when brought to the tennis court, are made worse. Bad personalities, immaturities, selfishness, lack of mutual regard and confidence, and resentments often suppressed in a marriage that is not on solid

ground emerge on the tennis court. Everything comes out. If one partner is a poor sport, inclined to blame the other when things do not go well, assumes a superiority that is

unjustified, finds it difficult to admit being wrong, and is convinced of being right but annoyed because of being

thought wrong by the partner, then these attitudes become quickly evident when doubles play does not go well. And naturally, if the relationship of partners incorporates these negative aspects, the play is unlikely to go well. To this listing of deficient partners we can add those who are prone

to recrimination, who must win or fall apart, who are overambitious, inconsiderate, and excessively critical. One partner may be given to a little cheating on close calls, and if the spouse rebuts the call instead of backing it up, the perpetrator considers the challenge disloyal.

As opponents, we can see unfolding before us on the other side of the court not only the negative playing results of inharmonious matching, namely, point-losing tennis, but also the deterioration of their marriage through the

injection of a tennis facet. Marginal marriages can be thrown on the rocks by doubles play. Those on the brink may go over before one set is completed. This is bad if the marriage could have survived without the mixing of tennis doubles into the marital stew. It is good, perhaps, if the dissolution of the partnership was sure to be only a matter of time and if tennis partnering accelerates the inevitable.

Singles net play is much harder to perform well than doubles net play. Of course, some similarities exist, but net nettling, blocking, poaching, and most strategy aspects of doubles net play are either absent entirely as useful tactics

in singles or else require such alterations as to be untranslatable profitably. Admittedly, net play in singles may tend

to increase your speed of muscular responses and your endurance. Only in this indirect way will it make you a better net player in a future doubles match. But you could

get the same effect by engaging in boxing or fencing.

There are two novel ways to play singles that will yield you remarkable gains in subsequent doubles play, your priority form of tennis. These approaches to the game

should be called half-doubles rather than singles. The first way could also be named diagonal or cross-court tennis. As

shown in Figure 34, in which the forehand courts are shaded, the play of the first point of the game, according to the rules of cross-court half-doubles, must be limited to this shaded area. A serves to B, and B must return, as must A next, only into the respective cross-court halves of the full doubles court. Any ball bouncing elsewhere, for exam-

ple, into either backhand cross-court, must be called out.

In playing this first point, it is as though A and B have partners C and D who are invisible but very much present. C and D never poach, but we pretend their skills are awesome. Both, we imagine, are certain to put away for an immediate point any ball that strays into their halves of the court, that is, any ball that fails to bounce in the crosscourted areas. Accordingly, A and B must avoid those two

unseen players. The penalty for sending the ball outside the cross-court regions is the loss of the point being played.

The second point and every alternate one thereafter calls for a serve from and to the backhand court. Again, the

playing out of the point is restricted to exchanges between

those cross-court areas. Any ball going into the two forehand half-courts is counted as an out ball.

Half-doubles requires the players to imagine a line (X-Y

in Fig. 34) to be drawn on the court. Since this line is not present on the markings of the standard court, a burden is

placed on players making close calls regarding balls bounc-

ing along that invisible line. But the main objective of the

game is to develop doubles skill, not to win singles con-

tests, still less to build up accuracy in the calling of close

shots. Thus inaccuracies in close calls should not be greatly disturbing to the players, although there may be more of

them than in a standard doubles match.

We notice that most elements of play in this half-doubles game have direct relation to regular doubles play. The area of the useful half-doubles court assigned to each player is exactly as in doubles. Serving and receiving of serves are

identical with corresponding doubles situations. Especially

so are the routine cross-court exchanges from the back courts. Moreover, if you put your opponent into deep

trouble with a very effective deep shot, you can practice dashing up to the net just as you might in doubles. The width of the net to defend or exploit is precisely that

covered by a net player in ordinary doubles under like

circumstances.

Admittedly, you will find some aspects of true doubles play missing or different, as for instance, your running behind a partner to return a lob, or the fact that the

invisible partner never takes over to provide you with respites during the playing out of a point. But everything you do in strokes and motions while playing cross-court half-doubles provides you with practice for a similar doubles situation. Half-doubles should improve your steadi-

ness, accuracy, power, and physical conditioning for the

playing of doubles.
