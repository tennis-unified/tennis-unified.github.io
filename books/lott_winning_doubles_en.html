In the late 1920's and early 1930's, George Lott was to doubles in tennis what Bill Tilden was to singles—namely, the world's best player.

He won the U.S. men's doubles championships a total of five times with three different partners. He won the French doubles championships with still another partner, with whom he eventually won the first of his two Wimbledon doubles championships. In mixed doubles, he added three more U.S. championships and a third Wimbledon title. He also starred for the U.S. in Davis Cup play and toured for years with the Tilden pro troupe.

His fame as a doubles player was such that in time he became the standard by which all doubles players were measured. And as renowned an expert as U.S. Open tournament director Bill Talbert has rated Lott and Australian great John Bromwich, unques-

tionably, as the greatest pure doubles players of all times. en What was it that made Lott, and later Bromwich, so extraordi-' >> nary? Was it their overwhelming serves? Their devastating overheads? Their bullet-like forehands and backhands? Their unerring placements? Not at all.

“Lott and Bromwich were the great masters of the game of doubles,’’ said Talbert, in that “they were primarily playmakers. In other words, they utilized thought, study, court craft, stroke variety, finesse, spin, tactics, fakes, teasers, anticipation and well-planned maneuvers to force their opponents either into making a weak return or leaving an opening. All these masters needed as a partner. . . was a rangy, power player to take maximum advantage of every opening they established.”

That's an analysis that Lott not only agrees with but it's a philosophy of doubles play that he still, today in his 70's, teaches at

DePaul University, where he's currently the tennis coach; at the Nielsen Tennis Center in nearby Winnetka, Ill., where he’s the tennis coordinator; and at junior and adult tennis clinics,.which he still finds time to run during the summer monihs.

George Lott has written numerous articles for TENNIS on the strokes and strategies so essential to an overall better game. But

here, for the first time in book form, is a distillation of the secrets of winning doubles play that he learned as a player half a century

ago and has taught so successfully to others ever since.

“But I already play singles pretty well,’’ you may be saying to yourself, “so why should I need any special help in doubles?’ I put

that question to George when we first started our work together, and he answered:

“Good singles play requires excellent technique, good power, fleetness of foot and considerable stamina. While these are all useful attributes for a doubles player, they are, to a degree, not quite so necessary. What is necessary is a head on your shoulders and the ability to use the brain that resides there. A good doubles player must use his head.

“Il have won a good many doubles titles, ranging from U.S. national junior championships to Wimbledon and Forest Hills doubles championships—more than 40 major titles in all, largely because my partners and I were able to outsmart our opponents. A thinking doubles team can often beat a team that is better equipped in terms of stroking ability simply through cunning and cleverness.

“That's what I hope to show in this book—that you, me, practically anyone can win at doubles by using his head. That's ‘why | call doubles the thinking player’s game. The more you analyze your own actions and those of your opponents, the better your doubles play will become.”

Let me add that this is not a book which will turn you into a doubles champion overnight. But read a few chapters and you'll find, I'm sure, a number of key ideas that you can quickly incorporate into your own game. My favorite is the short return of

service into the opposite alley. It's a simple tactic, easy to execute, but one that gives my opponents fits and tires them out in the bargain. And there are many more ideas like that here, ideas you

can pick out and put to work for you just as they've worked for George Lott over the years.

Remember, though, that doubles is a game to be enjoyed. Or as George himself is fond of saying, ‘Doubles doubles your fun.”

Of course, if you win, too—and this book will help you do that— well, so much the better.

When baseball immortal Willie Keeler was once asked the secret of

his amazing batting success, he shifted a large cud of tobacco from his right cheek to his left and said: “T hit ‘em where they ain't.” His grammar may have been weak, but his logic was inescapable.

And the same applies to tennis.

It means, in short, that the most useful attribute you can develop for doubles play is the ability to disguise your shots. Why?

Because the more you can disguise them, the less time your opponents will have to decide what you are doing and respond accordingly. And that will give you the edge you need, very often, to “hit

The three most important shots you will hit in this context are the forehand drive, the lob and the soft chip or dink shot, each of which must be hit with almost the same motion to achieve maximum effect.

The advantages of a good drive are obvious—it will be your workhorse stroke when you are returning serve or find yourself

forced to play from the baseline. A good lob will come into play when your opponents are up close to the net; it will drive them back

to the baseline and give you a chance to get back into the point. The advantages of a soft chip or dink shot, which I consider my

specialty, are so numerous that I'll be discussing them later in great detail. For now, let me say this is the one shot that is most likely to

send your opponents scrambling around the court—that is most likely to set up a weak return which you can then put away in short order.

All three of these shots you probably have in your arsenal right now. If not, you'll have to get them if you ever hope to achieve your full potential in doubles. They're not really so hard to perfect, though. You can probably master them—and learn to disguise them, too, if you haven't already—with the help of one of the many good instruction books on stroke-making now on the market. If you

can't, you may need the help and advice of a competent tennis teaching professional. If so, I most heartily recommend that you get

them. The time and energy you invest in these strokes will pay off

many times over in personal satisfaction and future on-court

success.

That's the first step in developing a winning doubles game. The second is learning to play in harness with your partner, whoever that might be.

When I was at the peak of my game, people were always asking me how I managed to win so many doubles titles—what was the secret of my success. The answer I usually gave was that I won because | had good partners. In those days, I was trying to

appear modest. But now that I've had plenty of time to reflect on the subject, I'm certain that was true. A doubles team, I'm convinced, is only as good as its weakest link.

At the tournament level—or any level, really—it means an ideal doubles team should consist of a tactician, a player who plans each point and sets up most of the winning situations, and a

“closer,” a stronger player who can be counted on to hit a slashing volley or a blistering overhead whenever it’s called for. In theory,

at least, the strengths of a doubles pair like that will complement each other and offset any individual weaknesses.

Unfortunately, in real life it rarely works out that way. Often you have to take what you can get. But that said, there are still

certain qualities you should look for in choosing a doubles partner, especially if you hope to make it a Jasting alliance.

The most successful and enduring partnerships I've found through the years are not those of players with complementary

skills but those of players who have similar skills. And for a very good reason; they usually work together so much better. Sotryand » get yourself a partner with a game similar to yourown, but make °»

sure it’s someone you can get along with pretty well. And when you find such a partner, try and practice with him as much as you can until you get to the point where each of you knows pretty much what the other will do in a given situation. Good doubles players

should be able to play together and understand each other with a minimum of verbal communication—and no acrimonious postmortems.

In time, you may find ways in which you can be helpful to your partner—and he or she to you. One of you may catch something

the other is doing wrong and that can easily be corrected. Make suggestions, but commands never. And never, never argue with your partner; don't even have a heated discussion, or you'll soon

find your game disintegrating. Complete harmony is essential, and a single pat on the back at an appropriate moment, if not a cure-all, is more productive by far than a thousand recriminations.

What happens if your partnership doesn't work out? Well, you should discuss the situation with your partner and try and reconcile your differences. But if you can't, it’s best to split up then and there

and look for someone else. And when you find that someone, stick with him; you'll both be better for it.

On occasion, of course, you'll find yourself playing with a doubles partner whom you'd never have chosen on your own, given the option. You can still have fun, though—and maybe win,

too—if you follow the principles I've outlined here and try to work together as a team as best you can.

It isn't always easy. But that’s what doubles is all about— teamwork. That's the keystone of a winning doubles game—the wellspring from which all else flows.

Doubles is such a fast-moving game it's impossible to reduce its requirements to a simple set of rules that will guarantee success on every occasion. There are, however, some basic principles of doubles play you should learn and remember—and try to put into play whenever you're on court. I call these the Six Axioms of Doubles Play. They are:

Use the whole court; Look before you hit; Plan your moves in advance;

Look at your court through your opponents’ eyes; Don't get set in your ways, and Make the best use of your partner. All of these, as you can see, and if you want to be good, require

that you think.

Of course, you can always go out and blast away at the ball— the way you do, perhaps, in singles. That doesn't take a great deal of thought, and you may get away with it a time or two in doubles. But once you move up in class—to where you're playing someone who knows the game—it's the quickest way I know to lose. The

If you compete as a member of a club, high school or college team or if you travel frequently, you will at some time find yourself playing on new and different courts. Although the shape of a

If you are going to play away from home, inquire about the courts before you leave. On a windy day, put a little extra spin on your shots. The spin will give you more control and help you keep the ball within the confines of the court. Put plenty of spin on your serve when the wind is behind you. The spin will bring the ball down faster and so

lob a couple of times unsuccessfully, wait until the wind drops before you use it again.

The sun can also be a nuisance, especially early in the morning or at the end of the day when it is low in the sky and hard for your eyes to avoid when you throw the ball up to serve. The

problem can be solved if one member of your team is a lefty since you can each serve with your backs to the sun. If that option is not

Indoor Courts es For those of us who live in the colder parts of the country, indoor =. courts now make it possible to play tennis year-round. In fact,

indoor courts have probably done a great deal to popularize doubles and mixed doubles play since the cost of renting an hour of

Many players are beginning to prefer to play indoors all year. Indoor courts are not only heated in winter, they are airconditioned in summer so indoor play is possible and comfortable. The conditions are consistent: the lighting is the same week in and week out, the temperature is generally closely controlled and the effects of wind and sun are absent.

Perhaps the only disadvantage of indoor play, especially for doubles, is the height of the building. It is all too easy to hit a high lob that hits the roof or a suspended light fixture. The only answer to that problem is to hit your lobs lower and perhaps hit mostly offensive lobs when you have the chance.

Some players find the lights of an indoor court to be a problem when serving. If that is so for you, alter your serving position as you

might do when playing outdoors in the sun. Many of the newer indoor courts have indirect lighting where the lights face upward onto a reflective roof so the lights will not be directly in a server's

line of vision. If you have trouble with indoor lights, I suggest you look for a club with indirect lighting.

Check Out the Courts Whether you play indoors or out, it will pay to check out an

unfamiliar court before playing. Is the surface unfamiliar to you? Are there any obstructions such as lights that might interfere with play? Is the court less than regulation size so there is less room between the sidelines and the fences or between the baselines and the back fences? Does the court itself look in good shape or might you expect to get some bad bounces? A few minutes of observation will help you decide on your game plan.

Whatever you do, try to take advantage of the playing conditions wherever you play. Please do not, as many tennis players do, blame your loss on the court or on the playing conditions. A good player can adapt to almost any situation; make that your goal, too.

A Very Sociable Game Although many of the touring professionals scoff at mixed doubles, I think it’s one of the most pleasant versions of the sport. There are very few sports where men and women can play together in

“intersexual competition” even though their abilities may be different. Mixed doubles can be the social variety you often see in the weekend afternoon games at many tennis clubs, or it can be quite ‘competitive.

Not too long ago, women were considered to be the lesser players when it came to mixed doubles. Many good male players

considered a woman's abilities sufficient if she could merely get her serve in and keep the ball in play on those occasions when the ball came straight to her. Fortunately, many women club players

today are the equal of their male partners and in more than a few cases they are definitely superior. Where the abilities of a mixed doubles team are pretty evenly matched, there's little difference

between mixed and regular doubles.

However, there are still plenty of teams where the tennisplaying skills differ and playing good mixed doubles is often a question of how to use those skills to best advantage. Even when abilities differ, it's still possible to play good tennis and for all the players to have an enjoyable match. How you can do that is what I

hope to show you in this chapter.

Picking Your Partner Let me come right out and say that, with very few exceptions, in mixed doubles spouses should not play on the same team. When

husband and wife play together, only one small error is needed for tempers to start becoming frayed and the tennis takes an almost

immediate nosedive. I'm not sure why spouses are so sensitive to each other on the tennis court—perhaps it's something related to the competitive nature of the sport—but spouses rarely seem to perform well together in mixed doubles play.

So the first essential is to pick a partner with whom you get along well on and off the court. If you are a very aggressive player,

you may fare better with an aggressive partner. If you are the

easy-going type, perhaps you ought to pick a partner who can

laugh with you at your own mistakes.

The second consideration is ability. The best mixed doubles teams are those in which the skills are pretty evenly matched. Your partner should have a reliable serve that won't fall apart under pressure. A competent return of serve ig equally useful especially if you know that you are likely to be facing some strong servers. And,

of course, your partner should not be afraid of going to the net when the opportunity occurs. You may not find all those abilities in

equal measure in a partner, but they are well worth looking for if you wish to play competitively.
