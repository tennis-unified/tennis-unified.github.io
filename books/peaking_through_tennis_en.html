New Perspectives on the Mind-Body Relationship

An intense young executive helped me crystalize the concepts laid out in this chapter. It certainly wasn’t intentional, but as he earnestly worked his way through a practice session at one of my clinics, he tossed off a comment that said it all. “I’m just not having any fun with this game. I have half a mind not to try so hard!”’ That old cliché ran through me like an electric current. I had started writing and was trying to think of a way to incorporate the research being done on the two hemispheres of the brain into my experiences with tennis. Indeed, my young friend did have half a brain that could rescue him from his in-

tense ‘‘trying’’—and I mean that most literally! All he had to do was tap it and open up a path leading to con-

sistently better and more rewarding play. Let me explain.

The Two Hemispheres of the Brain

Recently, there has been a great deal of scientific and psychological study exploring the functions of the two hemispheres of the brain. The left hemisphere is normally the dominant side and primarily controls the right side of the body (right-handedness is dominant

over left-handedness). As you might have guessed, the right hemisphere is primarily responsible for the left

side of the body.

What is of particular interest to us here are the different kinds of mental processes delegated to each hemisphere. Studies indicate that the left hemisphere is language dominant. It deals primarily with speech

and rational, complex thought patterns. It learns through words. On the other hand, the right hemi-

sphere normally deals with intuition. It speaks in the language of touch and imagery, learns in whole forms

and feelings, and is responsible for spatial creativity.

In the past, tennis teachers and coaches tended to teach to the left hemisphere exclusively. Words have been the main tool for helping a student master both

stroke production and emotions. But, tennis is a fast, reflex game. What player could be expected to

retrieve his coach’s precisely worded instructions when faced with a smashing backhand? ‘‘Now, let’s

see. My racquet is supposed to be here and my body here.’”’ Obviously, the ball would have whizzed by before the first word could be thought! Can you imagine the amateur’s frustration and sense of appearing

foolish over the missed shot? Perhaps, you’ve been there yourself. A totally different process takes place when a coach teaches through right brain imagery and explores the emotional aspects of the game. The calmly centered player forms a perfect stroke through the instant retrieval of an appropriate image. Then, be-

cause he has developed a relaxed awareness, he is in-

stantly ready for the ball’s return.

I first became aware of right brain effectiveness and consistent peak experiences when playing music in the 60’s. While playing bass, I would sometimes hit notes or play bass runs that would bring me an in-

tense sense of joy. It was as though I could see the colors of the notes and float calmly with the vibrations. I would become at one with the notes played by the

other musicians. It was interesting how I and other musicians could play thousands of notes and blend

them without making an error.

Later I realized that such a phenomenon of perfection was due to the right brain. I observed that musicians wouldn’t watch their instruments, nor did they

constantly think about the notes.

Observe musicians yourself, and you will see them “‘feel”’ the notes often with eyes closed rather than

“think”’ the notes. If I had thought about each note (left brain rational thought), I couldn’t have played

“out of my head,”’ so to speak.

Hitting tennis balls with any kind of consistency is like playing an instrument or trying to dance. The

harder you try, and the more you try to analyze the phenomena, the worse you do. You become inhibited by your own verbal instructions. If you feel what you

are supposed to do, detached from constant verbal commands, you will not only eliminate negative emo-

tions but also find that you are playing a peak performance. Your movements will be flowing instead of

halting, and you will sense a oneness with the game and the environment.

Endurance and Choreographing Movement

Because tennis is such a vigorous sport, you can’t expect to reach your peak if you only exercise the mus-

cle between your ears. That masterful mind of yours must be housed in a well-conditioned and responsive body.

I have often marveled at the way club players can win the first two sets 6-0, 6-1, and lose the third and

fourth sets 6-0, 6-0, due to lack of oxygen depth and lack of muscle endurance. I have also wondered at

club players who remain in one area of the court mumbling, ‘‘I can’t,” as the ball passes four feet from

their court position. It has saddened me to watch players move awkwardly about the court attacking

themselves more intensely than the ball through selfcriticism and memories of past athletic failures. More-

over, I am constantly amazed at the lack of body preparation I see at the club level and even at the tour-

ing pro level. The typical player will do a few deep

knee bends, hit thirty balls, and say with naive eagerness, ‘‘Want to play a set?’’ These same players will

often suffer from sprained ankles, twisted knees, torn

cartilage, and tennis elbow.

The tennis adventure involves stretching your body’s capacities as well as your mind’s. Most of us live in a rather sedentary world propelling ourselves

from place to place in automobiles; so, for the total picture of our fitness and health, the requirements for winning tennis’ Game 4, The Physical Game, are well worth mastering.

Let’s leave your psyche for a while and attend to your physique.

In tennis, three elements comprise the physical

game.

1) Endurance—Your capacity to play for long periods of time without muscle and oxygen

depth exhaustion 2) Fluidity—Graceful movements across the court and smooth approaches to the net 3) Flexibility—The capacity to stretch for volleys, reach difficult ground strokes and hit

high on serves and difficult volleys. (Flexibility will be discussed in depth in Chapter Three)

ACCEPTING THE PHYSICAL CHALLENGE

Aerobic and Anaerobic Preparation for Endurance

To overcome injury and the “third set 6-0, 6-0” trap of exhaustion, you must train in both aerobics and anaerobics. Aerobic training builds strength in

the muscular and cardiovascular system while anaerobic training builds the body’s ability to utilize oxygen.

Aerobic conditioning should be emphasized when the muscles tire before the oxygen runs out, and anaero-

bic conditioning should be emphasized when the

oxygen depth is exhausted before the muscles tire.

Since tennis is a game of both muscle strength and oxygen depth, proper training integrates both aerobic and anaerobic conditioning.

When you first start your conditioning program, work on aerobics as a base until your muscular system can endure long workouts. After five to seven weeks,

move toward anaerobic conditioning. Don’t let the time element put you off, since the quality of your life as well as the quality of your tennis is at stake.

One of the best forms of aerobic training is jogging. Unless you’ve worn blinders in bookstores lately, you probably already knew that! At any rate, jogging is a great way to build your cardiovascular and

muscular system. But, don’t expect to start out running a marathon! Begin with a short run with a goal of achieving a ten-minute-mile. If you’re healthy enough

to run (check with a doctor if you have any questions), such a goal is quite reasonable.

As you jog, run loosely and avoid clenching your fists and jaws and keep your spine relaxed. Run lightly with a heel to toe rocking motion. If possible, run on grassy areas rather than hard pavement to avoid

shocking your spine. For more than these brief tips, check out any of the popular literature on the subject.

For the sake of your tennis game, jogging four days a week is quite adequate. After five to seven weeks, you can start your anaerobic program; but, you must first build a muscular base.

Building oxygen depth can also be achieved best through the exercise of jogging. However, you might consider swimming or rope skipping. In anaerobic conditioning, you could also incorporate sprinting and/or running up stairs or hills into your program.

Distance and/or time span can best be determined by your own personal judgment.

Many joggers find that after they have run two or three miles they experience a euphoric feeling of lightness—an emotional and energy “‘high.’’ It’s as if

they could run all day. This peak experience has been described over and over again. It can be enhanced

through feelings and visual imagery.

Choreographing Your Movement— The Tennis Dance

If you watch tennis on television, you will note that in the slow motion playbacks, the pros seem to be dancing rather than playing a vigorous sport. There’s a simple reason for this. Tennis is actually nothing more than a dance—and, a very simple dance at that.

However, the movements are so critical to successful play, that in every workshop I spend at least a two

hour session or 1/4 of my time teaching them! I start out showing students strokes, but tossing balls for

specific returns is not the name of the game. That’s why I’ve been so concerned with endurance, fluidity and now with movement.

You can cover the entire baseline court in three steps, providing you are standing at the baseline area. The choreography takes you from one ready position to another. To do this all-important three-step dance,

start in the ready position by facing the net and bending your knees. For a forehand drive, move right/step, right/turn, left/step—a slide-one-two movement. To do this correctly, shuffle (don’t lift) your feet and click your heels—right/step (click), right/turn (the foot

turns and heels click) and take your left step towards the ball. On the backhand, the three-step shuffle takes you to the left side of the court. Slide left (click), left

turn (heels touch), right step. Actually, if you’re

standing in the center of the baseline, by means of this one shuffle movement you can hit any ball that’s on the court.

Without knowledge of this shuffle dance, players run to hit a ball, but after impact are off balance and

not ready for a split-second move to the return. What makes the dance so efficient is that (1) the turn gets the racquet back early and (2) you finish the movement with the back toe still on the ground for a

balanced step in any direction. The constant motion of

the dance not only keeps you from fighting the trap of inertia on court but also the one of hitting the ball and

stopping to see if it goes in. If you do stop, you'll end up running to compensate and your power posture is lost. You never want to lose your natural rhythm or

momentum. All you have to do is learn this motion and you can start playing like a champion! If your living (or any) room is big enough, practice the forehand

and backhand dance there. You could play a waltz and

move to the triple-time beat. It’s good exercise and great fun, and unless you’re in a workshop or clinic

setting, it’s rather nice to practice to music. But, I can’t stress enough—however you practice, PRAC- TICE! The dance gives you confidence that you can get to any ball. So practice it with the confidence that

it will enable you to reach any shot—that it is much

more efficient than running after balls.

Another Helpful Image— Moving Forward to the Net

While the tennis ‘‘dance’’ keeps the body rhythmically in motion, I like to use the image of a tank for further refinement of fluidity and form. A tank is admittedly a strange image, but bear with me. Your approach shots will benefit. First, as you hit a short ball

from mid-court, follow the ball’s direction with your approach movement. Stay low as you move, and you will increase your reaction time and avoid the awkwardness of raising and lowering your body as well.

Imagine that you are a tank as you move. Stay low to the ground. Be aggressively determined, and keep your ‘‘firing mechanism”’ (tennis racquet) in front of you ready for action. The three-step dance is “mellow,” but the tank is a ‘“‘no fooling around” hurry-up movement to get to the ball efficiently and

fast.

Quieting those Doubt-Producing Commentaries

Kick the judging and analyzing habit! Give the left hemisphere of your brain a rest. It should not in-

terfere with the images that are being produced from the right hemisphere. As you run for distance and you

“‘dance’’ on court, give your left hemisphere an object for its concentration. For example, when you jog

select a tree in the distance or focus on the wind. If your mind continues to chatter with too many dis-

quieting thoughts, place your concentration on your

breath. The rhythm of your inhaling and exhaling is a

constant part of you and thus an ideal focus for your attention. What you are doing here is essentially entertaining the left hemisphere of your brain, so it will be too busy to sabotage your best efforts with

doubts. This is what happens in Tennis Centering. There just isn’t room for comments like, ‘‘I can’t play

so aggressively. I’m a woman.” For any peak performance you want to short-circuit the self-image, ‘‘I can’t! I never have before!”’ barrier. Once you have

quieted your mind, you can focus all your energy on the here and now where the past is irrelevant, and from this point on, anything is possible.

e 1) The Wrist Turnover—One of the biggest problems many people have is hitting the ball too far back behind them—not hitting a contact point out in

front. In an attempt to compensate, they then turn their wrist over. This is a major problem because any time you start to turn your wrist over, the ball is not going to be forced back down on court. Rather the wrist turnover makes the ball go out even farther, and

that can be annoying—to say the least.

Correction—To break the wrist turnover problem on the forehand, (1) as the ball comes, point to it with your left hand; and, (2) after you have completed the

forehand stroke, use the left hand to catch the racquet at eye level.

To break the wrist turnover problem on the backhand, you can use the sword image. When you ‘‘draw the sword”’ make sure you keep it at your assailant. Another correction is to take the tip of your racquet and point it to the ball after impact.

e 2) Improper Contact Points—The ideal contact point on the groundstroke is out in front of your body

at waist level. The backhand contact point is farther

out in front of the body than the forehand contact point. Many players, however, hit the ball behind their body and consequently sacrifice balance, power, and control.

The three major causes of a behind-the-body contact point are: (A) late racquet back, (B) poor foot placement, and (C) lack of concentration.

Corrections—Problem A: Establish how quickly you are currently taking your racquet back. You

should have your racquet back by the time the ball

crosses the net or at least by the time the ball bounces on your side of the court.

Problem B: Always attempt to plant your back foot, and then step into the ball by placing your inside foot towards the net pole. If you are currently stepping too far behind you, rather than towards the net

pole, it will force you to contact the ball late and behind you.

Problem C: When the ball crosses the net, use any of the suggested concentration techniques described in Chapter Five. Once you hit the ball, keep your eyes

on the contact point for a second or two. This visual technique will make you aware of your typical contact point to discover whether impact is behind you. Once

awareness is established, adjustments come with

ease.

That toe needs to stay down. Right? Right!

Correction—To correct the balance distribution problem, simply ask yourself, “‘Am I keeping my back

toe on the ground?”’ As you plant your back foot and complete your stroke, ask, ‘‘Am I taking my back foot

off the ground?’’ Again, awareness is the major component of problem correction. Once you become aware

of the problem, you can normally correct it in a few

shots.

If you are just slightly off, the ball wili hit the net or carry out of the court.

Correction—To correct this problem, visualize that you are tossing a bucket of water over the net.

Next, watch the ball in its flight pattern. Does the ball arch over the net? Once you develop an arch by hitting low to high, the high-risk slice shot will be corrected.

stuck on your strings for a brief moment.

PART | THE ART AND CRAFT OF PLAY

Strategy is a game of knowing what to do and when to do it. Therefore, it is a process which involves thought analysis. Surprise—finally, there is a place to really use the left brain’s logical analysis and sequential planning ability.

The left brain strategy game can be mastered by following a ten rule plan.

1. Hit with accuracy—This can be as simple as hitting down-the-middle.

2. Keep the ball in play—Tennis is a game of errors. The winner of most matches is the one who makes the least number of errors. Let your opponent take the risk. Chances are that

his gamble will fail. 3. Hit high percentage shots—A down-themiddle shot is the highest percentage shot. 4. Go deep to your opponent’s backhand every chance you get—This will keep your opponent in a defensive posture. 5. Hit with depth—Keep the ball within three to six feet of the baseline. Topspin is a major factor in achieving depth. 6. Hit with pace—Hit the ball with pace only when you have mastered the art of control and moderate variation. 7. Hit with moderate variation—The idea of variation is over-emphasized. For moderate variation, simply move your target area or

change your spin. 8. Relax—You can hit with accuracy, depth, pace, and variation when you relax. 9. Concentrate—Watch the ball. Preoccupy the rational mind in the ways we have shown. 10. Get to the net as soon as possible—The player at the net always has the advantage; he has a better angle on the ball, and he has the opportunity to keep his opponent in a defensive posture.

The four major ingredients of the intellectual game are accuracy, pace, depth, and variation. Accuracy requires direction control, and pace requires

hitting a moderate to hard stroke. Depth requires keeping the ball deep, and variation requires advanced

strokes of chips, underspins (drop shots), and kick-

serves.

Point-To-Point Sequential Playing Strategies

The Frequent Short Ball Sequence—Perhaps the most effective strategy technique available to players at all levels is the short ball approach sequence. To employ the short ball sequence, hit all of your ground-

strokes deep down the middle, and wait until your opponent hits you a ball which bounces inside the service line. When this happens, approach the ball and hit a high-to-low chip shot deep to your opponent’s backhand. If you feel you can’t execute the high-tolow chip shot, then come in by hitting a punching motion deep to your opponent’s backhand. After you hit the approach shot, proceed to the net in the same

direction that your approach shot is traveling. Follow the direction of the ball even if it doesn’t go to your opponent’s backhand. Once you arrive at the net,

plant both feet and punch your volley to an area of the court which is furthest away from your opponent.

The Drop Shot Sequence—For variety you may wish to try a drop shot sequence. To hit the drop shot you should stroke the ball in a chipping motion. Contact with the ball is just under the middle.

To practice the drop shot sequence, hit most of your balls deep down the middle until your opponent

hits you a fairly short ball. Approach the short ball.

Hit your drop shot, and move quickly to the net. Approaching the net after you hit the drop shot will dramatically reduce the angles and the possible number of shots available for your opponent’s return.

For this reason, I suggest that you never try to hit your drop shot and stay back. You usually have for-

ward momentum which would need to be reversed, and then you will be off balance. Also, you leave your opponent at the net in command with a larger number of shots to work with. I also suggest that you avoid hitting the drop shot from your baseline because of the low percentage involved in this shot.

The Drop Shot/Lob Sequence—In some play situations, you may choose to hit a short ball and bring your opponent into the net while you wait at the baseline for his return. When your opponent approaches the net, hit your next shot as a lob. This

technique is especially effective when you wish to tire your opponent.

The Serve and Volley Sequence— Approaching the net quickly serves as an element of surprise and can end a point in a hurry. To get to the net quickly, you may wish to practice the serve and volley sequence.

For maximum effect, start with a 3/4 speed serve. This will allow you enough time to get to mid-court before you have to hit the return. As you serve and approach the net, try to get at least a foot inside the service line. Just before you hit the volley, plant both feet for balance. After you hit your volley, proceed to the net in the same direction that you hit the ball.

The Visual Court—Recently I was working with Judy Dixon, former Yale coach, and she provided me with some key visual frames of reference for strategy

situations. The following imagery process is paraphrased from her teachings.

To understand the court from a visual frame of reference, divide it into three parts. Part I should be

divided from the net to the service line. Part II should be divided from the service line to approximately five

feet beyond the service line. Part III encompasses the area from five feet beyond the service line to the baseline.

This three-part court is viewed as a traffic signal. The first part of the court is a green light area. The

second part is a yellow light area, and the third part is a red light area.

As you begin to play, keep in mind the connection between the above demarcations and the three traffic lights. If the ball bounces in the green light area, react

as you would if you were approaching a green light in your car. Put your body “‘in drive’”’ and proceed forward to the net. There’s no need to pause or stop. If the ball bounces in the yellow light area, again react as you would if you approached a yellow traffic signal. If you arrive early, you will probably continue on through. If you arrive late, you will use caution and

avoid proceeding forward. If the ball bounces in the red light area, stay back and don’t even consider proceeding forward.

Imagery Theory for Strategy—To apply the traffic signal image to play situations follow these guidelines:

1. When the ball bounces in the green light area, hit most of your approach shots deep to your opponent’s backhand and proceed to the net. 2. When balls are bouncing in your red light area, stay back and return most of your balls deep down-the-middle.

To further extend your visual imagery, imagine that you are driving a tank in traffic (see page 20). When the ball bounces in the green light area, proceed

forward like a tank—low and aggressive with your racquet out in front. Once your opponent makes contact with the ball, plant your feet for balance and then punch the ball aggressively.

One of the questions I am often asked is ‘‘How can you work on strategy and still concentrate?” I’m

the first to agree that though much has been written on concentration, little has been written on the integration of concentration techniques with actual

strategic play situations.

To understand how concentration lends itself to strategy, we must first address the major premise of the entire strategy process: keep the ball in the court!

It is also important to note what may seem even more obvious: keep the ball in the court one more time than your opponent. Now take off from the latter rule with

an interesting statistic involving the sixth shot.

The Sixth Shot Rule—To the surprise of most players, there is strong evidence to indicate that the ball almost never crosses over the net more than six

times in one rally. This is true both at beginner and advanced levels. It means that if you are able to hit three shots over the net, you normally win the point.

The reason that players seldom have a rally over six shots can be attributed to (1) lack of patience in players to hit the high percentage balls deep down-the-

middle (as opposed to flashy low percentage shots); (2) boredom of hitting fifteen or twenty balls while you wait for an opening; (3) and finally, panic in players

when it’s time to win the point with a put-away shot.

To handle boredom and to expand patience is the real value of concentration and can therefore be seen

as the missing ingredient in sound strategy.

Patience, Boredom and Concentration—To maintain high percentage strategy, it is important to hit the ball deep down-the-middle. Unfortunately, most players get bored with this regime and normally at-

tempt high risk shots as an antidote to boredom. If, however, you could amuse yourself on the baseline and wait for an opening, the probability of hitting high percentage shots increases as does the probability of your winning. To amuse yourself for three shots

should be easy if you practice the recommendations found in Chapters IV and V. For example, using the

musical mantra ‘‘Ba Dum,”’ or watching for the arch in the flight pattern of the ball.

Employ your favorite concentration techniques for six shots and see what happens. I could almost bet that within six shots your opponent will hit you a

short ball or make some other mistake. Then, you can approach the net with your approach volley sequence.

travel near the middle of the court and also force his opponents to hit their return wide to his partner’s

forehand. Leaving the alley open, also may force the opponent to consider going down-the-alley—a far

lower percentage shot for him than hitting crosscourt.

e The service receiver’s position involves moving in relationship to the server. If he is standing wide,

the receiver should move slightly to the right. If the

server is tucked in towards the middle of the court, then it is a good idea to move slightly to the left.

e The receiver’s partner should stand slightly in front of the service line. This is strategic for that quick, first step to get to the net. This is the basis of doubles strategy. You want to get to the net as quickly as possible! You don’t want to retreat—one of the most troubling impulses of intermediate players.

When you occupy this position, you should always think, ‘‘Move forwards! Don’t retreat!’’ Normally, the only time you should move backwards is when you see your opponent assuming an overhead stroke position.

Approaching the Net—There are two ways to play tennis: (1) offensively and (2) defensively. I favor tak-

ing an offensive posture (with the capability to play

defensively when necessary). Approaching the net often marks taking the offensive. And, it’s a good

team strategy, for winning at doubles requires seizing

the net! Then, the opposition has but three alternatives: (1) either hit right to you, (2) hit a low percentage down-the-line shot, or (3) hit a low percentage de-

fensive lob shot. If you get to the net first, odds are that you will win eighty per cent of your points.

But don’t let the odds trip you up. It’s important to know exactly when to take the net. Obviously, you can’t always approach with authority. Here are some rules for approaching:

¢ Approach when you receive a “green light”

short ball.

e Approach off of your first serve, even when

you're a lower intermediate player.

e Approach when you receive a weak second

serve.

e Approach if you hit deep to your opponents’

backhands.

e Approach when your opponents have to return

e Approach any time your opponents are in trou-

It is also important to understand the process involved in approaching the net. When you approach, keep these suggestions in mind:

e Follow the direction of the ball’s flight.

Poaching—When the net player attempts to head off the ball by moving laterally, parallel to the net, it is called ‘‘poaching.”” The purpose is for surprise and variation. Poaching is very effective for offensive

play; however, it can get you in serious trouble if you

don’t know the guidelines.

1) Poach for variety—every six shots is a good rule of thumb. 2) Once you poach, you must move to the other side of the court. Always clear out of the middle. Otherwise, you leave your partner on the baseline, second guessing whether he or she

should move left or right. 3) Poach most often on weak receivers. 4) Don’t poach on second serves.

The Middle-Low Shot—In doubles play, balls should be directed to the middle of the court rather than to the alley. There are two good reasons for this practice. First, the net is lower in the middle, and sec-

ond, your opponents may end up guessing who should

take the ball.

Avoid going for the alleys unless you are an expert. Most intermediates are only successful at this

shot twenty per cent of the time. You may wish to try the alley shot for variation, but don’t depend on it. For this reason, you should also pay more defensive attention to your own middle than to your alleys. Intermediates aren’t consistent on alley shots.

Always hit your ground shots low in doubles. As I said above, the key in doubles is to seize the net. If you hit a high shot to an opponent at the net, you are

finished. The low shot will more often than not force your opponent to hit below the net on his volleys, and it will force the opponent who is approaching to hit low to high. When your opponent hits a high ball to you, you gain angle for your volleys and normally win

the point.

Covering the Down-the-Middle Shot—After the point starts, most knowledgeable players will direct

the ball down the middle of the court because of the

favorable odds. To cover this shot, I suggest that the player closest to the net take the ball. He has the best

angles and the best percentage chance of hitting the ball. The person closest to the net should always take

the down-the-middle ball even when s/he is the weakest player.

Lobbing—Since it is predictable that most good doubles teams will be trying to seize the net, it is important to develop a strong defensive tactic—and the

most effective defense is the lob.

1) Lob offensively when a player crowds the net. 2) Lob with topspin. It gives you more time. 3) Lob often when your opponents seize the net.

4) Lob deep. 5) Lob for variety. 6) Most importantly, lob when you get in trouble. It will give you time to regroup.

Who Covers the Lob—Since the game of doubles is a game of keeping players off the net, you will soon

find that your opponents will use the lobbing tactic against you. When you're on the receiving end of the lob, I suggest the following procedures:

1) If you are lobbed on your side of the court and you see that the ball will land short of the ser-

vice line, hit your own shot. 2) If the lob lands beyond the service line on your side of the court, I suggest letting your partner take the shot. Allowing your partner to take the shot is the most effective move because

s/he can get to the shot quicker and can avoid the balance problems that you will experience if you try to run backwards to get your own

shot. For optimum efficiency, you should move back to the baseline as well, for you will now be in a defensive posture. To move back with your partner most effectively, I suggest using an X type formation.

Keep in mind that high lobs are best hit when they are first allowed to bounce. Trying to hit a high lob in the air tends to be a low percentage shot for all but top-rated players. One final suggestion is to let the lob bounce when you are playing at night since artificial lighting can play tricks with your depth perception.

Moving Together—One of the most important rules in doubles is to move with your partner. It can be costly if you don’t. Then you are likely to leave large gaps in your coverage of the court area which

can easily be hit into by your opponents.

Ideally, you should always be side-by-side and approximately ten to fifteen feet apart. The ten to fifteen foot concept is important—both at the baseline and at the net. If the ball is hit to the right back court, you and your partner should both move toward the

ball. If the ball is hit to the right while you are at the net, you should move to the ball together keeping a

distance of ten to fifteen feet apart. If the ball is hit to your left while you are at the net, you should again

move together. If the ball is lobbed while you and your partner are at the net both of you should move back to cover the shot.

Once you begin to move together, you will find that you seldom have the time to stand around wishing for a shot.

Ideal Positioning—The most effective positioning is when both you and your partner are at the net. Second in effectiveness is when you and your partner are both back at the baseline. The worst position that you can be in is when one of you is at the net and the other is at the baseline. Remember that you should always move together as a team in a highly coordinated

fashion. Such teamwork will win you matches that you never believed were possible.

Of course, there are times when rules are there to be broken. One of these times is when you have reason to surprise your opponents.

The tandem position involves the repositioning of the net man during the serve. In the tandem position, the net man moves to midcourt before the serve (most often on or slightly to the side of the midcourt line). The tandem position should be used only when you are behind or when you have lost numerous points in a row. The purpose is to confuse your opponents and to

short-circuit the effective groove they have developed.

A Summation of Rules for Doubles Strategy

For the most effective results in your doubles play, I suggest the following:

General Rules for Both Singles and Doubles

Whether you play as a team or as an individual, accuracy and depth are vital ingredients of winning play. Therefore, don’t attempt difficult shots if you

lack the accuracy to do so. Place most of your shots

deep, near the baseline and hit with moderate power (pace) but avoid the kill-the-ball syndrome.

Constructing Social Barriers—In the early chapters, we discussed the social game of tennis with emphasis on overcoming such emotional barriers as low self-image, past failures, lack of confidence, fear

and tension. Let’s turn that approach around! What barriers can you construct to force your opponent to lose the social game?

I offer the following section for players who compete with opponents of equal play levels. To work on

your opponents’ social weaknesses, I suggest a tempered, reserved, nonpredatory, game of social competition. In other words, play to your opponents’ weaknesses (all of them), but realize that all games have rules; don’t take it too far. Below I’ve listed some tactics for social competition.

1) When your opponent gets down on himself, note the causal factor and return to it often—i.e., ‘I hate myself when I miss overheads.’’ You should go to the overhead often. 2) When your opponent approaches the net—hit at his feet or lob. Soon he will lose confidence in

his approach shots. 3) When you play doubles—work on your opponent’s communication patterns. If your opponents begin to communicate as parent scold-

ing a child, work on shots which will trigger and reinforce such a communication—i.e., hit to the middle, and work on weaknesses. 4) When you warm up—take special care of each shot; hit your serves as impressively as possible. Work on easy shots such as the volley; to look good, hit all shots with precision; in this manner you will lower your opponents’ con-

fidence before the match. This tactic led me to

a new school record for my tennis team! 5) Practice your preparation program—yoga, breathing (impression management), etc., before you walk on court. Your opponent may think you have a secret weapon, which in effect, will lower his self-esteem. 6) Keep a positive self-image of yourself (even when behind). Your opponent will begin to wonder why you're so collected.

Constructing Physical Barriers—Your opponent’s physical game can be attacked most effectively by

wearing him out. Take him beyond his endurance threshold! Below are some suggestions.

1) Observe your opponent’s stamina level and work it to your advantage—if your opponent tires easily after a lob, lob him until he’s ex-

hausted.

2) Move your opponent often if he displays low oxygen depth—lob, then drop shot, then lob.

3) When your opponent tires, change courts in a hurry. Don’t give him time to regroup. 4) If you tire, learn to find acceptable stalls! I like to tie my shoe when I’m dragging. 5) Use the heat of the day to tire your opponent. 6) If your opponent tires easily—make him hit shots which tire him quickly (the serve and/or overhead). Finish your serve quickly.
