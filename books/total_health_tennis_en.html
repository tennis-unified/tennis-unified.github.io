WHEN ALL AGES MEET ON the common ground of acquired

skill, personal motivation differs visibly. A noncontact sport like

tennis (by focusing, goldfish bowl-style, on normally hidden

personal behaviors) triggers a full range of defensive explanations

in efforts to be a convenient criterion for sorting out players and

their primary motivations. For Gail Sheehy in Passages, age was

the prime determinate in all of life’s games. The fact is, however,

that self-segregation would always occur naturally in the tennis

setting, because both ability and social status seem to parallel

exactly where you are along life’s road. The only real exception to

this rule of thumb is in the context of serious competition where

results alone determine a player’s accessibility to the future. There

is always the child prodigy like Tracy Austin today, or Chris Evert

and Bjorn Borg a few years back.

Age categories then, while obvious and often convenient, are

ultimately only vehicles for dealing with different levels and types

of performance. When one matures ahead of expectancy, an

uncommon achievement is sometimes attributed to youth. Yet, this

same quality may elude the elderly. The fact is that both ends of the

continuum break down under expectation of age alone, and depend

most profoundly on the qualities of mind which influence an in-

dividual’s willingness to suffer pain or adversity as the price of accomplishment.

themselves and thereby approach adulthood with confidence.

as snugly as Gail Sheehy.

SOMETHING MORE THAN THE COUNTRY CLUB

History holds the game of lawn tennis to be an elitist sport

previously played in cable knit sweaters and long trousers on fine

green lawns and at clubs for only the most affluent. This history

only naturally has a certain romantic appeal, but the years have

changed that game and have brought it to the people. That is not to

assume, however, that there are no longer fine country clubs and

that there are no longer people who use the game most importantly

for the social enterprise and status that it brings. There are many clubs where the focus of a modern man’s or woman’s day is on

their luncheon, their Bloody Mary, after their tennis game, with the

game itself being only secondary. Social demands at many clubs

have made the tennis clothing business a multi-million-dollar af-

fair, for example. It is important to look the ‘‘proper’’ way on the

court, and while this is not to be belittled, since it is part of the

discipline and self-care interrelated with it all, it is sometimes easy

to lose the true point of performance, and that is what Total Health

Tennis is all about. For the social set, good health and skill

development were very often only an incidental by-product. For the

Total Health participant, looking well and caring for himself or

herself remain secondary (but still important) to the idea of overall

development. In its essence, Total Health Tennis questions

socialization for its own sake. It seeks not to abandon the grace and

beauty and history of the game, but it does transcend the

stigmatism and pressure of a purely social context, and it points towards the most personal development of the individual, by the

individual himself.

THE AGE OF ASSERTIVENESS—IN WHICH AGGRESSION

IS AS COMMON AN ERROR AS PASSIVITY

It is fair to say that we are living in an age of assertiveness, even

of brash competitiveness, as witnessed by the likes of Jimmy

Connors in the game of tennis. It is not uncommon to witness the

philosophy of ‘‘win at any cost,’’ with the old ethic of ‘‘how you

play the game’’ quietly being left behind. The fact that men and

women must be assertive in order to survive today is no more thana

fact of life. There is, however, a thin line between being con-

structively assertive and being out of control, and there are many

cases in which over-reaction and too much aggression are as much

in error as passivity. This is especially true for women today in their

efforts to realize themselves and the cause of feminine liberation.

Within this context, and this is true for both men and women, the

game of tennis, and Total Health as a program, offer an op-

portunity to practice in the search for balance in this idea. Over-

aggression and over-reaction, for example, in the game of tennis are not smart tennis. There is no reason to win points more

flamboyantly than is necessary, for flamboyance in and of itself is a

liability that can threaten the chance of winning or losing a point. The wise player of the mental game will always play his points to

the hilt but will win the points only as the flow of movement

demands. If an opponent has fallen on his face, there is no reason

to hit a one-hundred-mile-an-hour drive one inch from the sideline.

Extreme passivity is just as ineffective on the tennis court as

over-reaction. The fear of losing, the fear of failure, the anxiety of

choking—they can all induce a player to ‘‘push’’ the ball over the

net. There is a limit to the success any player will have when his fear

of error prevents and/or inhibits his attempts for constructive ball

placement.

The fact of life within the tennis experience is that heavy hitters

often beat pushers and pushers often beat heavy hitters—but

neither of them ever defeats the wise percentage player who has the

talents of both, but who implements them at the right time.

Within the exercise and running portions of the Total Health

Programs, these same extremes are most prevalent. There have

been many times where I have seen the macho male ego fail in its

attempt to run too fast too soon, or to do too many exercises with

too much weight. Similarly, I have often seen the self-limiting self —

of the female ego will itself short. Total Health Tennis is a medium

in which a person must conceptualize the reality of their own

potential. It is an exercise in finding reality and facing an objective

and systematic truth about ourself. Health and performance do not

come overnight. Through systematic methods, the training effect

of repetition and persistence will bring forth results that will change

the life of anyone who sees it through. The idea is like a good

swing, but every good swing needs a follow-through to keep the

ball in play.

For many adults, learning is a real problem because they cannot

stand the thought of ‘‘not knowing it.’? Within the context of Total

Health, however, overcoming weaknesses, the act of ‘‘becoming,”’

is put in a setting in which other strengths can be stressed at the

same time deficiencies are corrected. This makes the learning

environment very secure and always reinforcing.

Within this context, we are once again thrust into that

microcosm of life where court experience is a great teacher. There

are two main thresholds with which the Total Health participant

must come face to face. They are facts of life that we all encounter if we take personal initiative in our lives. The unavoidable

thresholds are (1) pain and (2) frustration. Both must be dealt with,

and they must be conquered. It is not easy, for example, to begin a

skill development as intricate as the game of tennis if you have not

played in twenty years, or perhaps never before. After all, that

‘*bloody ball’’ is moving, and it is being hit away from you. That

net is three feet high in the center but three-feet-six inches at the

side. Those lines—the dimensions of the court, seventy-eight feet

by thirty-six feet, they are such an imposing obstacle—but, on the

other hand, they are such a.challenge. Within the context of any

worthwhile challenge, every individual must learn to deal with the

frustration of being a beginner. The fact of life is that beginners

must begin. And in tennis the grace of the game, especially when

judged by a television performance, appears infinitely easier than

the way it really is. Thus, the beginning player who is attracted to

Total Health Tennis may see the validity of the idea, but may still

find unavoidable the pain and reality of a process which will not

replace his awkwardness overnight with the grace of the performing

professional.

For a person who has ignored any systematic maintenance of his

health through the years, or the person who may be overweight, or

for the person who has simply been led away from physical activity

through the technology of our age, the process of ‘‘getting in

shape’’ is a painful one. There is no getting around it. It often

seems as though every fiber and every muscle in your body is

aching. It takes courage to stick it out and to come back because of

an idea, even when that compelling idea is your own health. For

that, a bit of pain can become suddenly worthwhile. There are the

smokers who cough their way around my tennis courts as they do

their first laps, yet they are the same beautiful people who sixteen

weeks later are running two miles a day with clear eyes and more

energy than they have had in years. Those are the people who faced

the thresholds of pain and of frustration, and they faced them not only as they ‘‘got in shape,’’ but during the skill development of controlling the flight of that elusive tennis ball. The key word to

facing these difficult obstacles is courage. It takes courage to work

on yourself, and it takes facing a bit of reality to know that no one

task for us? A marshmallow might roll over and say, ‘‘Save me,

please.’’ A person of courage will be so busy working on the prob-

him.

PRACTICE IN THE REGULATED LIFE

We are living in a competitive age, an age that thrives on results

and getting the job done. Those persons most able to get their jobs

done are also the ones who know and care about themselves. They

are persons who regulate their own lives systematically, and that is

why good health and appearance is all part of the same productive

ball game. In the 1960s, it was fashionable to grow a beard, as a

part of the ‘‘uniform’’ of the hippy. Hippies were supposed to be

smart, sort of intellectual, and to have access to some special truth

that was somehow hidden from the rest of the world. The fad

became ‘‘reaction in opposite.’’ Because they cared, they took on

the appearance of not caring at all. The movement is now over, its

message having been delivered. Similarly, overt materialism and

spiffy Wall Street clothes have lost any implied social message.

Now a wide social spectrum agrees on the value of a regulated and

healthful life without a lot of extremes. The most productive people

are often those who value themselves. They may wear a flannel

shirt and a pair of Levis as a part of enjoying a Colorado

wilderness ‘‘high,’’ but the hidden story is likely to be that they

have also maintained the health of their body very systematically

throughout the years. You can recognize the color in a person’s

face, and whether their eyes shine as they smile. It is not hard to

detect the person who is in control of himself. Those seeking op-

portunity today should keep this obvious fact of life in mind, for

those in positions to employ or reward will certainly not overlook

it. An individual is not often given responsibilities over others when

it is clear that he or she may not yet have accepted proper

responsibility for himself or herself. An individual who has ac-

cepted responsibility for himself will be conscious of his weight, his

diet, and he will take a preventive maintenance approach to his

continued well-being. Such people are properly the leaders of this age of assertiveness and competition, and they will remain easy to

recognize. They are our renaissance people.

Visualization and Tennis Technique

VISUALIZATION IS A personal preview of the best and the

possible. Its subject matter is necessarily abstract but in its ap-

plication it becomes objective reality. There are many forms of

visualization and they have been present since the Hermetic

philosophy of Egypt three thousand years before Christ. Some are

deeply meditative in nature and are commonly associated with

Eastern religious thought, while others have a more practical ap-

plication to everyday life. In all cases, however, the subject matter

remains abstract and especially ambiguous to Western man. The

reason is the effect of science and technology on our culture, par-

ticularly its commitment to problem-solving methods that demand

logical and concrete proof. Scientific methods give the impression of being antithetical to any area of study so securely cradled in the

sanctuary of the human mind as visualization; yet, ways are being

found to take advantage of both of these powerful styles of mind.

The idea that a particular mental image can bring forth responses

in the real world is not new. The only thing that is really new is the

vantage point from which we now approach an area of study that

has intrigued man through the ages. Philosophers and priests have

historically used visualization as a tool for growth and rebirth.

‘‘Beginning with Hermes [the Egyptian Hermes Trismegistres], a

spiritual philosophy was born based on primacy of mind, as op-

posed to primacy of matter.’’?' Even as long ago as this, man was

aware of the effect images held in the mind had on their physical

universe. We see things not so much as they are but as we are;

thereafter, we bend reality to fit our perception of it.

It is hard to deny the credibility of parts of Alvin Toffler’s book

Future Shock because we are all living with the reality of the

technological pace we ourselves have set. Essentially, it would now

appear that technology is controlling us, that it is harder than ever

to get away, and that it is ever so difficult to put the transmission of

life into neutral—even if it be for only a few moments. It is for this

reason that many people today are seeking to reestablish a oneness

away from the technology around them. They want a new orien-

tation with the original ‘‘Mother.’’ For example, more people are

seeking the wilderness of Colorado every day, whether it be to

navigate the soft powder on skis, to explore the wilds through

backpacking, or to feel the rush of cold mountain water in a canoe.

Modern man seems to be ironically trying to reestablish the same

kind of unity between the physical and spiritual universe that the

“‘noble savage’’ of American folklore held years ago.

Weare people who live on the roof of the world; we are the sons of Father Sun, and with our religion we daily help our father go across

the sky. We do this not only for ourselves, but for the whole world. If we were to cease practicing our religion, in ten years the sun would no longer rise. Then it would be night forever.”?

Carl Jung’s response is to pose the dilemma of the less-contented

intellectual who knows that: ‘‘Knowledge does not enrich us, it

removes us more and more from the mythic world in which we were

once at home by right of birth.’’??

THE INVERSE RELATIONSHIP OF VISUALIZATION WITH

We cannot escape the use of words to think with whether we are

cursing ourselves on a tennis court, debating our success or failure

in a committee meeting, or admiring the sexual overtones of an

attractive mate while we should be working. We congratulate and

we scold ourselves silently through the vehicle of language. It is a

part of us; it extends us. Yet, historically, the use and development

of visualization can be documented to have occurred in an inverse

relation to the development of language, particularly its written

form. Initially, as in the case of hieroglyphics, language was based

more purely on images. As words appeared which were more ab-

stract than simple icons, attempts were still made to evoke imagi-

Unfortunately, the continued evolution of the written word dra-

matically increased the distance between what the human eye saw

and the meaning of the symbol. Eventually, speakers learned to

deliberately establish distance between themselves and their own

experience. The perceptions of others were likewise externalized.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this; however, there is no

denying the negative effect it has had on man’s ability to relate closely to the essence of his world. The use of language to system-

atically categorize life’s experiences does not have to negate sen-

sation-sharing experiences, but it does tend to reduce their credi-

bility. What literate man now faces is the necessity of breaking

through a conditioned inclination to enclose life in labels and

abstractions and the vehicle which seems best suited to this purpose

is ‘‘visualization.’’ This search for unity with experience can take

place through meditation, Yoga exercise, or any experience toward

which one chooses to focus the visionary capacity of his mind. Our

particular target area, of course, is our own health and the skill

development required to master the game of tennis.

VISUALIZING THE TENNIS ENVIRONMENT

If a person is to play a truly thoughtful and feeling game of

tennis, he must establish a personal awareness of the environment

he seeks to control. He must, of course, be aware of his own body

in a very personal way, but he must also feel an intimate knowledge

of the tools he uses as well as the court on which he plays. The

following guidelines are but the personal thoughts of this author

about the kinds of relationships that can be established on the court

through the vehicle of visualization.

The Racquet. Many people have a very detached relationship with

their tennis racquet. It is not uncommon to see them thrown into

the trunk of the car, left in the dampness of the garage, or even

thrown face down in anger during the act of playing the game. To

persons of this mind, their racquet is no more than a replaceable

tool. There is no personal relationship between themselves and

their racquet.

I make special efforts to really know my tennis racquet since I

consider it to be an extension of myself. In the act of playing the

game, it is important to feel the sensation of the ball on the strings.

This must proceed through the shot to the racquet, to the grip, and

finally into your hand. I find myself very often holding my racquet

at home while carrying on a totally unrelated conversation. I can

unconsciously hold the grip, and I feel as though I really know the

racquet as a part of myself, as a true extension of my arm.

Another footnote concerning my racquet concerns the fact that I

enjoy stringing my own racquet. Naturally, most people in the

world have neither the ability nor equipment to do this and I

mention it only because it is another means for me personally to

feel a oneness with my own racquet. There is something nice about

the feel of the unstrung gut and the knowledge that its uncreased

weave was put there under my own direction. There is some

considerable art in stringing a tennis racquet, and it is one that

affords a special pleasure to me as I prepare a racquet for my own use.

The Ball. A person does not have to be walking around in a daze to

enjoy some of the personal insights which contribute to a feeling of

oneness with the game. With respect to the ball, as previously noted

with the racquet, detailed familiarity is helpful. For example, the

tennis ball has seams, and for the observant player it will be these

same seams that will catch his eye as the ball approaches. Tim

Gallwey’s great prescription in his book, The Inner Game of

Tennis, was to ‘‘watch the seams of the ball.’’ I certainly feel that

this is a good idea and I recommend that every thoughtful student

become aware of what the seams look and feel like. The intensity of

concentration is, of course, what is at stake. Furthermore, the

tennis ball has a certain texture that changes with use and it is good to have a conscious awareness of exactly how much fuzz is or is not

left on the ball you are about to use. Its speed and bounce vary

accordingly. If you are using new balls, I recommend that you

pause to enjoy opening the new can, hearing the pressure of the can

as it is released, and detecting that unique smell which brings back

childhood memories of a game that is never fresher or newer than

on those rare occasions when new balls can be afforded.

The Court. The court, delineated by its lines of seventy-eight by

thirty-six feet, has an illusive quality that has challenged and stood

in defiance of even the very best. Those measurements are always

there, and they have remained the same as long as the game has

been played. A twilight walk on a tennis court with no one else

around can be a nostalgic experience for the sensitive player who

identifies with his surroundings. There is something especially

luring about those dimensions (in spite of their apparent objective

neutrality) and it is important to feel a closeness with them. You

should establish a love affair with the challenge they represent.

That challenge is a measure of the reach of man, not unlike the

magic four-minute mile for the runner. It is important to feel ‘‘in

tune’’ with the environment of the court and to be thoroughly

familiar with all of its parts, including the height of the net and the

very texture of its surface. It is an interesting experience to view, silently and alone as with the twilight walk just noted, the imposing

height of the net from the perspective of the other end of the day,

the early dawn as the sun comes up, with the gentle breeze in the

air. Anticipation of the day’s challenges can make this perspective

an unmatched experience for the inspired purist of the game. Those

lines and all of the court’s composite challenge will remain a cold

and unyielding standard. It is the human variable that changes.

Man himself provides all the highs and lows of the tennis drama,

his personal limits ultimately becoming the limits of the game.

VISUALIZING SPECIFIC TENNIS TECHNIQUES

The Ready Position. The ready position is just what its name

implies. It is a position of the body that stands at maximum

readiness for movement. Movement is the essence of tennis

inasmuch as the most obvious fact of all is that someone is hitting

the ball away from you for your impairment. To solve this problem

one must master the concept and reality of adaption through

movement. The tennis point is an ever-flowing act of preparation,

and central to this is the position in which one holds his racquet

between each shot. It is this position that is commonly known as

The ready position is obtained by facing the net and by mentally

readying oneself for potential action on either side of the body. The

racquet should be cradled at the throat in the left hand (assuming

one is right-handed) and it should be held lightly there. Persons

who clutch their racquet or grab it at the throat are always more

frantic in the tennis experience than those who carry it softly. This

is true of the right hand as well, as it holds the grip of the shot. One

should never let the pressure to perform affect the rigidity of the

body. It is not necessary to grip the racquet tightly except as the

ball contacts the racquet. It is important to remember that the game

is an experience in fluidity, and this can never be achieved if the

muscle tone on either the right or left arm is rigid. The goal of the

inspired player is to find and experience the art of the game, and

this is done in the same way that the reed bows to the forces of the

wind—naturally. The artful tennis experience can never be a brittle,

segmented performance.

Because being ready requires a readiness not only to stroke but to

move, there are specific visualization techniques that are helpful

for one’s legs and feet as well. It is important, for example, not to

be rooted to the court with the weight of your body on your heels.

The trunk of your body should be slightly curled forward at the

waist, and the weight distributed on the insides of your feet with

your knees bent. I find it particularly beneficial to sway slightly

from side to side as I wait for the initial start of the point in order to

keep the weight of my body from being isolated. The sway of-gentle

movement and light touch on the throat of my racquet with my left

hand prepare me for the smoothness of movement that is to come. I become at one with the movement of the ball as it ap-

proaches, and I do not wait until it bounces to frantically chase it

down. There is a flow present with every tennis exchange and I always try to become a part of that most beautiful movement. It is

an act of control and much of one’s success in blending with it lies

in adequate visualization of the true concept of ‘‘being ready.’’

The Grip. How a person holds his tennis racquet is of utmost

importance. It is, however, an experience that must be ‘‘felt,’’

because the tennis point requires that a number of shots be hit ina

row. This is opposed, of course, to a more segmented experience

such as golf, where a person is able to deliberate longer for each

specific shot hit. Once the ball is in play, there is never time to look

down and check one’s grip. The golfer, on the other hand, can

preoccupy himself to the point of obsession with how he holds his

club. The tennis player must feel his grips, and how vividly he can

do this will depend a great deal on his ability to visualize the shape

of the racquet grip in his hand.

Proper grips are absolutely critical to effective stroking because

they directly determine the angle of the racquet face as it contacts

the ball. Furthermore, when done properly, they serve to align the

wrist in a supportive position behind the racquet. This firmness of

the wrist is what allows the player to experience the sensation of

oneness between his racquet and his body.

The eastern forehand grip is most commonly attained by shaking

hands with the racquet. This common procedure will align the ‘‘V”’

in your hand between the thumb and first finger on top of the slanted edge of your racquet on the right side (for right-handed

players). It should be possible to have a very specific awareness of

not only the ‘‘V”’ in your hand as it rests on that bevel, but of the

creases in your hand and how they fit into position on your grip. Also, it is important that the first finger be slightly separated from

the other fingers of the hand in such a way as to give a wider grip

and therefore more control over the racquet and the stroking

process. Hitting from a closed fist, which is a common inclination

of beginners, always induces a more wristy ‘‘slapping’’ effect into

the ball.

The eastern backhand grip for the right-handed player is but a

quarter of a turn to the left edge of the racquet. This turn is not a

time-consuming obstacle as many novices like to think, because it is

a process that is always done at the same time the player does

something else. It takes only a fraction of a second, and the precise

process requires that this slight change in grip be done by turning

the throat of the racquet with the left hand. The beginning player

who has not mastered the ready position will often try and flip the

racquet full circle when changing grips, and it is for this reason that

it will go way too far around and miss the proper grip altogether.

The act of changing grips is always most effectively done by gently

turning the racquet with the fingers of the left hand and by doing it

only a quarter of a turn—enough to keep the angle of the racquet

face perpendicular to the ground at impact, and enough to set the

wrist firmly behind the impact of the shot.

The Volley. The volley in tennis is the name of the shot contacted in

the air before the ball bounces, and this is the first tennis shot I

teach my students. This is where visualization begins. It is a very

controlled environment, not only for the student to first begin

experiencing the coordination of movement in their hands and feet,

but it is an excellent place to begin their awareness of the flight of

the ball. Visualization of the ball as it approaches is quite important. The ball is a spear, like a vapor trail, that is passing

through space and it occupies a specific plane (i.e., height and arc)

as it approaches. One should be supersensitive to that flight and to

its velocity as it passes through space towards your racquet. It is

always a player’s job, of course, to guide the center of his racquet

onto this same plane. This is why precise recognition of the plane is

so vitally important.

When volleying, it is always to one’s advantage to get fairly close

to the net. The best position is approximately one-third of the way

from the net to the service line. Any farther back than this and one

faces the liability of having to ‘‘volley up,’’ and this is a very

difficult proposition for even the best of players. Our goal should

always be to move the ball in such a way as to ‘‘volley down.’’

There are several facts of life that must be dealt with when en-

countering a ball from a short distance. Being so close to the net,

one has about half as much time to respond. It is for this reason

that a person’s actual swing into the ball must be comparatively

short, and the swift body movement to reach the ball must be the

most efficient possible. This shortness of swing is referred to by

many as blocking the ball or punching it. In essence, it means no

backswing and no follow-through. As far as footwork is con-

cerned, moving efficiently necessitates always stepping across. This

single fact will allow the player to cover almost twice as much

ground and is particularly important in a shot where the player is

forced to cover the same width of the court but in about half the time.

The Forehand Volley. The forehand volley is most often the first

tennis shot learned by beginning students. It requires from the very

start that they coordinate their hands and feet. I gently feed the ball

in a predictable arc so that it can be defined clearly and encourage

them to, gently and softly, return the ball to me no faster than I hit

it to them. I encourage them to watch the seams of the ball as it

approaches and to activate all of their senses so that they ‘‘feel’’ the

entire sensation of the ball on the strings. Touching the ball softly,

feeling and guiding their body into a sideways position, is the thrust

of this first learning experience.

Through his mind’s eye, the player should be ready with a

preformed image of the upcoming action, his weight gently shifting

from side to side in his ready position, while the ball approaches.

His vision should focus on the flight of the ball coming toward

him, and the oneness of his left foot crossing and his right arm

blocking the ball should all be felt: He should come to know his

movement as a completely integrated experience, one that softly

returns the ball, while then gliding in uninterrupted motion back to

readiness for the next shot.

The Backhand Volley. The specific techniques for forehand and

backhand volleys are essentially the same on both sides except that

the footwork changes conversely and the player guides his racquet

back into position with the left hand on the racquet’s throat. The

execution of the backhand volley requires that the right foot cross

as the ball approaches, and that the left hand guide the racquet

back, and then gently let go as the racquet punches forward toward

its meeting with the flight of the ball. In both forehand and

backhand volley, the racquet face should be essentially facing the

net at the completion of the shot, and this is an image I try to instill

early in my students. Side-to-side volley drills impart this message

very quickly to even the most beginning student, since it makes

them realize exactly how much movement and preparation is

required to maintain the continuity of a tennis point.

These side-to-side drills begin with the ready position, swaying

back and forth and waiting; then, as the ball approaches, allowing

the left foot to cross, racquet back, and then blocking the ball;

returning to the center, maintaining that constant rhythm,

changing grip, guiding the racquet back, stepping with the right

foot, blocking the ball; returning to the center and staying with the

flow, changing grip, turning, stepping, contacting the ball, re-

turning to the middle, and so on. This is an everflowing, always

moving experience in the art form of tennis, and this can begin

during a player’s very first lesson. It is an experience in the grace of

continuous movement, and it is a lesson in the importance of maintaining continuity between the mental image and action.

The High Volley and Low Volley. The game invites an infinite

variety of player responses, and it is for this reason that one must

be able to deal with all heights and levels of the ball as it ap-

proaches. The tennis experience is, by definition, everchanging and

always moving. The mental exercise of detached visualization such

as this reading will prepare the student well for the uncertainty

always present in the game. It will serve to eliminate surprises, and

it will activate your subconscious mind to respond more in-

stinctively and in better preparation.

High balls for the player at net are a dream come true because

they offer such substantial angle into the opponent’s court. They

are missed quite often, however, by inexperienced players who,

through a desire to ‘‘kill’’ the ball, overplay their shot by making

their swing too big and altering the technique of sound stroke

production. One must always remember the importance of

retaining the continuity of thoughtful stroke-making. Within the

context of the volley, this means keeping the swing short, placing

the ball, and positioning the body properly by stepping across and

keeping the shoulders turned sideways. I find that controlling high

balls centers largely around my ability to lean on the front side of

my body, in the case of my forehand volley to really press on my

left leg and /ean on my left shoulder as I contact the ball. In the case

of the backhand volley, I concentrate on leaning on my right

shoulder, on keeping my right arm straight so that I do not push the

ball with unnecessary flexibility in my elbow, and on keeping my

wrist firm so as not to let it flop over the ball and dump it in the net. All of these reminders are tantamount to saying keep your basic

volley technique the same, with the intensity of focus in your mind

being primarily aimed at the dynamics of leaning more. Do not

slap, hit, or power the ball. Stay calm and in control and flow with

the opportunity to conclude the point that is now at hand.

Low volleys are a very sophisticated tennis shot. They require

that the player get down with the ball, and this means not only that

their knees bend but that their faces get down near the impact of

the ball. It is impossible to make low volleys successfully from a

detached viewpoint high above the contacting point. You cannot,

for example, fail to bend your knees and simply drop your racquet head and scoop the ball up. In order to make a low volley, the

player must be consciously aware of his racquet. It is noteworthy

here that this shot requires /ess swing than any other in tennis. It is

simply a matter of taking the body down with the ball, feeling the

intimacy of association and control, and opening the face of the racquet so that the pace already on. the approaching ball does the

work for you. It allows the ball to literally bounce off your racquet.

It is not a question of hitting the ball back. That would be a gross

overreaction and not within the realm of the ‘‘touch’’ and ‘‘feel’’

necessary to execute this shot. In a way very similar to the high volley, the low volley requires a fundamental simplification of

every part of the stroking process. The swing, however, is even

shorter than a regular or high volley, and watching the ball intently

is absolutely imperative. It is not uncommon on shots like this to

become distracted by the tape of the net, or preoccupied by where

you intend to hit the ball. Because of its inherent precision, it is

absolutely necessary that the player follow the ball all the way to

the strings of his racquet. There is never any substitute for watching

the ball, and absolute focus as it approaches is quite essential

during this most delicate shot.

The Groundstrokes. The term groundstroke in tennis is used in

reference to all balls encountered after they have bounced one time.

Because of this, these shots are most often hit considerably farther

away from the net than the volley just described, and it is for this

reason that they require more forward energy to project the ball

farther. This energy is only naturally projected through the use of a

larger swing. In other words, when hitting the ball from the

baseline, one’s backswing must be larger, and one’s swing should

be allowed to continue after impact in a portion of the swing

known as follow-through. In terms of the act of visualization, the

player should become as in tune as possible with the flight of the

ball as it approaches. An increased awareness of its arc over the net

and its velocity is absolutely necessary. The swing should start back

early as the ball leaves the opponent’s racquet, thus allowing the

calculating process of the mind to have already begun as early as

when the player takes that first step in anticipation of the ball.

The Forehand Drive. Every aspect of hitting groundstrokes is the

same as that in the volley except the size of the swing. That is to

say, all elements of body position relative to impact with the ball

are always constant. Within the context of the forehand drive, this

means that the initial image as one approaches the ball is to get sideways. The first pivot at the waist will turn the body into

position, while the left hand should begin gently guiding the

racquet into its backswing. This should occur at the same time that

the left foot begins to step over, and the arm and racquet should

continue in motion throughout the swing without hesitation. It is

possible to visualize the swing paralleling the shape of a small letter

‘*c,’’ inasmuch as rhythmic motion demands that the racquet head

not stop or hitch in the backswing. The racquet head should be

guided back in a position higher than the level of the waist and

about shoulder high, and then should gradually arc around until

the racquet shaft is parallel to the ground. It is in this position that

it should proceed forward toward impact with the ball. This should

most ideally occur with the racquet parallel to the ground and at

about waist height. From this point, the momentum created by the

continuing motion of the swing should pull the arm forward after

the ball. This part of the swing is known as the follow-through,

exactly as its name would seem to imply, follow-through, following

the ball. At the completion of this shot, the player should then

catch the racquet at its throat and smoothly guide it back into a

ready position for the next encounter.

It is most important to visualize the continuity of movement

prerequisite to playing the artistic point. That is, once the point has

begun, the specific movement of the player’s body should not be

segmented or interrupted. It is not a stop and start situation;

rather, it is an ever-moving and flowing aesthetic experience of

grace. Your mental image must include a definition of beauty and

you must guard yourself against thinking of it ‘‘as only a game.”’

The thoughtful player will look at himself as a creator and master

planner who through introspection will learn to live the reality of

spiritual and physical oneness within himself.

The Backhand Drive. When introducing forehands and backhands

to my students, I always stress the continuity of the game. The world’s most graceful players have always mastered that unique

quality. They have not appeared to use one stroking method on one

side of their body and another on the other side. With this in mind,

I instruct the backhand as merely the converse of the forehand. In this way, the racquet should be gently guided back with the left

hand at the throat, giving the player a vehicle to support the weight of the racquet and allowing him to keep the racquet head high. This

duplicates the small letter ‘‘c’’ in our forehand backswing, and it

insures the continuity of uninterrupted motion. This motion

requires that the racquet head proceed back about shoulder high

and curl around during the backswing at a level about waist high,

when the racquet is parallel to the ground. It should sweep forward

into impact with the momentum of the swing being in the top of the

racquet. This momentum will serve to pull the arm forward

naturally into the follow-through of the swing. It is important to

note that the follow-through is a natural flowing process, not one

to be rigidly manipulated in the manner of many novice players.

Your arm should not be locked and should not stop in frozen

motion at the completion of the swing. It should flow forward

along the path of the ball and then curl back again, since the player

should be readying himself for his next shot.

The backhand drive is one of the most natural shots in the game

of tennis because the arm of the hitter is located in the front of his

body. This gives him an almost pendulum-like swing into the ball. If this pendulum action occurs with the racquet parallel to the

ground at impact, one will experience a most exhilarating sense

of power by uncoiling. The backswing on both forehand and back-

hand, but particularly on backhand, allows the shoulders to curl

inward just enough to create a bodily coil in the upper part of the

body. The unleashing of this coil need not be hard or dramatic,

but the fact that it does exist allows for a very natural and flowing

approach of the racquet into the ball. This inherent acceleration of

motion will make the follow-through a natural occurrence and not a manipulated one.

Persons who incorrectly manipulate their swing on backhand are likely to have too much of their motion originating in their elbow.

The elbow should not lead the swing, rather the arm should be kept

close to the body in order to prevent this kind of pushing action.

The swing should always originate at the shoulder since it is this

position that establishes that fundamental continuity between the

racquet and the arm. It necessitates that one not only stroke the

ball with an arm and racquet oneness, but that one hold the racquet

properly. If the racquet is held too far around, for example, on

backhand, it will create an angle between the racquet and arm that

cannot be overcome as it proceeds forward, and it is for this reason

that it will inhibit the follow-through. This is what I refer to as

pushing the ball. All beginners tend to push the ball, rather than

stroke it, on backhand. More than anything, this response is one of

fear. A ping-pong push of sorts tends to make the player feel more

secure about keeping the ball within the lines.

Needless to say, proper visualization techniques should always

transcend value judgments concerning where the ball actually goes

during the learning process. Visualization at this point must always

be centered on the act of proper hitting technique and awareness of

your own body. And this can never be mixed with the externality of

success and failure with respect to the lines on the court. Whether

the ball is in or out, and how any specific action may or may not

affect game scores, present or future, is absolutely irrelevant. A

perfected synergy of movement, of course, raises aggregate scores

and in the end justifies the perfectionist approach to technique. High and Low Groundstrokes. Dealing with high balls from the

baseline is a totally different experience than encountering them at

the net. Not only do you fail to have any substantial angle down

into the court to work from, but the distance from your hitting

position to the'net is significantly greater. In most cases, high balls

on baseline shots are also balls with a minimum amount of pace.

This should tell the hitter immediately that he must be careful. One

of the most common errors made by the novice player is overhitting

slow balls and attempting to attack them in a manner similar to the

way described at the net. Proper visualization should guide the

body with an awareness of this liability. Even the best players in the

world realize the difficulty of applying excess pace to these balls.

As far as specific visualization is concerned, I find it useful to

imagine returning the ball in a way similar to which it approached.

In that context, the only exception would be when the opposing

player follows his shot to the net. This simple strategy allows the

player to simply put the high ball initiative back into his opponent’s

lap. Returning a semi-lob with another of the same kind is not that difficult. The difficulty always lies either with impatience or lack of

knowledge when handling balls of this type. For example, it is not

unusual to see even the most experienced player back up so that

these balls come down to a more comfortable stroking level.

If balls of this type are encountered in doubles where a net man is

almost always present, or in singles when the opponent chooses to

follow this shot to the net, much more care must be taken with our

choices. Visualization must include keeping these returns low and

away from the opponent in such a manner as to make him volley

up, a strategy that would open play on the next shot. These are not

“‘nower shots,’’ and it is often tempting for the more inexperienced

player to feel as though all passing shots must be hard shots. This is

not true. The visualization process when hitting these balls should

include a delicate feel of the ball on the strings and a gentle

placement to either side of the court. This requires not only a vivid

imagery of the ball being carried on the strings but of the path of

the ball changing and of successfully passing the opponent after

contact with the racquet.

Remember that visualization in any act can be either positive or

negative. Negative visualization is just as destructive as positive

imagery is constructive. On high threat shots such as high balls,

shots where the player is given almost too much time to think, this

seemingly obvious fact becomes important. Remember that fear is

anxiety-producing, and that lack of confidence has a way of almost

always fulfilling its own prophesy.

Low balls within the game of tennis differ from high ones in that

their speed, flight, and degree of spin are all more variable. This

makes playing the low ball infinitely more unpredictable. In any

case, handling the low ball always requires that the player lower his

own center of gravity in relation to the court. It is desirable to have

the feeling, unlikely as it may seem, of being a part of the court.

Many players tend to lunge or leap or reach in a manner that seems

contradictory to this. It has been my experience that the greatest

stroke masters in the game usually maintain a high degree of

oneness with the court on which they play. Jimmy Connors, for

example, may power high balls by jumping and throwing his weight

into them, but very few people have ever stayed as low on the low

balls as he does. Ken Rosewall is another wonderful example of a

player who maintained a definite oneness with the court through

his technique. In teaching, I have found it helpful to have students

lower their trailing knee all the way to the court surface as I feed

them gentle practice shots from a basket. It gives the player the

sensation of being lower than ever before, and it adds new per-

spective as far as perception of the flight of the ball over the net is

concerned. Studying the flight of the ball from a low vantage point

is extremely useful because it makes one aware of the net, and the

role it really plays as a barrier to the flight of the ball. This means

to the thoughtful player that he must literally, in some way, lift the

ball up in order to get it over the net. This seemingly obvious fact is

one of the most overlooked axioms of tennis, mainly because it is

possible to see the opposing court in our vision through the net. By

keeping one leg down on the court and thereby lowering our

perspective, it is possible to somehow get a more realistic vision of

exactly what it takes to handle low balls effectively. For example,

low balls that are short must always be handled delicately, and can

never be hit hard and flat at the same time. The trajectory of a hard

hit flat ball will always go up—over the net, yes, but somewhere

outside the back line. The only way to hit a short ball hard is with

the application of top-spin as a controlling agent, and this is a very

advanced technique. It requires a very delicate but high speed

brushing of the back of the ball rather than contacting it from

directly behind.

The essence of low groundstrokes, then, lies in the player’s abil-

ity to stay down, and to realize even on the most conscious level

that the net really is in his way. This barrier is, indeed, an obstacle

and one over which the ball must be lifted. This lift must be

handled with precision and exactly how much is required is totally

dependent on the depth of the ball as it is encountered on the court.

Short low balls have to be handled more softly than those all the way back. The feeling of movement is all encompassing but it must

center within a person’s legs. The player must have certain

knowledge that his legs are a vehicle and that they will lead him to

the flow of the point. The Overhead Smash. The overhead smash is without a doubt one

of the most decisive shots in tennis, but more importantly to me, it

is a stepping stone for my students to learn serving. Never has

visualization of the flight of the ball been more important than in

the overhead smash. The arc of the ball is always different, and at

times it appears to be dropping straight down. This is particularly

difficult because there is no external part of the court (your target

area) in the back field of your vision. As one approaches contact

with a ball lobbed over one’s head, there is no other frame of

reference with which to judge the ball except the sky. Obviously

this is no earthly reference, and it is for that reason that it is often

difficult to judge the precise fall of the ball. Having earthly, part of

the court, frames of reference is very important.

The most significant imagery the player should incorporate when

hitting an overhead smash centers around the movement of his feet

and the fact that they and only they are the vehicle which can carry

him to proper position beneath the ball. This movement should be

done by means of very rhythmic side steps that align the body

directly beneath the ball in a sideways position. There should be a

very special and unified rhythm felt in the arms as the right arm

goes back in an abbreviated motion behind the head, while the left

hand goes up in a motion that will parallel that of the toss in ser-

ving. This action of the left hand is not inconsequential because it is

the main agent for balance in this very difficult shot. Furthermore,

the raising of the left hand can serve as an aiming device of sorts,

whereby the player can use it as a secondary pattern in his visual image to guide himself underneath the ball. Once underneath the

ball and well planted with the weight on the back foot, the prominent sensation of this shot should be the feeling of extension

into the ball. This, of course, requires that one judge the falling

ball properly. If this is done, a fully outstretched body, to the point

of reaching up on one’s toes, will characterize this shot. This ex-

tension will move all of the weight of the player’s body into the shot

and it will give him maximum angle down into his opponent’s

court. The imagery one should incorporate in hitting the overhead

smash should be one of continuous and flowing movement. It

should begin with the identification of the arc of the ball and the

flight you wish to direct it, and it should be accompanied by a

gradual retreat underneath the ball. At this time, the right foot

should be planted and an extension begun. This occurs after the

player has set himself underneath the shot, with the left hand acting

as a guiding mechanism for the body placement. The overhead

smash is one of the most decisive shots in tennis and it is an ideal preface to the totally self-contained motion of serving.

The placement of the overhead smash is consistent with the

placement of any tennis ball inasmuch as it is directly related to the

openness or closeness of the player’s stance. For example, if the

hitter desires to hit the ball down the line, he should close his stance

more than if he chooses to hit crosscourt. These axioms of

placement are as true for the overhead smash as for any other shot.

The crosscourt placement will leave the shoulders more facing the

net, or more ‘‘open.’’ The degree of openness or closeness of the

player’s stance is what affects the angle at which the racquet

contacts the ball. A more inexperienced player will make errors in

overhead placement if their efforts to control the ball center around

manipulation of the racquet with the wrist. It should never be the

goal of the thoughtful player to change his swing for placement.

Placements require that the swing itself remain consistent, but that

the point of contact, and therefore the point of position, be

maneuvered in a constructive and thoughtful way.

In summary, visualization seems to involve an underdeveloped dimension of human intelligence. It is not totally mysterious,

however, because such attributes seem to respond to deliberate

cultivation and training.

Many aspects of behavior are ‘‘automatic,’’ almost like heart-

beat and swallowing, until intentionally raised to a level of con-

sciousness. A superficial example is memory of which shoelace you tied first this morning—it’s probably the same one to come first

every morning for as long as you can remember. A complex

example is the use of biofeedback (via handwarming) techniques to

reduce blood pressure and the likelihood of an impending migraine

headache.

This chapter has stressed consciousness-raising strategies for

refining specific tennis techniques. More importantly, it has built a

case for a gestalt of related techniques which, upon mastery, rise

above the level of the sum of their parts. The result is, once again,

virtually automatic behavior in which the mind does not find it

necessary to use a checklist for every court response. But this time

the aggregate behavior is refined well beyond chance, producing a

tennis player who responds like a well-tuned engine to whatever

demands are placed upon him. Visualizing the integrated final

result is the strategy which leads to perfection.

THERE ARE SEVERAL SPORTS other than tennis that im-

mediately evoke an artistic, as well as an athletic, response from the

participant. They have in common the disciplined behavior of a

single individual. A gymnast, for example, relates most profoundly

to his own body and to a particular piece of apparatus. Similarly

individualistic exercises are seen in the martial arts sequences of the

late Kung Fu master, Bruce Lee. In gymnastic movement, as well as

the Kung Fu self-defense exercises, the participant is involved in the

personal mastery of a very contained exercise that is always flowing

and in definite harmony with a rhythm he himself sets. While the

Kung Fu exercise is a ritualized preparation for the possibility of

encounter with another individual, the emphasis of. the sport lies in

the mastery of specific body movements and responses. It is an

integrated and artistic experience developed in the Far East where

people characteristically value introspective meanings which can be

expressed as a disciplined total life.

More familiar to those of us in the United States would be the

graceful moves of ice skaters like Peggy Fleming, whose gliding

motion represents a poetic exercise in continuous movement. The

fluidity of such a performance is evident to even the casual observer

of the skater’s uninterrupted program, well-suited to musical

accompaniment; likewise, the floor exercises in gymnastics.

Veteran performers Olga Korbut and Nellie Kim, and the youthful

star Nadia Comaneci were brilliant examples in the last Olympic

games. In another field, the screen classic, West Side Story, was not

only a successful musical but it was an artistic application of

modern dance to a contemporary New York theme. More recently,

Hollywood’s box office hit, Rocky, contains fight sequences that

were choreographed down to the very last blow. Every jab, every

left hook, every dodging, feinting move was preplanned. When

interviewed on television, author-actor Sylvester Stallone was able

to recreate the exact movements portrayed in the movie sequence of

the final fight. The perfection of body movement thus became a

major communications medium. Had the fight been for real, a

winner would have likely resulted from-_such meticulous attention

to detail. The competitive personal sports seem to respond

especially well to perfectionist preconditioning.

THE MODERN DANCE ASPECT OF TENNIS

All of these sports have been described because they illustrate a

mastery of movement and continuous motion at a very high level.

This is not an inconsistent theme in the game of tennis either, since

slow motion replays of championship matches always depict the

great players staying in continuous flowing motion from the

beginning of a point to the very end. It is not a segmented ‘‘run

here, then run there’’ kind of performance. The art of tennis is

ever-moving and always graceful and continuous. For the in-

dividual learning the game, the most obvious complication to

becoming familiar with this kind of rhythm is the ball. The fact that

it is moving and being constructively hit away from the player acts

as a considerable impairment to well-integrated and artful ex-

pression. Most of the other performances just described do not

have any direct external obstacles to their success. Tennis, however,

like boxing, is an act of competition, and there is an opponent on

the other side of the net intentionally trying to complicate your

task.

Although it seems revolutionary now, it appears possible that

mastery of the classical body movements of tennis can occur before

mastery of the ball. I have found a great deal of personal success

with all of my students when I stress mastery of a personal rhythm

and feel of movement before they attempt to hit the ball back and

forth, or to play the game. It seems that once a person is con-

taminated by the act of competition, it is very difficult to undo the

unconscious self-generated pressure to master the score rather than

themselves. Many of my players have found the idea of tennis

choreography to be very helpful, inasmuch as it makes it possible

for them to master specific tennis movements alone. After these

sophisticated bodily moves become recognizable and repeatable,

almost all players find it much easier to transfer the experience to

the act of hitting the ball. These players become artists of the game

and always relate to their experience differently than the players who began their learning cycle from the other end of the skill

sequence. A person can choose to hack his way to victory by getting

the ball over the net any way he can think of, or he can choose a

more beautiful and self-contained exercise of controlling himself.

The interesting footnote to this prescription is that not only do the

tennis artists experience a more rewarding result from their effort

personally, they dissipate less energy in awkward body isometrics.

The increased endurance and performance control do not assure

victory, but that is usually the result.

CHOREOGRAPHING TENNIS’ RECURRING BEHAVIORS

The most unusual aspect of the tennis choreography when first

observed is the fact that there is no racquet or ball. The exercise of

tennis choreography is strictly related to the specific movements of

different parts of the body in simulated swinging motions, and in

the feet in order to reinforce correct and rhythmic footwork. The

experience becomes much like a tennis ballet and it is useful to

perform in front of a mirror, so as to reinforce this proper technique through the use of images and visualization as already

discussed in previous chapters.

Lateral Foot Movement. Effective movement during a tennis point

requires that the player always establish a consistent position to the ball. This necessitates that a prédetermined alignment of the feet

always be attained. (The specifics of these techniques are discussed in subsequent chapters.) The player uses foot positioning to estab-

lish consistency in stroking the ball. Competence centers around

his ability to move in small, rhythmic steps not unlike Muhammad Ali’s carefully controlled ‘‘dance’’ and ‘‘shuffle.’? The novice

player will be more inclined to reach for the ball with a measure of desperation and to move in very large steps—movement which

is much more cumbersome and less effective in terms of speed

and the resulting body position for returning the ball. It is for

this reason that one of the first choreographed moves for our tennis

exercise be lateral movement in small steps which leads the player

into a sideways hitting position. The sequence begins by turning the

shoulders to the right (if you are a right-handed player attempting a

forehand drive) and then running smoothly in small steps to the

side, ending up in such a manner as to plant the lead foot (in this

case the left leg) and then simulate the swinging motion with the

right arm. This sequence should begin from the classic ready

position, then lead smoothly to the right side with both arms acting

in unison in an effort to give the player a distinct feeling of balance

during the exercise. Many tennis players forget the use of their left

hand and for this reason are more off balance than those who

consider the limbs of their body as parts of a whole that can and

should be perfectly balanced for sound stroke execution. The

simulation of the swing should lead the right hand back in con-

tinuous motion with no hitching in the backswing and no un-

necessary movement of the wrist, a common error likely to occur if

the player had begun these dry run exercises by holding a racquet.

The idea is to feel the motion of the arm and therefore the racquet

as an extension of the arm once it is introduced to the learning

experience. A smooth motion of the right arm should then lead to

an extension in front of the body, at which time the player should

simulate and visualize precisely what a correct follow-through and

conclusion to the swing should be. It is at that point that the player

should bring his two arms back to a simulation of the ready position and, without stopping, shuffle gracefully back to the

center of the court.

An Imaginary Court. These exercises need not be performed on a

tennis court but the visualization of the participant should include

the lines of the court and the fact that he must return to the center

in order to defend it properly. These shuffle steps just described are

very much like those used by basketball players when they defend

against a team that has the offense. They are steps that are very

rhythmic and allow the player to move laterally back to the middle

of the court without turning his body sideways in a conventional

running stance. This is valuable to the tennis player because it

allows him to observe the entire play of the ball as it occurs in the

court in front of him. If the player had turned sideways to return to

the center of the court, he would be peering over his shoulder to

watch his opponent play the ball and this would not be conducive

to establishing sound body position.

This same sequence can be performed on the backhand side. It

should begin as the forehand did, moving from the basic ready

position in small steps to a position of readiness for visualized ball

contact. It is important that the right foot be placed across just

previous to the imagined contact with the ball, and it is important

that the player understand that the left hand should be acting to

cradle the throat of the racquet once it is introduced. Again, it is

quite important to instill the notion of balance into the player, since

as his right hand begins its motion into contact the left hand should

counterbalance the forward motion by extending backwards.

Again, this represents a butterfly-like opening of the arms. The

feeling is one of freedom and grace. The two players who im-

mediately come to mind with respect to this backhand movement

are Ken Rosewall and Evonne Goolagong, both of whom are

natural masters of these concepts of balance.

The First Sequence. The first choreographed tennis sequence

should thus incorporate side-to-side movements of forehands and

backhands because the player should realize the importance of

continuous flowing motion from one imagined shot back to

another. The idea of returning to the middle in rhythm and entering another shot is of critical importance. The golfer, for example, is responsible for making one shot at atime. The tennis player, on the

other hand, must deal with hitting a series of shots in a row, and

this is why a concept of preparation is so necessary to this learning

experience. The sequence should go as follows:

Begin small side steps toward the side

The arms still in a parallel position in front of the body (not

hanging at random) Take five rhythmic steps to the right and then plant the left

Bring the arms back in a simulated backswing

Set the left hand in a position of imagined contact with the ball

Allow the right hand to continue its small looping motion

Bring the right hand toward the contact point over the lead

Follow-through, and in the same movement begin shuffle

Three or four rhythmic shuffle steps back to the center, and

into the ready position which leads the player

Into a pivot to the opposite side toward backhand

Taking five small steps to the left

As the player guides the racquet back toward backhand,

with the left hand on an imaginary throat of the racquet

Pressing the right leg to transfer weight forward

Opening the arms in the movement of balance

Which leads the back side of the right hand into an

imaginary contact with the ball ,

And then farther into a completion of the swing

Which leads again into rhythmic movement of shuffle steps

Balance and Rhythm. This sequence should be performed in

repetition and rhythm, within which no interruption should be

noticed. It should be smooth and easy, and the focus of one’s

attention should be on balance in the upper part of the body and

rhythmic movement in the feet.

This same kind of side-to-side drill can be simulated with

movements toward forehand and backhand volleys at the net. The

only difference in these sequences lies in the fact that the shot called

volley requires considerably less swing, and these imaginary

sequences should therefore include only a short punch stroke as

opposed to a longer backswing and foliow-through. All of the

elements of movement in the feet and of balance and position

remain the same. The player should practice side-to-side movement

in this manner for both groundstrokes and volleys. Complete visualization should always precede any attempt to begin these

sequences with the racquet and ball. Thorough familiarity with the

feeling of integrated movement within the participant’s own body

is a prerequisite to any actual playing experience.

Assertive Action Sequences. Other than side-to-side drills for

groundstroke or volley, dry run exercises of this type are very

useful for the difficult sequence of getting from the baseline to the

net, one being behind a strong first serve and the other behind an

approach shot following a short ball hit by your opponent. In the

serve-and-volley sequence where the player follows his service

directly to the net, it is important that a conscious effort be made to

incorporate considerable forward motion in the actual swing of his

serve. This will lead the player to follow-through by crossing his

back foot over the baseline during the completion of his serve, and

this last step of the swing acts also as the first step in his advance to

the net. The rhythm of the sequence should start in a meditative

stance of preparation like one would incorporate in serving. The

player should visualize the service court across the net and should

then open his arms in unison as if taking the racquet back and

tossing the ball with the left hand. The right arm should, in flowing

motion, reach behind the head, then over the head, and finally

across the body with the stepping motion of the right leg just

described. This will lead a player into his rush of the net where,

depending on the player’s size, he should take four to five steps in

order toreach the service line. — Successful on-court experience necessitates that the player get at

least as far as the service line and this should be a main objective in

the choreographed sequence. Once the player has reached the

service line, he should plant both feet, then bend the knees into an

intense ready position with both arms parallel to one another. It is

from here that the player should then take one step across in either

direction for the first volley. It is important for the player to un-

derstand that this first volley in the sequence is most likely to be low

since very rarely will a player get all the way to the net. This harsh

fact of life should be incorporated in the choreographed sequence,

inasmuch as the player should make a conscious effort to really

bend the knees and get all of his body down close to the ground for

the simulated shot. From that shot forward the player should then

move several steps forward and into position for the next volley.

It is worth noting here that these are sequences of shots and not

ones where the player is winning points with a single blow. Tennis

points are most constructively won by thinking in terms of a series

of initiatives and anticipated responses. Thus, the serve and volley

sequence should begin behind the baseline, smoothly and gracefully

opening the arms together, then moving four to five steps forward,

planting in a ready position and then, in alternating fashion,

taking one step across for a visualized low volley, then proceeding

forward several more steps into a more aggressive position where

the player should visualize a more decisive volley down into the

court, since he is at that time in a position much closer to the net.

Once this serve and volley three-shot sequence is mastered, it is

often useful to add a fourth shot to the series by putting an

overhead smash at the end. In the game situation, when the volleys

are not decisive or the opponent is of superior ability, the player

will often encounter a lob by his opposition. It is for this reason

that it is useful to practice graceful movement both forward and

backward from the net. At the completion of the second visualized

volley, it is important for the player to then turn into a sideways

position and retreat under an imagined lob through the use of side

steps that allow his body to be turned in a position very similar to

that used when serving. The key to hitting good overhead smashes

is always to be directly underneath the ball, and it is for this reason

that effective movement with side steps is so important. The player

should position himself at about the service line and then raise both

arms together in simulated effort like serving with the right arm

behind the head and the left arm up in a motion like the toss. At

this point, the player should reach over his head in imagined

contact with the ball for satisfactory conclusion to the point. There

should be no hesitation between one sequence and another and,

after this four-shot sequence, the player should then immediately

retreat backwards to the baseline and begin another sequence

exactly the same way.

This same sequence of approaching the net can be achieved when

the player takes advantage of a short ball by his opponent. This

choreographed sequence should begin with side-to-side forehand

and backhand groundstrokes and then be interspersed with a

diagonal movement to either side in order to take advantage of a

visualized short ball which would lead the player to sound volleying

position at the net. The rhythm of this sequence should be just as

continuous and flowing as all of the others just described. It should

begin with a forehand groundstroke, then a shuffle back to the

middle, followed by a backhand groundstroke, concluding with a

Then comes the imagined recognition of a short ball and a

diagonal run toward the net, contacting the ball on the run and

positioning oneself in the low ready position of attack near the

service line. It is from this position that the player then steps across

in either a forehand or backhand volley depending on which side he

imagines hitting his approach shot. The player should get in the

habit of defending to the side that the approach shot was hit since it

is his main objective to protect the down the line passing shot of his

opponent. This is a rather advanced technique and not necessarily

applicable to beginning players, but it does not take long for any

player to learn about the geometry of the tennis point. The

sequence, then, should be forehand groundstroke, shuffle to the

middle, backhand groundstroke, shuffle to the middle, forehand

approach shot, backhand volley, forehand volley, overhead smash,

then hustle back to the middle and begin the entire sequence again,

except approach the net this time from a simulated backhand

approach shot, etc.

These ever-flowing and continuously rhythmic movements are

excellent training for beginning or advanced players. They allow the player to detach himself from the act of hitting the ball and

bring his attention more inward and toward a truly artistic ex-

perience on the court. It is all too easy to be distracted by the act of

competition in playing the game. It is the recommendation of this

author that every player uncover the special awareness of beauty

that can be found in his own controlled and balanced body move-

ment.

Tennis choreography takes three common forms: (1) home

practice for singles; (2) a recurring group exercise at the tennis club;

and (3) a simulated doubles match involving complementary,

rather than identical, player positions.

It is possible that such early beginnings will eventually be for-

malized as artistic presentations set to music and coached by

professional choreographers. The author has already introduced

them through television. For now, however, a more pragmatic

claim can be made for a major advance in instructional strategy,

the key being to discipline the total body first, adding the racquet,

ball, and an adversary player only after the fundamentals of

balance, rhythm, and sequence progression have been programmed

into the player’s nervous system. A significant reduction in player

inhibitions is already apparent. Effects on tournament scores

remain uncertain but promising.

IN ITS PUREST FORM placement of the tennis ball is a target

exercise. The difficulty that separates the game of tennis from other

target exercises is that the target always moves. The target in tennis

moves in direct relation to where the opposing player is not, and

this instills a certain pressure on the player not only to hit the target

but to figure out exactly where it is. All of this is complicated by the

fact that the vehicle for hitting the target, the ball, is also moving

and that it all occurs within seconds.

There are several alternatives of placement in the game of tennis

and they are known as crosscourt, down the middle, and down the

line. These terms apply to both volleys and groundstrokes and

unlike their names imply, they don’t always have a direct relation

to either crossing the court or going down any specific lines on the

court. Many times they will, but not always. For example,

crosscourt balls very often do cross the diagonal of the court.

Whether or not they do, however, depends totally on where the

player executing the shot is standing. A person does know,

however, that a crosscourt ball will always cross from one side of

the player hitting it to the other. If the ball is hit, the hitter will

always be present, and this is the only absolute of placement. The

crosscourt placement requires very simply that the ball be contacted farther in front of the hitter’s body than any other shot. He must

hit it sooner. In the same way that a baseball player might pull the:

ball to third base, the tennis player directing the crosscourt shot

must hit his ball in front of him. This is precisely what player

Therefore, whether we speak of forehands or backhands or

forehand volleys or backhand volleys, the fact of placing the ball

crosscourt is always the same: Hit the ball sooner and follow-

that there is an absence of follow-through during the volley.)

The down the middle placement alternative is not as widely used

as crosscourt or down the line, since its inherent capability of

making the opposition run is less. It is, however, a useful tool

against any player who objects to hitting crowded balls,and who is

reluctant and/or lazy to move his feet on balls close to his body. It

forces the opposing player to hit from the center of his court and

therefore create his own angle, should he want to begin a con-

structive attack against you. Many women players have found that

attacking the net by following shots hit down the middle to be an

effective strategy against Chris Evert, simply because she hits either

crosscourt or down the line so well when given the angle of hitting

from the sides of the court. The down the middle shot should be

contacted precisely at one’s side. Effective visualization should

include a mental image of the racquet parallel to the ground di-

rectly over the lead leg of the shot being hit.

The down the line placement in tennis is by far the most precise,

because most of the time it occurs over the highest portion of the

net, the sides. There is considerably less margin for error than the

crosscourt ball, and it is more delicate and requires better timing

because it is contacted slightly behind the player. If the hitter is

standing in the center of the court and wishes to hit down the line,

his contact point must be approximately six inches farther back

than the down the middle shot, and his shoulders must be turned

more so that the angle of his racquet face is pointed toward the

sideline. In similar proportion to this, the finish to the swing must

follow-through toward that same point.

Many players tend to overreact and panic oncé the ball gets

slightly behind them and in this way overplay down the line shots. I

believe that it is important to remember that it is the control of this

shot that counts the most and not the power. It takes considerable

concentration, and therefore precise and vivid visualization, in

order to control a ball from a position of comparative weakness

farther behind you as you hit. Down the line shots are controlled,

and high quality control comes from seeing acutely through the

mind’s eye.

The serve in tennis is without a doubt the most important shot in

the game. It is deserving of such an absolute title since every player

is obligated to serve half of the games that are played. In this way, a

strong service will give the player an advantage half of the time,

while a weak one will be a liability to defend a similar amount of

the time.

Not only is the serve the most important stroke in tennis, it must

also be considered the most sophisticated and the most beautiful.

Unlike any other shot in the game, the service stroke is completely

self-contained, and it is this one fact that lends it so well to the art

of constructive visualization. It allows the player to be more precise not only in defining his target but in controlling the mechanism with which he has to hit it. All other shotmaking in the game

centers around movement, not only of the opposition, but of every

ball the player is required to hit. In the act of serving, the player can

isolate these difficult complications to placement and bring them

all under his direct control. ‘ When readying to serve, the player should station himself in a

sideways position to the baseline with his left foot at approximately

a forty-five degree angle to the baseline, and his right foot parallel

to it. It is important that this same position be used every time

because isolating the distance from the player to the court, and therefore the angle which the ball must travel to clear the net, is

a primary objective. Starting from a different place every time, or

moving out from under the ball once the process has been started,

are both distinct liabilities to effective serving.

Specific visualization in serving usually begins with the service

toss. The arms of the player should be relaxed comfortably at his

sides, with the left hand at the throat of the racquet before

beginning the motion. It is from this starting point that the left

hand should rise into its full extension before releasing the ball.

This graceful and gradual release of the ball is in diametric op-

position to the flipping action a novice player might be inclined to

use when tossing. The player must understand that the toss of the

serve is the only variable within the motion. It is the only part of the

motion that can cause change in a practical swing, and it is for this

reason that mastery of the toss is prerequisite to any consistent

serve. There can never be a repeatable accuracy in serving without

consistency in tossing, and this is harder than it may seem since

most people use their less dominant hand with considerably less

effectiveness. Specific visualization of the good toss should have it

being placed as high as one can extend their racquet. The liability of

a toss too high is that it forces the player to hit a falling ball,

equivalent almost to hitting an overhead smash from the farthest

reaches of the court. The image the server should have in his mind

should be of the ball just sitting there for that small instant before

impact and before gravity can pull it down. There is a very literal

pause at the very height of the toss, and it is at this small instant in

time that the server should focus his image.

The swing of the serve marks the beginning of the most graceful

of all tennis motions. In the same way that the most graceful birds

might fly, so must the server begin to slowly open his arms. It is an

act of unison and harmony. As the left arm begins to rise and re-

lease the ball, the right arm will drop gently to the side, similar in

fact to the way the pendulum in a clock might fall. The racquet

should continue until it is parallel to the ground and pointed

toward the screen behind the player. It is at this time that the rac-

quet arm should bend at the elbow, thus allowing the head of the

racquet to circle behind the player’s head and down behind his

back. It is important to note that it is this portion of the swing from

which the greatest servers have always derived their strength. A

typical negative visualization that inhibits many beginning players

as they learn to serve is the advice of many coaches to ‘“‘scratch

their back.’’ This vivid form of imagery has the negative effect of

causing these players to stop their motion in this very critical area

of the swing, and this is bad. It is true that the arm and racquet pass

through a stage that could be described like this, but the emphasis

given by an image of a back scratch is such that it causes a negative

‘‘halting’’ result. It is absolutely imperative that effective serving

keep the motion of the racquet moving. The imagery that I en-

courage my students to use centers more around the similarity

between serving and throwing a ball. Throwing a ball keeps the

elbow pointed back to the screen in that critical position, and that

allows for proper shoulder involvement in the stroke. This is

particularly useful for women who learn to ‘‘toss’’ balls, rather

than throw them, and therefore have their elbow pointed toward

the net in their feminine throwing motion. The racquet speed when

it passes through the backscratching position will be so fast that the

conscious mind should not even try to isolate it. At that stage of the

swing a person’s visionary capacity should be focused on reaching

as high as possible to contact the ball. Similar to the overhead

smash, this full extension of the body enables the player to have a

maximum angle down into the service court. Once the ball is

contacted, player visualization should focus on the concept of relaxation, of letting the racquet and weight of the body simply

follow the motion of the swing already created. Anything more

than that can only result in gross manipulation, and that interferes

with spontaneity and fluidity. If this is done, the forward pull of

the right arm will allow the racquet to cross to the left side of the

body, and the right leg to come over into the playing area. This

transfers the weight of the body in the direction the player is hitting

the ball and for this reason serves to do work for the player.

One of the most elusive and difficult aspects of serving centers

from the fact that the player must generate pace from an absolutely still ball. In every other shot in tennis he is able to work from pace

that is already on the ball and thereby use it for his own purposes in

directing it back. In serving, however, the player must essentially

deal with a stationary ball. Whatever pace is applied to the ball

must be done through effective technique in his swing, combined

with a very coordinated effort in transferring body weight into the

shot. This transfer of body weight is called coiling and works in the

same way that a spring might coil down when it is pushed and then

extend outward when it is released. This is in essence what the

advanced server should try and do.

Within this context, this specified technique dictates that the

server begin with his weight on his back foot. From this point, and

as the motion from the arms begins, his weight should transfer to

his front leg. For the right-handed player this would mean that the

weight of his body has been transferred from his right leg to the

outside edge of his left. The image and sensation of this position

should have the body arched in such a way that if a pane of glass

were extended upward from the baseline only the player’s left hip

would extend far enough to touch it. This arc in the body is a

statement of balance as well as coil. The left leg should be bent and

the weight of the body should be on the left side of the foot. The

right foot should be parallel to the left, but bearing relatively little

weight of the body. When I serve, I have a very clear image and feel

of this portion of the swing. This is the most beautiful position in

tennis. It is the most artistic and the most balanced and it is from this coil that the strength of all good servers comes. As one begins

to push with both legs, the coil is released. Extension into the ball is

more than just a reaching out of the racquet; rather, it is a full

upward thrust propelled by the entire body. This push upward with

the legs allows for a complete transfer of weight from the coiled left

leg, back to the right leg, which the forward motion will then pull

across. This occurs during the last part of the swing and is known

as the follow-through to the motion.

In a motion as complicated as serving, where there are several

inherent images operating at the same time (the toss, the swing, and

the coil) it is important that one have a larger, all-encompassing

image of the three together. It is not possible to envision each of

these images differently, or in succession, because that will serve

only to segment your motion. This is precisely the undoing magic that thinking in words and instructions does. The player should

form his image of the whole, but before that is possible, he must

fully understand each part of that whole. A complete visualization

would have the player gliding smoothly into the coiling position as

he tosses, then bringing the racquet through a throwing position

behind his head, over his head as he extends his legs, and then

across to the left side as the motion pulls his body into follow-

through. As previously noted, the service in tennis is the most

beautiful and the most purely artistic form of movement in the

game. More than any other court performance, it is the serve which

will lend itself to mastery through visualization.

VISUALIZING SPIN WHILE SERVING

A constructive application of spin in the game of tennis neces-

sitates a very clear image of precisely what occurs as the tennis

racquet contacts the ball. Hitting spin serves is very much an act of

precision since only an edge of the ball is actually contacted. Which

edge of the ball is brushed depends on precisely which service type

the player is using—a slice, top-spin or the American twist.

It is important to understand while hitting any spin serve that it is to your advantage not to reduce the racquet speed of your swing.

That is, many players become too careful as they begin learning

spin serves and forget the obvious fact that, since only a piece of

the ball is being hit, that every bit of racquet speed is necessary in

order to make it turn and still project it forward. Very clear visualization should occur in the’act of serving, mainly because

there is so much ¢ime to prepare the image through the mind’s eye. You are not rushed from shot to shot, but rather can prepare for

your service almost at will. Spin serves very literally must be carried

along the face of the racquet. The clearest visualization will bring

to the player an almost photographic image of his racquet grazing

along, string-by-string, the edge of the ball. As the racquet moves

over the ball, the friction between each string will begin it turning

and its projection forward, over the net, will occur simultaneously.

The slice serve is generally the first spin serve learned in tennis. All spin serves are accomplished by a fundamental change in the

player’s grip (it must be changed from the forehand to backhand

grip) and a change in the service toss, which should no longer be

directly over the player’s head. The change in grip has the effect of

creating an angle on the racquet face, and this, combined with the

placement of the toss, is what allows the racquet to hit only the

edge of the ball. It is quite like peeling an orange, since, in a way,

your racquet almost wraps around the ball.

The slice serve is obtained by using the backhand grip and tossing

the ball to the right side (for right-handed players). This will allow

the racquet to graze along the right side of the ball and it will induce

the flight of the ball and its subsequent bounce to curve from right

to left. It is important to have a clear visualization not only of the

contact point as the racquet hits the ball, but also of this most

important flight that the ball should take after it is hit. This serve is

often used by the intermediate player as a second serve. Once

mastered, the ability of the ball to curve from right to left is an

invaluable tool of exploitation, particularly in the outside corner of

the deuce court and the inside of the add court.

The top-spin serve acts exactly as its name implies. The ball spins

straight over the top. It is important to note that the arc and flight

of the top-spin serve is considerably different from that of the slice.

The flight of the top-spin serve will tend to arc over the net and stay

in the air longer. Additionally, the top-spin serve will take a very

aggressive bounce up after the ball hits the ground. The spin itself,

irrespective of speed, makes this shot aggressive, and tends to force

the receiver back. The ball will bounce higher and will tend to

increase in speed after the ball hits. The increased speed, as well as

the fact that many balls are encountered by the receiver as high

balls, make this a very desirable serve indeed. Furthermore, it

becomes for the advanced player not only an attacking weapon but

a percentage serve as well, inasmuch as the raised flight of the ball

gives the player a very safe percentage over the net. Very few

top-spin serves by an experienced player will go into the net.

Specific technique and visualization necessary to hit this serve

require that a very clear image be formed of the ball toss back over

the server’s head, toward the left side. It is only from this vantage

point, and by arching the back, that the player can have the proper

angle to brush up the back side of the ball. This brushing straight

up the back of the ball aiters the swing somewhat and leads the

follow through from behind the head in a wider arc toward the

right side and then around. It is a brushing and lifting motion, and

once the ball has been hit, it is important that a complete image of

this shot also include the arc of the ball as it carries over the net, as

well as its bounce after it hits.

The American twist is the most difficult of all spin serves

because, in many ways, it combines the effects of both slice and

topspin. Specific visualization necessary to complete this very

aggressive and advanced serve begins with an image of the player’s

back arched even more than on the top-spin serve, since the service

toss must necessarily be even farther over his head and to the left. This toss must also be farther over the player’s right shoulder,

rather than left as in the top-spin also. This odd position of the toss

is what allows the player to really brush the ball dramatically, at an angle beginning at the lower left edge of the ball and finishing a

diagonal path up toward the right. This strange angle of grazing the

ball will induce the ball to spin in a very erratic and always un-

predictable way. This element of unpredictability alone is enough

to inhibit its return, but its high bounce and kick complicate other

matters for the receiver as well. The actual flight of the ball over the

net is similar to the top-spin, but even more pronounced, since it

arcs over the net and tends to stay in the air an absolute maximum

amount of time. This is used by most experienced players in

combination with the slice, top-spin, and flat serves, and can be

interspersed at points on either first or second serves. This is one of

the most advanced weapons at any tennis player’s disposal and it

takes considerable practice to learn it. Much of a player’s success in

learning a shot as difficult as this lies in his ability to visualize,

through his mind’s eye, precisely what he is trying to make happen.

The first profound image in this particular serve should be of one’s

own arched back and the position of the service toss. The suc-

ceeding image that one should hold is clearly the diagonal path of the racquet strings, when the ball brushes the strings, and then

followed by a picture of a very arcing ball as it clears the net.

The flat serve represents the negation of spin. It is the no-spin

serve and it is characterized most profoundly by its speed. Many

players choose to use a forehand grip on this service in order to

keep the racquet face absolutely flat (facing the net) as it contacts

the ball. Many advanced players, however, still use their backhand

grip but simply roll their wrist inward just prior to contact. There is

no absolute rule of thumb on this point since it seems to vary with

personal preference. The only invariable thing about this serve,

however, is that the racquet must always be flat at impact, and, of

course, this is an important image on which to concentrate.

The toss of the flat serve must be directly in front of the player.

Since maximum speed on the ball is so important when hitting this

shot, one of the most effective images a player should have centers

around the effective technique of uncoiling. The motion must

proceed smoothly and easily and slide eventually into a very

dynamic uncoiling of the body into the ball. As the body is un-

coiled, a clear image should be held of the racquet face coming

from squarely behind the ball. It should appear through the mind’s

eye as if the ball is very symmetrically located squarely in the center

of a perfect checkerboard of strings. Also central among the

imagery of this serve should be vivid visualization of the feel of

stretching and reaching as. high as possible for contact. This will

give the player maximum angle down into the court which becomes

quite important on this serve where there is so little margin for

error in its flight.

There is no circumstance in the game of tennis where the act of

visualization is so clearly suited as in the placement of the serve.

This should be one of the first exercises in imagery that a player

should undertake. The reason, of course, is that there is more time.

Visualization, especially for the inexperienced, is a step-by-step

thinking process, and one not to be rushed. The act of playing the

game, however, does not afford the luxury of time-consuming

thought, so the service is an excellent place to get good at construc-

tive imagery. Here it is possible, for example, to stand at the line and bounce the ball several times and truly focus the attention of your mind on what you are trying to do. A more inexperienced

player will rush to the line in order to get the ball in play as soon as

possible. The thoughtful player will spend more effort in his prepa-

ration for serving and will realize the importance of beginning the

initiative of play.

When considering placement of the serve, it is most important

that the player realize that it is not done through any deviation in

the swing. Placement of the serve stems primarily from the stance of the body in relationship to the baseline. For example, with the

right-handed server directing his serve toward the outside corner of

the deuce court, his shoulder stance would be more open (more

facing the net although not absolutely so). As his serves move to the

remaining corners from left to right, his stance should close in a

corresponding manner. By the time he is serving toward the outside

corner of the add court, the farthest right corner at his disposal, his left shoulder should be turned more than at any other time. These

movements in body position are very often not terribly dramatic

and can be affected merely by moving the back foot. It is a small

change but a very significant one.

The specific imagery incorporated by the server, once these

particular positions are established, starts by focusing one’s mind

on a specific spot in the opposing court. Many more inexperienced

players will allow their target to be the entire service court and this

is a fundamental error. It is important to pick a small point and to

then intensely focus the eye on it, and through it, the mind. It is

through the power of the mind’s eye that precision placement

occurs. Before the ball is even tossed, there should be a clear image

in the mind of the ball and its flight as it passes precisely toward the

chosen target. It should be a smooth and ever-flowing execution

that puts the ball onto an already identified plane through space. It

is more than mere suggestion, it is the last word in constructive

imagery. It is from this most powerful and clear context that one

must then extend their learning of visualization to other parts of the

game that afford less time and opportunity than the serve.

THE VISION OF THE WINNING POINT

The creative mechanism within you its impersonal. It will work

automatically and impersonally to achieve goals of success and happiness, or unhappiness and failure, depending upon the goals which you yourself set for it. Present it ‘success goals’ and it func-

tions as a ‘success mechanism.’ Present it with negative goals and it

operates just as impersonally and just as faithfully as a ‘failure

For many tennis players one of the most imposing obstacles .n

the game is their inability to conclude a point. It is not uncommon

to witness long, hard-fought points end anticlimactically as a play-

er misses a comparatively easy shot for some unknown reason. To

win, aS opposed to your opponent losing, thereby awarding you a

victory, are two entirely different things. Winning necessarily

implies a constructive initiative be taken successfully to your op-

ponent. For your opponent to lose implies that an error was made,

thereby creating a default of sorts in your favor. As players learn

the game, and they become aware of the parallels between tennis and other games of life, they learn the importance of following

through, and actually finishing a given swing or point. Nothing in

the tennis game is more vulnerable to the negative association of

self-limiting imagery, and it is for this reason that I will now

describe a series of instructive visualizations for winning points.

One of the most commonly missed shots in tennis, one of the

most frequently witnessed non-forced errors, is the passing shot.

Many players intimidate themselves into attempting to make their

passing shots too good, and therefore make errors because of it.

This unconscious demand to make near-perfect shots results from a

negative image of the net man as a looming, all encompassing,

obstacle at the net. In most cases, the image of a giant at the net is

not a real fact of life, but rather an extension of the player’s

overactive imagination.

Let’s say, for example, that a long rally had preceded your

opponent’s rush to the net behind a drive hit to your backhand. A

negative, self-limiting visualization will form images that dem-

onstrate the ominous presence of the opponent, the very small

area available to hit the shot, the fact that you must hit it with your

‘‘awful backhand,’’ and the fact that you had worked so hard for

this point, all to have this happen—before you have even tried the

shot. This is self-limiting and self-fulfilling visualization. It is only

accidental to experience success in spite of mental obstructions like

these. The goal of the thoughtful player must be to eliminate

conditioned behavior by eradicating negative visualization as he

approaches such shots.

A constructive alternative in a situation like this might run as

follows. Assuming your opponent’s approach shot was hit to your

backhand, a positive visualization would include picturing in your

mind the literal tunnel of air space down the line past your approaching opponent. It should be enlightening and even exciting to

operate within the knowledge that there is a tunnel through which

you can pass the ball where it will never cross in front of your

opponent. This is why so many expert players elect to hit passing

shots down the sidelines when they are pulled wide, as opposed to

hitting across the court. You should feel your body moving in

constant rhythm on your right shoulder as you begin to press on

your right leg, and then uncoiling in a free form of movement and

expression that will guide the ball, almost willfully, into that

preconceived tunnel between the sideline and your opponent’s

reach.

Similar visualization can occur for either forehands or

backhands, and for passing shots hit either crosscourt or down the

line. The completely positive image, however, should always

remain essentially the same, and in that way it allows the player to

complete his response with the highest percentage of success, the

only variable being his own talent and how much investment he has

made in himself through training and specific skill development.

The power of the mind to use positive imagery and suggestion is

more sophisticated than we are even able to understand. It is a fact

of life that deserves, more than any, simply to be trusted. One must

first believe, and then one can operate.

THE PUT-AWAY VOLLEY AND OVERHEAD SMASH

It is not uncommon to see players in excellent position to win

points at net ‘“‘choke’’ or somehow blow it. They may have served

extremely well, or have approached perfectly behind a deep drive to

their opponent’s backhand. Yet, for some unexplained reason,

these same players who have just made very convincing and dif-

ficult shots, miss what appear to be otherwise routine volleys. The

key, of course, is that those easy shots must cross the psychological

bridge between either winning or losing the point. This pressure

complicates and makes these ‘‘easy shots’’ difficult indeed. Nega-

tive imagery may induce the player to consider his vulnerability by

being at the net, the fact that he will have so little time to respond to

the ball, or that he does not really know whether his opponent will

attempt to pass him down the line, or crosscourt, or even over his

head with a lob. This player’s mind will be racing so fast in his consideration of negative alternatives that he will be surprised at

whatever is hit by the opponent, and, for this reason, is very likely

to overreact, making his. shot too hard or ‘‘too good,’’ conse-

quently forcing himself into nonforced errors.

Positive visualization, on the other hand, will induce the player

to feel the power of his position as he nears the net. His awareness,

and the focus of his mind, should be on the increasing angle down

into the court he will have on his next shot. His mind should be

thinking of the pressure that his approach, as well as his mere

presence at the net, is putting on his opponent. He should be aware

of his command of the point, and he should be ready to decisively

conclude the good work he has already done. This should include a

very positive mind set and a self-image of himself as the ag-

gressor—not some shaky figure standing in a cold area of vulner-

ability and surprise.

This same system of positive visualization during the act of

playing the game is true for the overhead smash. Specific image

formation is required for identifying the arc of the ball. However,

the positive ‘‘attack of the game,’’ as well as the mental initiative of

winning the point, parallels that just described. The only difference

is that the overhead smash could possibly give the player a

maximum feeling of exhilaration and strength. Similarly, it is a shot

where the time involved in watching the ball drop allows the door

of negative assumption concerning success and failure to be

opened. Experience has shown that the most concentration and

intensity of mind is often required on ‘‘easy shots.’’ Smashing lobs

is a prime example of this.

Concluding a point with a successful drop shot is more than

saying to yourself, ‘‘Maybe I’ll try dinking it over.’’ The ‘‘I wonder

if I can, I think I’ll try it’? phenomenon will never work with drop

shots since marginal confidence will always be detected by your

opponent. The success factor of winning points with drop shots

necessitates that disguise be the most important element. In other

words, these shots must look exactly like a regular groundstroke,

and so must your expression.

Positive visualization while executing the drop shot is similar to

that of the passing shot inasmuch as one should be acutely aware of

an imaginary tunnel through which the ball must travel. In the case

of the drop shot, this tunnel should arc over the net in such a way as

to allow the ball to bounce as nearly straight up as possible when it

hits. This is opposed, of course, to the flight and bounce of a basic

drive where the ball bounces considerably toward the opponent.

The drop shot is a shot of finesse, touch, and feel. One should have

a mental image of the ball as it touches the strings and how, once

on the racquet, it is carried in such a way as to allow the racquet to

move gradually underneath the ball, thereby negating the forward

motion of the swing and functioning to apply backspin to the ball.

This backspin will cause the ball to float gingerly just over the net

and then bounce vertically rather than into your opponent’s rac-

quet.

The term ‘‘ace’’ is used in reference:to any serve hit so well that

the receiver does not touch it with his racquet. This obviously

striking shot presents tennis’ most perfect opportunity for positive

visualization. As mentioned previously with respect to the service

motion, maximum time is afforded the player when serving to

concentrate his mental energies to a specific place, a precise target.

This element of time is of critical importance because for many

players this extra preparation allows them to focus more power into

their vision than in ordinary exchanges. This is especially true with

professional players since it is not uncommon to see them serving

extremely well when they are down, say 15-40 or love-40. In those

cases, the score demands that their attention be focused, and that is

always a time they will concentrate the most. In professional-level

tennis, the implications of the service break are too important to

ignore.

The power of the positive image must begin in a very determined

mind set. Fear of double faulting, and thereby losing a critical

point, always rests on the opposite side of this scale. The

aggressively determined player with a strong service at his disposal will always be most likely to serve his best when he needs it the

most. It is not uncommon to see very good serves when players are

in danger of losing their service game. It is at this time that the

server wil! bounce the ball several more times than usual, pausing

to focus his attention more purely on that certain spot in the service

court, and to become more acutely aware of that magic tunnel of

air space between his extended racquet and the selected destination

of the ball. The service ace is the most deliberate and planned-out

initiative of winning vision in the game of tennis. It is dramatic and

beautiful, but it is only one part of positive imagination, and it can

be extended to every other shot or movement on the court, and

even to the strategy of the game as a whole. Serving lends itself well

to study because it is isolated, but the same mental gymnastics can

be applied to every other part of the game.

Similar in many ways to the drop shot, the winning lob is most

obviously an act of déception. The negative counterpart to positive

Amby was a boy six years old with carrot-top red hair, and it

was a sunny day when he and his mother and Babaloo Bamboo

arrived at the tennis club for his (their) first tennis lesson. No

one else, including Amby’s mother, knew that Babaloo was

being included, of course, because he was invisible, and only an

“‘imaginary’’ figure according to all of the grownups like

Amby’s mother.

Amby was anxious to find the tennis pro as soon as they

entered the crowded tennis shop. He had heard a lot about tennis pros and had even seen some on television before, but he

had never seen one in real life. When they were standing behind

the counter, Amby could not see who his mother was talking to,

but as soon as he stood on his tip-toes he knew right away that it

was the pro. Babaloo anxiously perched himself on Amby’s back

so that he could see, too, and they were both so excited when

they realized who it was that they bumped their heads trying to

get the best view. They had both waited a long time to become

six so that they could learn about tennis, so seeing their pro for

the first time was a really big deal.

The pro was not unusually big but was a man of about

medium height and a bit thin. He talked in a soft, almost

southern voice, wore all white tennis clothes, and had a dark tan

from all of the hours he spent in the sun. He even wore a floppy Australian tennis hat, and Amby felt that his pro looked just the

way the ‘‘real thing’’ was supposed to look.

As soon as Amby’s mother finished her conversation, the pro

led Amby and Babaloo onto the patio where they were to meet

the rest of Amby’s group. When the pro walked out, everyone

seemed to know who he was, and they all got in line quickly,

exactly the way they were supposed to. All of the children

silently followed their new teacher to a special enclosed teaching

court. There was no fooling around and it was clear from the

very beginning that everyone else in the class was just as anxious

to learn as Amby and Babaloo were. All of the parents said that

Heggie, the pro, looked like the Pied Piper, and many of the

grownups even stopped their games to watch as the children

marched by.

Once everyone was assembled on the court, Heggie’s first

instruction was to line up on the back line so that the roll could

be called. Amby was not afraid, and he did not feel un-

comfortable out there, not only because he brought Babaloo

with him, but because all of the other children appeared to be

about his same age and height. Everyone was still very quiet and

they all listened patiently for their names to be called—Kacki,

Kippi, Cuzzie, Weezie, Daniel, Leland, Jamie, Mark, Christi,

Stephanie, Tonya, Christa, Yancey, Ty, and Amby. That

numbered fifteen in all, not counting Babaloo, of course, but

Heggie couldn’t have been expected to know that he was there.

The first thing their group learned that day was how to hold

their tennis racquets properly. Heggie circled all the children

around him and showed each player precisely how to shake

hands with his racquet. He kidded them about becoming friends

with their racquets, but he made sure that each one of them

understood that there was a very specific ‘‘right’’ way to hold his

racquet, and that their thumbs should always be around the

racquet and not sitting on the top like so many of the little hands wanted to do. Everyone seemed to understand ““shaking

hands’’ with his racquet and they all learned that that was called

the eastern forehand grip.

The next item of business that day was to learn what Heggie

called the ‘‘ready position.’’ He explained that the game of

tennis required hitting several shots in a row, and in order to do

that the player needed to always start the same way. Heggie bent

over slightly at the waist, bent his knees as if he were getting

ready to run, and he held his racquet at its throat with his left

hand. He really looked the way tennis players were supposed to,

and the whole group was really excited because they were all able

to imitate him so well. Even Babaloo Bamboo was impressed by

the perfectly straight line of fifteen ideal ready positions.

After it became clear that everyone knew the ready position,

Heggie circled his group around him as he talked to them. He

told them about a shot called volley, and how they would be expected to hit this shot in the air before the ball bounced. He

described to them how they would stand very close to the net

great deal of time talking to his children about the ball, and how

it had seams on it and how they should try and focus their eyes

on them. He told them about how the ball travels through the air, and how each of them should try to identify what he called

the ‘‘flight of the ball.”’ ; It all seemed like such an exciting exercise as the group lined

net. Babaloo Bamboo was so proud of Amby because he was

able to hit his very first ball right in the middle of the racquet,

and that was such a great way to start learning. All of the

children learned the forehand volley that day and every single

one of them smiled and couldn’t wait to tell his parents how well

he had done. Some of the children had been afraid and had not

known whether or not they could learn. For these children their

first tennis lesson was especially rewarding. It felt so good to

succeed. Heggie had a way of keeping things really simple. He

pointed out only those most essential elements and he taught

every child there how to focus his mind on a specific point, even if it were just for a very short time. The children didn’t know

those things, of course, but Babaloo Bamboo did and he was

VISUALIZATION, by definition, implies a picture in the mind.

This, however, is not the end of the line for such an important

human capacity. While specific physical tasks, like the tennis

performances just described, are a realm more obvious, there is a

spiritual extension of man which can legitimately be used as a

vehicle for more abstract conceptualization. It is possible to

visualize in the most positive way, not only specific physical acts,

but ideas as well. This notion, that man’s imagination can have far-

reaching effects on his life, is important to the building of a strong

self-image. This, in turn, becomes everyone’s vehicle for moving

effectively through life while maintaining consistent over-all

health. The ways in which positive conceptualization can be used to

enhance our lives, and the fact that thought and physical reality are

related, is not new. It has, however, experienced a renaissance in

recent years, and that is the subject of this discussion.

The use of visualization to cure disease dates long before the

advent of modern science, and it is conceded by many to be the

most ancient healing procedure used by primitive man. There have

been records of these early techniques found on ancient cuneiform

These earliest concepts of healing center, of course, around

belief in spirits, gods, and magical figures, and they typically in-

volved an exorcism by a ‘‘shaman,”’ that special person believed to

have an unusual contact with the positive forces of healing and

good health. The specifics of their ceremonies varied, but the

shaman’s vehicle for reaching the spiritual world always involved a

visualization technique of some sort, whether it was through

dreams, visions, or some other more distinctly mystical experience.

At the same time this shamanistic method of healing was

evolving, so was a more subjective philosophy that allowed for

unrestricted individual visualization, irrespective of any sha-

manistic affiliation. This tradition can be historically traced to

the Hermetic philosophers of Egypt, to Platonic philosophy in

Greece, as well as to the evolution of Buddhists and Hindus in

India and the Orient. These interesting philosophies held that there

was a universal spiritual center to the world and that it could be

tapped by the individual through specialized techniques of

meditation and visualization.

These philosophies believed in the primacy of spirit over matter, of

mind over body; they believed that matter is a manifestation of the spirit. They believed that visualizations manifest themselves as

After that time, it became clear that in many ways science and

religion were mutually exclusive. There is, for example, no rational

explanation for any religion. Religion must be accepted on faith

alone, for it is simply man’s way of justifying to himself all things

that are unexplainable. The two disciplines inevitably grew away

from each other, with healing through drug therapy and surgery

becoming especially established in the tradition of Western man.
