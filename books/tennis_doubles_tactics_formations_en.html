You make a sliced serve by throwing the ball somewhat

ahead of your right shoulder and slicing round it. At the

moment of contact between ball and strings you feel your elbow

is about fully straightened and you feel you are hitting down

and round the ball—like throwing an out-curve.

You make a topspin serve by throwing the ball above your

head and bending your elbow sharply so that you have enough

force to strike the ball with the strings of a racket head that is

still rising (to put forward roll or topspin on the ball). At the

moment of contact between ball and strings you feel your elbow

is still in the process of straightening itself.

You make a topspin-sliced serve by throwing the ball not

as far forward and to the right as for a sliced serve, and by hitting the ball with a feeling that your elbow is still to some

extent uncoiling itself.

A flat serve has less margin for error than any other. Not being sliced or topspun, it does not curve or dip appreciably.

Following a more direct line, after crossing the net it has the shortest possible space in which to land in court and therefore can have but little safe net clearance. Its main characteristic is

Returning from the high plane of cannonball serving to an everyday level, the right court is the Danger Court. There are more game points in the left court, but you are more aware of these. If you lose the points when serving to the right court

you are always recovering on the following ones, always trying

to save 0-30 or 15-40 or game.

You need to be able to protect yourself from a situation where

a right-court receiver with a strong forehand takes your

service, particularly the second one, by the scruff of the neck and continually puts you a point down. Your basic need is the ability to land your second service deep to the receiver’s backhand, while serving from at least halfway out to the sideline.

Do not regard this as a defensive serve away from his fore-

hand or you will fall into the wrong frame of mind. Make your

serve a firm attack on his backhand. There’s all the difference

in the world.

Whatever your intentions, wherever your aim, wherever you

throw the ball—watch it. Keep your head up.

If you can hold the points in the right court you will handle

those in the left court well enough. You have the confidence of

being a point ahead and also it is much easier to find your

opponent’s backhand in this court. Your easily made diagonal

serve goes to it. You can land your topspin serve into the left

court backhand corner with less strain than anywhere else.

However, this assumes that your opponents are about equal

in strength. Often they are not, and then the stronger one

usually plays in the left court, from where he can control more of the game. You should remember the Danger Court and not

let points unnecessarily slip away from you when serving to the

weaker right-court partner. A girl serving in mixed doubles

should be aware of this from the first point. She may carelessly

lose it and not recover, the score going love—30, 15-30, 15-40, 30-40, game. This sounds male chauvinistic, with the male

receiver too good to be true, but it often happens that way.

A left-court receiver’s usual return will be to your backhand

side, so you should protect this area as much as possible by

serving from rather more than halfway out to the doubles

sideline. Serving from farther out also gives you a wider angle

to the receiver’s backhand, making it more difficult for him to

step round it and play a forehand. If he lobs over your partner’s

head, you will have a longer chase than necessary, but this time

the ball will be on your forehand side.

If your opponent plays in the left court for the main reason that his forehand is weak (which you should be able to notice during the warm-up or soon after) you can serve to it from

nearer the center mark. Otherwise center-mark serving sets up a receiver’s forehand attack to your backhand, and that is a poor tactic on your part.

Second guessing? Serving from the center mark but to his backhand? This allows him to play a crosscourt backhand wide of your own backhand. You mean surprising him the first

time with a backhand so that your net partner could intercept for a winner, and next time aceing him down the center? Well,

the surprise serve to the backhand might work, but the intended ace down the center is not likely to surprise him. When a server stands close to the center mark, the receiver always feels threatened by a center-line ace and instinctively guards against it. If your center-line serve does not inconvenience the

receiver, your backhand side is widely exposed and you are in a poor position. As server, you had the choice of a better one.

Watching doubles play from high to low you won’t see many players serving from near the center mark, and most will serve from farther out on the left side than they do from the right.

You beat him in singles and now he’s dominating you in

There is. Play Tandem. Call it Tandem too, a short

descriptive word, and regard it as a normal part of your

doubles game to call for and use when needed. To call it the Australian Formation is too remote. In Australia it is regarded

as being an American formation, so neither country has a

mortgage on it.

To play Tandem (meaning one behind the other) your net partner stands on the same side of the court as you do. In this position he blocks the left-hander’s two natural service

returns: crosscourt forehand and off-side backhand. You stand right beside the center mark, intending to swing a sliced serve down the center line to his backhand. After that you will deal with his return, which you can expect to be weaker than it was before. However, as soon as you have served you must move at once to cover your totally exposed forehand side. Serve and

move—just as if you were serving and taking the net in one co-

ordinated movement.

You do this—and the left-hander, from backhand (or forehand, if your serve did not find his backhand) hits the ball down the middle of the court between you and your partner into unguarded territory and scores a clean winner. What’s

gone wrong this time? It’s the net man. See Illus. 8.

Since you will be moving to your right to cover the exposed

right side of the court, it is the net man who must protect the center. To have a chance of doing so he must begin by

standing much nearer the center than usual, at least halfway

between the singles sideline and the center line. He also needs

to stand farther back than before, at least halfway back from

the net to the service line, to give himself more time to move.

A net man may feel exposed and vulnerable in this position,

but Tandem is not a natural and comfortable formation for the

server either. All told you may feel you’re going to a lot of fuss

and bother. On the other hand, it is the best tactic to contain

an otherwise dominating left-hander. With plenty of practice

beforehand you can apply it at will in a match.

Keep Tandem in mind not only for left-handers but also for

any left-court right-hander with an over-developed backhand

and an under-developed forehand. Many two-handed players

are like that.

Though rallies in doubles are seldom as prolonged as they

are in singles, a certain amount of baseline rallying does take place. It usually lasts longer from right side to right court,

forehand to forehand.

Unless your opponent’s return of service has been so short

and high that you can knock the ball for a winner past his net

partner or almost clean through him, you soon come to realize you would rather this net partner were standing back (Two Back receiving) instead of being positioned at the net (One Up One Back receiving). A receiving Two Back formation allows

the server much more scope. From a weak to fair service return,

or awkward return you haven’t got a threatening opposing

If you have a stronger forehand than your baseline opponent,

your tactics are to wear him down. Do not regard this as un-

imaginative, because getting on top in a forehand driving

exchange is never dull. If his forehand is better than yours,

your tactics are to break up any driving exchange as soon as possible, before it is too late.

The preferred first shot in this intended break-up is a lob over

the opposing net man’s head. That’s doing more than avoiding

the driving exchange, it’s attacking. If you aren’t on the lookout

to play this shot at the first opportunity you may not get a

second chance. You are likely to receive the worst of the driving

exchange and be forced to lob defensively. The net man then

has the initiative, whether he chooses to take one or more bites at the cherry.

You're the receiver in a high-standard game where both net
