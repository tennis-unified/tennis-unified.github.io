Section 1

Another reason why singles overshadowed doubles is related to the players themselves and their feelings about the game of doubles. One contention is that the pros did not enjoy doubles and participated in doubles only because of tournament format and

obligations. This is true to some extent, but the players’ preferences were dictated by other factors. Doubles wins did not bring the

status necessary to have tournament entries accepted. Nor did doubles wins bring the greater prize money. In fact, an early-round

singles loss brought more money than a late-round doubles win. Why get tired from a tough doubles match and take a chance on losing a later singles match? After a first- or second-round singles defeat, why not move on to the next competition for acclimatization and practice rather than stay for a less profitable and less prestigious doubles match?

A third reason why doubles lagged behind is that, with the exception of a very few players like Billie Jean King, the superstars of singles are not superstars of doubles. Singles and doubles are two different games, and many players understand and favor one over

the other. However, a highly acclaimed singles champion like Chris Evert is extremely popular even though she is not a brilliant doubles player. On the other hand, a less well-known champion of doubles like Frew McMillan does not command the same attention because he is not a singles giant.

The influx of big money into professional tennis and the tremendous surge in the popularity of tennis created the conditions necessary to boost doubles to a better balance with singles.

Consider the days of amateur tennis of the past. Doubles was considered the domain of weekend players—it was not real tennis.

In tournaments it was relegated to the back courts, and singles

scheduling took priority. Perhaps a few spectators would stop by for some light entertainment.

Compare this with the situation today. Ever-increasing demand for spectator tennis and ever-increasing available capital have encouraged extending the tournament circuits into the bad weather months. Enclosed arenas have been built—arenas with one court. By necessity all play is set on the same stage. Voila! Doubles has its audience; it is no longer set out of sight. You can witness the drama of gut-busting, arm-stretching doubles—serves that bob like elusive apples, shotgun volleys, feather-duster touch shots, angles that stretch players parallel to the ground, lobs to the wall returned with contorted backfiring, drop shots that tease an opponent into the desperate effort to make the return before the ball dies at his or her feet. And you love it!

Doubles players now have not only an enthusiastic audience and more recognition than they had before, but also more financial reward. Better prize money is being awarded. Grand Prix points for the men and International series points for the women designate top teams for extra compensation, and the Mixed Doubles Championship on television in the spring has become a major winter event.

In nonprofessional tennis doubles has become the answer to the staggering demand for courts, a demand brought on by the tennis

boom. Many facilities—public and private—have eased court availability during the warm weather months by requiring doubles during prime time—a very palatable solution for tennis enthusiasts. If you live in an area where bad weather months prohibit playing outdoors, doubles provides the answer for the otherwise prohibitive cost of using indoor courts.

Just as more and more spectators enjoy watching doubles, so do

more and more players enjoy playing doubles. If you are a beginner, novice doubles allows you to concentrate on one discipline at a time—service, net, and so on. If you are an advanced player, you may still prefer one-on-one confrontation, but hopefully you are learning to appreciate the challenge of playing in a team situation and working for the power, placement, and movement that blend to make a doubles team.

Now that you see how the alleys of the doubles court increase the possibilities of angles, surrounding playing area, crosscourt shots, and flight time, you should become more aware of the role these factors play in the tactics and strategy of doubles.

As I mentioned before, singles and doubles are two very different games. Let us define their differences in terms of the strokes that are the tools of both games. By understanding the possibilities and limitations of each stroke, you will be better able to understand how and why the tactics and strategy of using these strokes separate one game from another.

Tennis popularity started to grow rapidly during the era of the serve-and-volley game. We have all been impressed with the

rocket-like serves of pros like Stan Smith, Arthur Ashe, and Roscoe Tanner. It is therefore understandable that many amateur players consider power the key to a successful serve.

However, as I’ll now show, in neither singles nor doubles does the serve have to be powerful to be effective. Nor should the serve be the sole offensive weapon in either game. I have managed to stay in the top ten among women players in the world for more

than fifteen years with a serve that Bud Collins has dubbed “pittypat.” It is so slow that in a fast-serve contest in Washington, D. C., my serve never moved the needle on the machine used to judge the contest.

Consider first the serve in singles. There are three basic strategies in singles: serve-and-volley, backcourt, or an all-court mixture of the two. Each strategy has a different serving requirement. The following guidelines might help you to determine your strategy and your service tactics according to the weaknesses and strengths of your opponent:

- If you see that your opponent needs a net target for a successful return or can only reflex a ball back, do not play serveand-volley. This strategy will only make up for his or her limitations rather than take advantage of them. A steady backcourt game and slow, twisting serves will frustrate him or her out of the match.

* You may find that a hard, wide serve is an asset for a weaker, less experienced opponent who desperately swings late and thereby unintentionally passes you down the line. If you then change your tactic to a one-half-speed serve, he or she will probably hit everything out.

* If your opponent is weak-armed and only blocks balls back, serve wide to either side. He or she will be stretched far and will not have the strength for a successful return.

* If his or her footwork is poor and preparation slow, serve threequarter speed into the body.

* No matter what the quality of your opponent, keep him or her off balance by varying the speed, twist, and spin of your serve.

* If you are losing, try deep, soft first serves to the backhand.

Unless your opponent is having even more success, keep up the tactic. Over a period of time the pressure of your reduced errors

and the constant attack on his or her backhand will take its toll. Your opponent will have to work for each point and will lose momentum. You will be forcing your opponent to win each point instead of losing it yourself.

In doubles there is only one basic strategy: Get your first serve in and go to the net as fast as you can. You cannot afford to stay on

the baseline while your partner is at net. Such team-splitting gives your opponents the opportunity to close the net and gain the offen-

sive. See Diagram 6. Suppose you serve and do not follow your serve to the net. The receiver feels no pressure when he returns and approaches the net (Ri > Re). The receiving team now has a

united offensive position at the net. Each of them has two different easy-volley areas—a total of four easy-volley areas—open because your team has neither a united offensive position nor a united defensive position. You have lost the advantage of serving.

Although you use the big serve to get to the net in singles, you do not use it to get to the net in doubles. Why not?

The Hard First Serve in Singles

In singles a fast, wide, or jamming first serve weakens your opponent’s return and thereby allows you to move up and maintain the offensive. You can take the first volley as far back as between the baseline and the service line and still play the ball safely to an opening in the court far enough away from the receiver to allow you time to move even closer to the net.

More important, should your first serve be a fault, you need not follow your second serve to the net. You have the option of waiting for a strong return of your second serve at the baseline and lobbing or passing if your opponent rushes to the net. You therefore do not have the pressure of getting in your first serve and can afford to strive for the big first serve.

The Hard First Serve in Doubles

In doubles, however, to gain the time to get into a strong position at the net—an absolute must after your serve in doubles— the same big serve must be at least 70 percent successful in producing a weak return from the receiver.

Think what happens if both your serve and the receiver’s return are hard and fast: Your netman’s time and ability to intimidate

the receiver with the threat of poaching is reduced. Your own time to move to the net is reduced, and you must make your first volley—probably a weak one—just inside the baseline. The receiver’s netman will probably intercept your weak volley, and your

netman will not have time to cover the interception. The receiver will close the net before you do, and your opponents will take command of the net.

Also, in doubles your percentage of first serves in is critical. Unlike when serving in singles, you do not have the option of staying back on the second serve. If you do stay back, you will face the same set of circumstances I just described, and the receiver will close the net before you do. In fact, with the second serve, the receiver can take a bolder, more offensive stance. Knowing that your second serve will not be a power serve, he or she will move a step or two closer to the net. There he or she will be in a better position to aim for wider angles and to perhaps test out your netman. Again, the receiver has a better opportunity of closing the net than you do.

The Most Effective Doubles Serve

If the hard, fast serve is not the most effective serve for doubles, what is? One approach to this question is to consider what returns of serve are most effective in doubles and from there determine what serve will prevent or frustrate such returns.

In singles your opponent’s best return of your serve is one that is

deep and wide, thereby forcing you to move laterally and slowing your advance to the net. You therefore serve with all the slice, spin, and power you can muster in order to limit the receiver’s opportunity to pass you and make you move to the side rather than up.

In doubles, however, your netman’s ability to cover half the court at the net reduces the possibility of effective lateral returns.

Your opponent’s best return is at your feet as you come in to the

net after serving—a very difficult shot to handle. To counteract the effectiveness of this kind of return, use a three-quarter-speed serve with some twist to allow yourself time to get in close enough for the net to actually protect your feet. The added twist on your serve may unbalance the receiver enough to weaken the return and thereby

give you more opportunity to make an effective volley. Billy Jean King says that my serve is so slow and well-placed that I’m standing at the net before she gets a chance to hit the return.

I discussed the return of serve briefly in the previous section, but let us try to define further the differences between the returns of serve in singles and doubles. Imagine now that you are the receiver and consider how to most effectively neutralize the advantage of the server by your return.

Area of Return. As I pointed out earlier, the area of return in

singles is much less restricted than in doubles because you have only one opponent. You are able to return deep and wide and thereby to pull the server to the side and impede his or her advance to the net.

Stroke of Return. In singles you also have the time to execute a normal groundstroke as a return. If your opponent comes to net after a big serve, you have a good possibility of passing him or her with an equally powerful groundstroke. If your opponent does not come to the net (there is no pressure in singles to do so), then your strong return at least will somewhat reduce his or her offensive potential.

In fact your return might feel so strong or well-placed that you can follow your return to the net and thereby gain the offensive position. Or you can stay at the baseline and use deep groundstrokes to keep your opponent at the baseline until you feel you have placed a ball so well that your opponent will not be able to pass you if you come to the net. Once you come to the net, however, you must try to win the point as soon as possible, because it is difficult to cover the entire net yourself for more than one or two volleys.

In doubles as well as in singles, the server, because he starts play, controls the offensive position. Your return should be an attempt

to neutralize that advantage. The key elements of an effective return in doubles are placement and quickness.

Area of Return. Remember that your area of return is restricted

because the server’s netman can intercept returns on at least one half of the court. You must return, therefore, in the direction of the incoming server. However, the return must be one that he or she

cannot easily volley away for a winner. A return at the server’s feet with a troublesome bounce or drop of the ball will frustrate and weaken his or her ability to place a strong volley and thereby give you a stand-off—another chance to play the ball and hopefully to further reduce his or her initial advantage as server.

The most popular stroke to achieve this ideal return is the chip shot. In this stroke you hit the ball softly and with underspin for control. The ball drops quickly and dies on the bounce.

Stroke of Return. In singles you know that the server might move in behind the serve and volley from approximately the service line. In doubles you know that the server wz// move in and volley from well within the service line. Also, in doubles you are more likely to move to the net yourself after your return. You therefore cannot afford

the time, as in singles, to stand back and execute a powerful groundstroke with exaggerated backswing and followthrough as a return. Such exaggeration affords the server more time to move in tighter to the net and gives you less time to follow your return to

the net. Instead, you must stand in as close as possible to receive and use a groundstroke return with abbreviated backswing and shortened, firm-wristed followthrough. The quickness of this kind of return shortens the time the server has to get to the net and increases your time to follow your return to the net.

Groundstrokes are basically for backcourt play, and are used in singles much more than in doubles. You keep your opponent back with deep corner-to-corner placements that barely clear the net.

In doubles you cannot afford the time to execute the full backswing and followthrough of baseline groundstrokes. Before you even recover from such a stroke, your opponents will be at the

net and will volley away your shot. To save time, you shorten your backswing and followthrough and take the ball above the net. To avoid or frustrate your opponent’s volley, you hit down at an

opponent’s feet or make a soft shot that dies just as it clears the net. You thereby force your opponent to hit up. Then you can make the putaway shot down.

If, however, your team is forced to the baseline, you can use groundstrokes as a defensive measure against your opponents at the net. Hit these groundstrokes with extra topspin so that the ball stays low, dips as it clears the net, and therefore is difficult for your opponents to handle. To hit topspin, keep your racquet face vertical and brush up—bottom to top—on the ball as it leaves your racquet to give it spin in the direction it is traveling. The spin of the ball makes the ball difficult to volley. And if it bounces, it usually hops and gains pace.

Or, given the same situation of being at the baseline with your opponents at net, you also can try a power play with groundstrokes—that is, hitting two or three flat, hard balls to intimidate your opponents into taking a step or two back from the net, and then hitting a topspin shot that will drop at their feet.

The lob is primarily a defensive stroke in both singles and doubles. By sending the ball high, you can gain time to recover after being pulled out of position by a well-placed shot. Or you can use the lob to open the net.

Consider the following possibilities of the lob in doubles:

* A good lob, by giving you the opportunity to move up as your opponents retreat to retrieve, may reverse a situation where your team is at the baseline and your opponents are at the net.

* A quick lob volley over your opponents’ heads may even catch 20

them flat-footed and turn out to be a winner for you. However, use this tactic only if your opponents are so tight at the net that you have relatively few options. When the ball is above the net, it is always best to volley downward, but the lob volley is an occasional answer for the ball below the net.

- A lob over the head of the server’s netman as a return of serve can unbalance the net attack of the server and thereby give your team the opportunity to reduce the server’s advantage.

In singles you must earn the net with placements that will not allow your opponent to make passing shots. And once you are at the net, you must win the point as soon as possible because it becomes increasingly more difficult for you to cover the entire net yourself once you have made your first or second volley.

In doubles you control the net as server by virtue of the starting position of your netman. He is able to cover 50 percent of the net before you even serve the ball, and if your serve is good, you can close the other 50 percent almost before the ball reaches the

receiver. As in singles, once you have control of the net, you should win the point as soon as possible to prevent your opponents from gaining a stand-off position that could possibly weaken your advantage.

Dominating the net does not mean that you should limit your offense to power volleys. Like big serves, big net shots poorly

played often beget even faster returns that do not give you enough time to recover and prepare. Early interception and solid placement, not just power, are the keys to good net play. However, do not hesitate to hit out when you know you have a good setup. Even so, always be prepared for a return.

The antithesis of the lob, and of comparable value, is the overhead. The overhead, or smash, requires a blend of power and placement. Like the serve, it is an offensive shot that, if played incorrectly, can produce a backlash rather than a winner.

In singles, placement, and not power, of the overhead is of primary importance. Because your opponent has his or her entire court to cover, you usually can win your point by simply hitting the ball where he or she is not.

In doubles, because you must hit through two opponents, you have less chance of placing the ball where someone is not. Power is more important than placement. Also, because your opponents will be fairly close to the net, a hard smash will reduce their reflex time.

The half-volley is a technique to handle a low ball that bounces before a volley is possible, but not soon enough to allow a normal groundstroke. The ball is taken at the bounce with no backswing, but with a short, sweeping-up followthrough to direct and lift the ball.

In singles you half-volley down the line to stretch your opponent wide if he or she is at the net and keep your opponent deep in the corner if he or she is at the baseline.

In doubles you half-volley down the line, or if an opponent is covering the line, you half-volley crosscourt to the feet of your other opponent.

Remember that the half-volley is rarely a winner. It is a difficult shot to execute, but if you are successful in simply playing it back, you can hope to close in immediately and make the easier volley.

The approach shot is not a specific stroke. It is a groundstroke, a volley, or a half-volley taken inside the baseline and followed to the net.

In singles, place your approach shot deep down the line. Because a down-the-line ball must have an appreciable angle of rise to clear the net, a medium-pace ball will travel upward after clearing the net. Your opponent not only will be stretched to reach the ball, but

also will have to withstand the power of a ball hit straight ahead. He or she will be forced into making a weak defensive shot or attempting a low-percentage winner.

In doubles you should not go down the line on an approach shot. If you do, one of your opponents will close the line and volley away the rising ball. Instead, follow the general rule of a crosscourt approach shot fo the feet of your opponent. A medium-pace crosscourt shot usually has a gentle angle of rise so that the ball drops just as it clears the net. Your opponent must hit up, and you have a good chance of winning the point with a strong volley.

Occasionally the netman directly opposite you will anticipate

the crosscourt shot and will move from his or her position to intercept it. In this case a short, easy shot down the line may prove quite effective.

The drop shot is effective in singles to make an outright winner, to tire your opponent, to force a strong backcourt player forward to play a weak net game, or simply to catch your opponent off balance and set up an opening.

The drop shot in itself does not have the same effectiveness in doubles that it does in singles. But if you modify it to a soft angle shot, you increase its possibilities in doubles. Soft angle shots skim the net and drop quickly so that your opponents have little opportunity for making forceful plays. More important, an opponent is forced to hit up. If he tries to hit with power, the ball will carry high enough above the net for you or your partner to volley it away for a winner. Your opponent would be wise to answer your soft, short angle with another soft, short angle.

My hope is that this review of strokes and of the differences in tactics and strategy of using them in singles and doubles has made it evident that strokes are your tools for building a good game. You must learn to execute each stroke and shot separately before you can attempt to progress to an integrated all-court game of either singles or doubles.

For the amateur, the progression to good tennis is a straight line.

Your service is most effective if you vary the speed and spin of each serve and thereby keep the receiver off balance. To accomplish such differences in delivery, change your racquet grip and

your ball toss, not the speed of your stroke. Your racquet grip and ball toss determine the angle of your racquet face when it hits the ball, and it is the angle of your racquet face that determines how much of the power of your stroke will be distributed to velocity of the ball and how much to spin of the ball.

There are three standard serves—flat, slice, and twist. A fourth serve—the American twist—is a variation of the twist.

For the flat serve, use the forehand grip (usually the eastern or shake-hands grip) and a forward toss.

The advantage of the serve is its speed, its potential for overpowering the receiver.

This same speed, however, often backfires to become a disadvantage because it shortens your net approach time. See page 16 for a discussion of a fast serve in doubles. The Serve and Control of the Net

Control of the net is the goal of both the serving and receiving teams, and the serve is extremely important in determining which team will be able to close the net first.

As I pointed out in foregoing sections, the speed, slice, spin, and twist of the ball are not the only factors contributing to the effectiveness of your serve. Your serving position, the angle of your

serve, and your serving target are instrumental in not only eliciting weak returns, but also stretching the receiver laterally or otherwise impeding his or her forward approach after the return so that you can close the net before your opponent does.

How the Server Moves to the Net

Your forward movement to the net begins with the followthrough of the serve itself. The downward swing or tail end of the followthrough brings your shoulders and hips forward, and your back foot steps out to balance your body for a continuous, even acceleration. An erratic toss, an unrhythmic motion, a stiff wrist, or overhitting will hamper the followthrough and impede your approach to the net.

To take advantage of the acceleration and balance generated by a good followthrough, stay low for easy change of direction, but not so low as to impede speed. Take full strides, keep your shoulders down for balance, and extend your hands to enable you to maneuver your racquet quickly and to hit the ball as early as possible.

When the receiver’s racquet starts forward, shorten your steps to stutter steps—short, quick, forward-moving steps—to allow you to move laterally quickly if necessary but to still keep your forward momentum for early interception of the ball.

Continue moving while volleying to enable you to keep longer control of the ball on your racquet and to close the net more tightly rather than stopping, volleying, and starting up again.

The Role of the Server’s Netman

The effectiveness of your netman is also dependent upon your serve. A good serve allows him to overplay his area—move beyond his position—and intimidate the receiver. A poor serve may make him a target, thereby limiting his intimidation and leaving you with large lateral areas and hard returns to handle yourself. Given average serves, your netman should play conservatively and rely upon the advantage of your starting position as server to win the points.

The key to effective service is to get your first serve in with a variety of speed, spin, twist, angle, and target that will probe and capitalize on the receiver’s weaknesses in movement, racquet preparation, footwork, and forehand and backhand returns.

Like the serve, the return of serve is a crucial segment of each point. The serving team is now vulnerable because the server has not yet closed the net, and the receiving team must get the ball back in order to even play.

There seem to be two possible ways to play the return. One is to attack the vulnerability of the serving team; the other is to just get

the ball back. Actually, both of these are one and the same. Getting the ball back in itself attacks the serving team’s vulnerability, and getting it back consistently undermines the server’s confidence.

However, do not confuse getting the ball back with playing it safe. Playing it safe is a negative approach. If you are too

concerned with control, your stroke may become stiff; if you are

too intent on relaxing, your hand or wrist may become too limp; and if you are afraid of overhitting, you may tend to hold back on a full followthrough. Think more in terms of a positive approach— of hitting out. You may make some errors, but you will be using a natural stroke that eventually will work for you.

Taking the Ball in Front of Your Body

When you take the ball in front of your body, you gain three distinct advantages in return of serve: you can maintain your balance, you can utilize your peripheral vision, and you can take one or two steps closer to the net.

Only your stroke should influence the ball. A shift of weight or incorrect muscle sequence at the moment of impact can upset the return.

If such improper body use is a matter of habit, then your return will be consistently erratic. Should this be the case, work with an instructor or coach to break the habit and rebuild a basic technique. Do not be afraid of change, of relearning in order to improve. To make a correction, slow down the stroke and intellectualize the muscle memory—that is, keep track of the entire stroke until the new correction has been incorporated. If your racquet moves more quickly than your mind, then the old muscle memory re-introduces itself and the old error reappears.

Building up mental awareness takes as much practice as developing physical abilities. In fact, mental development must be in proportion to physical development. Both the amateur social player and the professional player must maintain a certain level of mental discipline in order to realize the complete physical dimension of their game. See Diagram 19.

The professional player’s level of mental involvement, of course, is very high. On return of serve he should be able to concentrate on such physical considerations as the execution of his return stroke, where and how to place the return, and when and how to approach the net. The beginner, on the other hand, is able to concentrate only on getting the ball back by keeping it in front of the body and using the simplest stroke possible.

If you wish to use such effects as spin and speed, study them separately and use them only as options, not as standard high-percentage returns.

Whatever you do, do not expend so much mental energy on the idea of winning that you fail to concentrate on the moment-tomoment play of the game.

Use an Abbreviated Groundstroke

Just before the wrist snap of the server, make a slight hop to bring your weight to the balls of your feet. When you determine the direction of the ball, keep your hands low, pivot your shoulders, and make a short backswing. You will develop power by

meeting the ball with a solid forward swing and utilizing the power of the serve itself. During your forward swing, keep the ball on the strings of your racquet long enough to impart direction, speed, and spin. What you are doing, basically, is abbreviating (or condensing) a groundstroke.

Do not worry about getting the lower half of your body—your hips and feet—around; you do not have enough time. Preparing your upper body—turning your shoulders, arms, and racquet as one unit—is sufficient to hit solidly through the ball. Your hips will turn slightly with your shoulders anyway, and your left foot eventually will step naturally with the followthrough. If your shoulders, hips, and feet cannot support the followthrough, then sheer arm muscle and mind will take over.

Many good players return an opponent’s serve well with no more than a jab. The jab offers a definite advantage to the

receiver—it eliminates the need for a backswing. You can take the ball on the rise and very soon after the bounce, sometimes almost like a half-volley. The quick return so reduces the server’s approach time that he or she must make the first volley from behind the service line, and you are able to advance to the net more quickly.

However, the jab has some disadvantages. It can be inconsistent on soft surfaces, it offers little possibility of variation, and it 1s difficult to master.

The chip shot, especially off the backhand, is one of the best methods of maintaining a consistent return. You achieve the chip by using an underspin followthrough.

With little backswing, take the ball on the rise. Instead of

holding your racquet perpendicular to the ground and contacting the ball at a 90-degree angle to produce a full rebound effect, lay back your racquet face to reduce the degree of rebound of the ball and increase the length of time you can keep the ball on your racquet. Remember—the longer you keep the ball on your racquet, the more control you will have.

Hit the high balls downward, because underspinning requires starting your racquet above the ball. Slide the low balls above the net.

Comparison with the Jab Return

The chip return requires less precise timing than the jab return. In the jab return, both your body and your racquet are moving forward, and thus you have two considerations.

The chip return, however, requires more backswing, and thus your body moves forward after the stroke rather than during. You have only the forward movement of your racquet to consider before and during the stroke.

Both returns require your taking the ball on the rise and playing it downward to the feet of the incoming server.

As you move closer to the net, your strokes require more quickness and less power. Thus, at the baseline you use your hand, shoulders, and step to achieve sufficient power and control for your groundstrokes. When you return or make an approach shot, only your hands and shoulders are necessary for power and control. And when you are close enough to the net to volley, only your hands are the essential factor.

In the volley your racquet itself rebounds the ball. Your hands, with only a short forward motion, control the shot. If you try to

create power with backswing and full followthrough, you will be wasting energy and time. A backswing requires extra time, the

extra time allows the ball to drop before you hit it, and a full

followthrough takes away from your time to prepare for the next volley. The best way to reinforce this action at the net is during practice to put a headband around your upper arms to force your elbows together so that you cannot swing but only punch the volley.

To examine the volley further, let us isolate the four positions in doubles—the server, the receiver, the server’s netman, and the receiver’s netman—and see what their roles are in relation to the net.

When you serve, you try to achieve two results: maximum flight time of your serve so that you can approach and get in tight at the net, and a limited return from the receiver that you or your netman can volley away easily at the net. When you approach, you try to volley the receiver’s return. The closer you are to the net and the more quickly you make this first volley, the less time the receiver has to recover and return the volley. If the receiver’s return is high, you can play your first volley through the opposing netman. If the return is such that you must make a low or deep first volley, play it crosscourt and then move forward to close the angle. If you or your partner do not win the point by the second volley, your serving advantage will be neutralized.

Sometimes your body speed as you approach the net acts as a hitting force, and you run through the shot. Or you build up so much speed that you do not have time to prepare your racquet or change direction. To temper such loss of control, keep in mind the following procedure for the first volley.

When you approach after your serve, run only as fast as maintaining your balance will allow. Shorten your stride to stutter

steps as the receiver hits the return. When you make your first volley, reach through the ball so that your stroke, not your body speed, controls the ball. Angle the face of your racquet for underspin and control. After hitting the first volley, keep moving forward for a better angle and a better chance to complete the

close of the net by the second volley. Shorten your steps to maintain balance, control body speed, and permit easier change of direction. Throughout the approach, keep your hands as far forward as possible so that you can reach for the ball without your body interfering. However, do not drop your racquet head below your hand, or your wrist will lose its firmness. Always try to get your hand below the ball by bending your knees and bringing your shoulders forward.

The second volley usually determines the point. Up to now your team has maintained its offense and thwarted the attempts of the receiving team to gain control. Now is the time to win the point.

However, do not put the second volley into a category separate from the flow of the preceding or following shots. In theory it may be a pivotal play, but in practice it is just another shot that you play to obtain the best results. Do not break up the rhythm of your play or impose another psychological pressure on yourself by considering the shot special. It is more important for you to play each shot as well as possible and in support of the next. Such consistency is an attack in itself, because the opposing team knows it cannot rely on your errors to get points, but rather must earn them.

As receiver you have two goals when you return: to limit the

time the server has to get to the net by cutting off the angle of the serve and taking the ball on the rise, and to make a low or difficult return with enough angle to increase the flight time of the ball and to pull the server away from the middle of the court.

Once you make your return, you try to thwart the advance of

the server by returning his or her first and second volleys and thereby obtaining a stand-off that eventually will neutralize the server’s initial offensive advantage. Perhaps you can create an opening for your own approach to the net by mixing speeds, lobbing, or using any other such means to frustrate the serving team. Unlike the server’s clear-cut tactic of serve-and-volley, your tactic as receiver is to constantly probe to find the best way to get to the net and disrupt the server’s offensive attack.

Even if you do not make the best possible return, your single most important task is to make a return. If you are forced to make an error, make it by hitting too deep rather than hitting too short and into the net. There is always the possibility of the other team’s playing it back.

As the server’s netman your role when your partner is approaching is to put pressure on the receiver by threatening to poach, poaching, or covering angles. Do not overplay so much that you constantly leave openings; the suggestion of movement is often more effective than the actual poach.

To restrict the receiver as much as possible, maintain the classic net position—halfway between the center service line and the doubles line, and one step and a racquet reach to the net. This

Your partner, the server, can help keep your position offensive by serving to the receiver’s weaknesses, but you must .be ready to cover all angles of service.

As the receiver’s netman you have two responsibilities before you settle in at the net: to cover the center in case the server’s netman poaches crosscourt, and to cover the alley in case the server makes his or her first volley down the line. Once the ball passes the server’s netman, you focus on the server and try to intimidate his or

However, if the receiver is returning weakly or the server is serving well or the receiver’s opportunity to come to the net is somehow

limited, then you start farther back in proportion to the weakness of the return. Whatever the reason, you must so position yourself that you and your partner can develop a united attack.

Sometimes on the second serve you can stand as close as possible

to the center service line to psychologically reduce the serving area. You can afford to overplay this way because the second serve usually brings a strong return that forces a weak volley from the server. However, if the receiver returns weakly, you may be caught ~ in an overcommitted position. Also, you must be careful not to be hit by the ball; if the ball touches you, the point goes to the server.

In fact, this is one reason you do not attempt this tactic on the first serve; the server could aim his or her first volley at you and win the point.

Once all four players are at the net, everyone is on equal ground. All the advantages and disadvantages of starting positions disap-

pear, the separate considerations change to team considerations, and the points will be lost more by errors than by planned strategy of attack. Balls will be played a bit too high or too wide, and the opposing team will try to capitalize on such advantages. The key at this point is to play advantages until an opening develops and then to play the opening. If the first opening does not work, go back to playing the percentages; with patience you will win the point.

Guidelines for Playing the Net

Although playing advantages is the general rule-of-thumb for playing the net, there are several guidelines for you to follow when you and your partner are at net.

* When you play the net, you are completely committed to an

offensive position. And such commitment requires your making anticipated, well-played volleys rather than guess-and-swing swats at the ball.

* The first person who can get to the ball takes the ball. You do not have time to wait for the ball or discuss the pros and cons of who should take the shot. Exceptions to this basic rule will stand out, and you can change play accordingly.

* Be aware of possible responses in order to develop anticipation. Whether your opponent plays the ball to you or your partner, stay ahead of the possibilities and maintain a constant awareness of the

ball. We have discussed some likely strategies, and you should count on just such shots from your opponents. Sometimes the unexpected happens, but the percentage of such shots nullifies their effect.

* Regardless of where your opponent plays the ball, reach out your hand and lean your body toward the ball. This reaction gets you in rhythm with the play, prepares you for any occurrence, and encourages continual flow to the ball rather than jerky stop-andstart responses. keeps the ball in front of you instead of to the side, because your body, or at least your shoulders, are turning when your hand reaches.

We have discussed the basic net shots—volley, overhead, and covering the overhead. Now let us touch upon some of the basic net tactics.

Poaching—crossing in front of your partner at the net and taking his shot—is perhaps the most dramatic net tactic. However, it often

creates more problems for your team than for your opponents, and some of the problems have nothing to do with the success or failure of the tactic itself.

There are definite times to poach, and both you and your partner must be ready to handle the response. You poach only if you can make a better shot than your partner to benefit the overall position of the team: to take advantage of a setup (when your

opponent has been forced to make a defensive, weak, or obvious shot); to reduce pressure on your partner; to cut off an angle; or to

limit the time for the other team to get in position for a setup. Once you poach, you must continue to cross, and your partner

must go behind you and cover the vacated area. You cannot stop and return to your original position. For more discussion of poaching see pages 100-103.

The lob volley also can be an effective tactic at the net, but it not only is precarious and difficult to execute, but also can turn you into a target. You must be careful how you hit it as well as when you play it.

Never play the lob volley from above the net; you will be sacrificing an offensive shot for a defensive one. Also, play it when there is no alternative. For example, if you are making a desperate effort to reach a low shot and your opponents have closed the net, your only hope is to go over them. Or if an angle pulls a netman wide and he or she returns low and races to close the net again, then a lob is perfect to take advantage of his or her overcommitted forward movement. A lob also can take advantage of wind, sun, lack of confidence, slow reaction, or a disheartened opponent.

One final word about the lob volley: always play it so that the

Opposition must move back, not stand still or move forward.

Hitting or Jamming an Opponent

Hitting or jamming an opponent when you are at net is an effective tactic. When the ball is below the net, angle the ball to an opening or to the body of an opposing netman, specifically to his or her forehand hip (a difficult shot to get back). You hit at your opponent not with the intention of hurting him or her but of

making that opponent step back and thereby creating an opening for a better angle. Or, inversely, you may jam an opponent after you have hit an effective angle that has put him or her off balance.

Whenever you are at the net and there is any question as to where you should play the ball, play it low to the middle. A shot down the middle always has plenty of court available to it, confuses the opposition as to who should take the shot, and limits the angles of return, thereby producing returns that are easy to cover.

Take the Shot as High as Possible

A basic rule-of-thumb for playing the net is to take the ball as high as possible. Sometimes your opponent will hit a drop shot— the ball drops over the net and stays below net level even after it bounces—and your response may be another drop shot. However, do not make the mistake of not taking the ball when it is above net

level. Similarly, if the ball has dropped below the net level, let it bounce and come back up after its bounce before you hit it so that you can make a driving shot rather than having to play an angle or defensive shot.

A commitment to offensive play is the key to playing the net. You might have to back away or play defensive shots when the opposition has a stronger offensive position, but at no time is your position defensive—it is always offensive.

Once you understand the physics of tennis and have mastered all the strokes, do not assume that practice and competition alone will bring success. What you have accomplished so far is merely a foundation; what you build from this foundation depends on your willingness to consider new building blocks of the game—percentage shots, anticipation, communication with your partner, and influence of score on determination of tactics, strategy, and stroke—

and to ask new questions about the game: What makes a team effective? What makes it ineffective? How does a team compensate for its weaknesses?

Let us examine the first building block mentioned above— percentage shots. Knowing why, when, and where to make a particular shot is as important as knowing how to make the shot. You determine the why, when, and where of a shot by its percentage of success. I have already discussed and evaluated

percentages of effectiveness of certain shots in previous chapters, but you must make your own analyses and thereby sharpen your awareness of various possibilities and situations.

To evaluate the percentage of success of a shot, consider three elements of equal value: the difficulty of the shot, the effect of the shot, and the possible response of the opponent. For example, a shot that is very easy to make might rate a 33 percent, but it may have only a medium effect of 15 percent. If it allows the best possible opportunity for the opponent, it merits 0 percent. Added together (33 + 15 + 0), the shot’s chance of success is 48 percent.

Also, as difficult as it may be, you must separate the effect of the shot from the response of the opponent to make an accurate evaluation of the shot. The effect of the shot does not necessarily control the response. For example, see Diagram 36, and assume you are the server’s netman. When analyzing the effect of a shot, do not forget the hidden psychological impact of certain shots. You can intimidate a team with power or make them feel foolish by running them all over the court or catching them offguard. Such psychological influences can be far more important than the physical influences.

Learning to play percentages requires developing an awareness and discipline that eventually becomes second nature. Although

this learning is not very exciting at first, once accomplished, it improves the quality of your play and the ease with which you handle tough situations and maintain a mental edge. Playing

percentages also simplifies play for you. If you cannot assess situations and possibilities, you can become so confused that your strokes are hesitant, you let opportunities go by, and you and your partner both converge on the ball and then let it pass.

Another building block of the game is anticipation. Anticipation

is too often considered a talent for guessing correctly. A better definition is awareness of ball-controlling factors. Developing such awareness is like working on any other part of your game. You must learn to break down and then reassemble actions and results so that you can understand individual influences as well as their role in the overall picture.

For the return of serve, you prepare prior to and during the toss, and you anticipate from such indicators as score, glance of the server before his or her toss, the position of the server on the baseline, whether the server is right-handed or left-handed, the direction of the wind, the success of previous serves, the server’s

grip, the condition of the balls, and the past history of the server’s overall play, especially his or her first volley. After impact, the speed, angle, spin, arc of flight, and target of the ball all give you information for the response.

For all other strokes, your anticipation has two parts: you prepare during the flight of the ball to your opponent, and you respond to the return of the ball by your opponent. During the first e2 eee

part you must consider the distance your opponent must move to get to the ball and then get back in position, the amount of backswing he or she uses for the stroke, his or her balance during the shot, the angle of the racquet head as he or she swings through the ball, the position of the ball in relation to his or her body, the balance of the opposing team, the balance of your own team, and the best percentage response. Once your opponent hits the ball, your focus should be on the ball, not on your opponents. Evaluate your best position of interception—the point from which you will play back the ball. If your opponent has made a good shot, your only possible response may be defensive. However, never accept your position of interception—choose it. Always try to take the ball at your best advantage, not your opponent’s.

After you have watched the ball and analyzed the possibilities and have selected your point of interception, what’s missing? The stroke, and often players do just that—miss the stroke. You must ready your racquet when the ball is leaving your opponent’s

racquet. It must be in position to hit as the ball crosses the net, whether or not the rest of your body is ready. If there is not enough time to get your body ready as well, at least you can bring the racquet forward on the chance of getting back the ball. Perhaps the best definition of anticipation is having your racquet ready all the time.

Communicating With Your Partner

Being ready all the time also means communicating with your

partner—a very important building block of doubles. The means of communication depends on the players. Some partners pass

information with the same elaborateness and dramatics as a baseball coach; some partners use an on-court huddle to discuss strategy in as technical a fashion as a professional football play

book; some use a wink or a look or a behind-the-back gesture. Betty Store and I played together for years and could make moves automatically without any signaling and could compensate for each other’s situations.

If anticipation is not possible or you have not yet developed the necessary awareness, rely upon the following generally accepted practices: ee)

1. The left-court player covers the center court. 2. Once the netman starts to cross, he must continue; his partner in the back must move to fill the opening.

3. If there is a one-up-one-back situation, the back man has an overall view and captains the situation.

4. Try to cover your own overhead. If you cannot, cross and wait for your partner, who must run the ball down, to give instructions to stay up or come back.

5. In any situation of possible confusion, talk.

6. Use the score to help decide tactics.

This last practice is often overlooked, but consideration of score is another very important building block. The score not only indicates the count, but also your focus—whether you should probe for information, maintain a certain strategy, or bring in different tactics.

All points are important. In the early part of the match you may use certain shots to test both your team’s and your opponents’ weaknesses and strengths. These probes, along with the basic game plan, are the foundation of the momentum of your attack. You maintain that momentum by recognizing the pivotal importance of score.

Let us isolate each point and look at the different considerations for each. Remember that we will be establishing guidelines that you should monitor with common sense and flexibility. If modification or even complete reversal of a guideline is successful, then by all means make the change.

In the following breakdown of the scores of a game and the evaluation of each team’s strategy at certain scores, consider yourself the server for each score.

At the score 0-0 your team, because of its serving advantage, can overplay to increase your intimidation. The receiver wants to get the ball in play to give his or her netman a chance to poach your first volley. Even if the receiving team is not successful, they have put pressure on your team to win the point. The opening point can set the tone for the whole game.

At the score 15-0 your team has a chance for a two-point lead of 30-0. However, your team’s court positions are not so strong when

the left-court receiver is returning, because he or she has the opportunity for the natural crosscourt backhand return to your backhand. You therefore hope to have your serve dominate the point rather than chance a strong return. The left-court receiver

feels the pressure to win the point just to stay even. He or she must play the percentage for a solid crosscourt return that will allow him or her to close the net. The receiver’s netman must stay tight to the net to pick off any error, but should not try any spectacular or risky plays.

At the score 0-15 you feel the pressure to win the point so that your team will not be down 0-30. Again, rely on your serving dominance and basic solid play. However, the left-court receiver has a chance to offset your serving advantage. If he or she can win the point for a 0-30 lead, you will need four points to win or three to go to deuce; the receiving team will need only two points to win or one to go to deuce. The left-court receiver therefore might try

for a bigger shot, because the lower percentage of the shot is offset by the advantages of success. If the shot is not successful, the score is tied, and no ground is lost.

At the score 15-15 both teams are facing a critical point; the result could be 15-30 or 30-15—the most pivotal score of the game. Again, you and the receiver should play basic tennis.

At the score 0-30 you are in trouble and must become more aggressive to force the receiver to make an error. Becoming more

aggressive does not mean making power shots or otherwise taking chances; it involves concentration and hustle. The right-court receiver can now be a little more offensive.

At the score 15-30 rely on basics. If you lose the point at this score, you will need a minimum of four points to win, and the receiving team will need only one. The receiver should go after this point and force you, not just feed into your serving dominance by making a weak return. The lower-percentage big shot balances the chance of success. If he or she misses, no ground is lost.

At the score 0-40 try something to break the momentum of the receiver—a big serve, the I-formation (discussed later), or some other tactic. Although the receiver has many chances for the one point he needs to win, he or she must’ not let up and assume

SUCCESS.

At the score 40-0 the receiver has nothing to lose. He or she must try something to get back into the game. You must maintain your attack.

The score 30-30 and 40-40 (deuce) present the same situation.

Your team should rely on serving dominance and basic strategy. The right-court receiver wants to give his or her partner a game

point. He or she must get the ball in play, but then close quickly and go for the point. If the right-court receiver does not get the point, perhaps the left-court receiver has a chance of returning the

score to deuce. If he or she does get the point, then perhaps the leftcourt receiver can make the final point to win the game.

At the score 40-15 play solid, basic tennis, but do not play hesitantly. The right-court receiver must look for any opportunity to go for a strong return. If he or she wins the point, you are reminded that your opponents still are playing aggressive tennis. A strong winning return may even undermine your confidence and lead the way to a deuce score.

At the score 15-40 rely on your serving dominance and solid basics to win the point. The right-court receiver should pressure you to play well and not allow you to rely on your serve to win the point. He or she should not necessarily go for an all-out winning return; a solid return and offensive posture are more effective.

At the score 40-30 you again should rely on serving dominance and solid basics. The left-court receiver should concentrate on making a good return that will pressure you to win the point.

At the score 30-40 again use standard play. The left-court receiver should go for any opening and attack.

Besides these guidelines for play at certain points of the game, there are some more general considerations:

* When ahead, keep up the attack. Never let your momentum drop and never let someone back into a match by assuming victory.

* Change a losing game one step at a time. If you do not, you will lose the good tactics, and the transition will be too radical for you to carry out.

* Note that the left-court receiver has all the pivotal and most of the game-winning points. The right-court receiver can only win or

lose at 40-15 or 15-40. However, without the support of the rightcourt receiver, the left-court receiver never has the opportunity to win the game, only the opportunity to tie.

* You cannot afford to lose your service game. A service break generally costs the set, because it is especially difficult to break back in doubles.

- The receiving team expects to lose most games, not by giving up but by the design of the game itself. However, they must put pressure on the serving team to win the game as opposed to their simply losing.

* The best chance to break is early in the set—before the serving team can warm up. Also, taking chances is least costly at this stage of the match.

* The most critical game for the team that started serving is at 4-4. If the receiving team breaks, they can serve out the set. The most critical game for the team that received first is 3-4.

* Maintain flexibility. Change the standard plays and introduce exotic ones when there is a need. Be willing to constantly revise for changing conditions.

Now that we have discussed some of the building blocks of doubles, let us look at some basic questions about the makeup of your team, its strengths and weaknesses, and how to make it more effective.

Understand first that the balance of power and range within each team is not 50-50. If you play the right court, you have the tougher side to win or control points because your backhand covers the middle. You must turn your body, or at least your shoulders,

135 degrees on some shots to return crosscourt backhands. In contrast, if you play the left court, your forehand covers the

middle. In fact, three quarters of the court is to your forehand side. Also, your crosscourt backhand is along a more natural angle. You therefore have the better power range for all your strokes.

As the left-court player, you also have the better chance to win the point from return of serve. The serve to the right court is stronger and more consistent because the natural curve of the serve enables the ball to pass over the lower part of the net and still hit any target. The serve to the left court is more of a problem for the server, because the curve of the serve to the backhand takes the ball over a higher part of the net. As the receiver in the left court, G7

If Both Partners Are Right-Handed

It should be obvious from the above that if you and your partner are right-handed, the stronger player should play the left court.

His or her forehand will be to the middle and thus can cover more than one half of the width of the court. He or she thus can back up or run down almost any shot, make overheads, and poach—all with the forehand. The right-court player has the job of keeping the ball in play long enough for the left-court player to take advantage of a setup or a weakness and make the winner.

The more effective server should serve the most times, and therefore should serve first. However, consider also the influence of the sun and the possibility that a sideline fence too close to the court may be used to the advantage of the weaker server.

But what if one member of your team is left-handed and the other right-handed? Although the left-hander often plays the left court and the right-hander the right court to enable strong forehand crosscourt shots and extra range of forehand coverage of wide angles, there are some arguments against this accepted procedure.

Remember that the most important key to breaking service is the return of serve and the first volley. By placing the backhands of

your team to the middle (right-hander in the right court; lefthander in the left court), both of you must deal with the two most difficult shots—the backhand return and the backhand volley— from the middle. Also, as netmen, both of you must poach with your backhand.

Consider now the arguments for the opposite arrangement—the left-hander of your team in the right court and the right-hander in

the left court. Both your returns will be more effective because both of your backhands will have the natural crosscourt stroke. Also because both forehands will be to the middle, you will have a

better chance to handle power plays through the middle. And your backhand volleys will be more effective in cutting off and returning crosscourt angles than in guarding the middle. As netmen, you can both poach with your forehand. It is true that you will sacrifice some range in covering wide angles, but compared to the advantage of your team’s inner court, this sacrifice is minimal.

Remember also that the right-handed player in the left court offers the strength of his or her being able to back up or run down almost any shot or make an overhead—all with the forehand. As for the serving advantage, both of you can spin the ball respectively in either sideways direction. The right-handed player can spin his or her serve to the right; the left-handed player can spin his or her serve to the left.

One of the best ways to increase the effectiveness of your team is by poaching or offensive crossing. You need not be the netman on the serving team to pgach. As the receiver’s netman, you often can take advantage of the weak position of the server when he or she makes his or her first volley. See Diagram 38 for an example of the effectiveness of the poach of the receiver’s netman in the left court.

Poaching on the first volley as receiver’s netman in the right court, however, is a much weaker tactic. When you cross from the right court, you must poach with your backhand, and your partner, who most often will have returned from the wide position, will have a great deal of court to cover.

Wins the Point by Poaching in the Left Court

Sometimes the server’s first volley is so weak or defensive that

you feel confident about crossing and winning the point outright without even forewarning your partner. However, this is the exception to the general rule of somehow signaling and commu-

nicating to your partner that you intend to poach so that he or she can fill the gap you leave. Often you use the tactic of poaching to simply break up the rhythm of the serving team, regardless of the conditions; you may win the point by poaching, or your opponents may return your shot. In either case, your partner must be ready to cover for you.

The serving team most often will not be able to prevent your winning poach, but they should try to force you to play the ball between them and through two racquets rather than to the outside where only one of them has a chance to get back your shot.

Sometimes the serving team sees that its advantageous position is threatened by exceptionally good crosscourt returns that do not allow the server to approach and volley successfully.

An effective strategy of the serving team to undermine such strong crosscourt returns, especially by the left-court receiver, is the

I-formation. See Diagram 40. As the server’s netman you stand in the middle of the court (to form a straight line or J with the server)

to cut off the crosscourt angles of the returns. Your partner, the server, serves wide to gain flight time and limit the receiver to only one angle of return. However, he or she must be prepared to cover

for you if you decide to move. You start a step or two back to be ready for a lob, but still maintain the advantage to step forward to poach with your forehand. When the receiver’s netman sees your team’s I-formation, he should start play a step or two wider to force you to play the middle and a step or two in to take advantage of any momentary off-balance of your team. The receiver should return down the line and close quickly, knowing that his netman will cover the possibility of a crosscourt shot by the server. Once

the ball is in play, the server moves to cover your normal position, or you can move back to your normal position while the server makes his or her usual approach.

Note that this tactic of the I-formation is weak against a rightcourt return. Down-the-line forehand returns from the right court usually are much stronger than from the left court, and backhand returns of such an angled serve are likely to be beyond the server’s attempt to cover your (server’s netman’s) normal position. Also,

your partner, the server, will have to cover a down-the-line return with his or her backhand, and if you poach, you also will have to use your backhand.

Do not use the I-formation in either court unless regular positioning is not successful, and even then, make sure that your partner’s serve is effective enough for the modified play. Even with a left-court return, if his or her serve is to the forehand, the receiver can angle it away from both of you. On the other hand, a serve to the backhand in the left court is very effective. The receiver cannot use the natural crosscourt backhand because you, as netman in the

I-formation, are covering just such a return. He or she must return late down the line, and the advancing server has sufficient time to cover the return with his or her forehand.

Mutual Respect and Understanding in a Team

Perhaps the single most unifying element in a team is mutual respect and understanding. Whether your partner is weaker than or equal to you in skills, experience, and knowledge of the game, you must be supportive of each other.

If you are the stronger player, you must encourage your partner to contribute whatever strengths he or she does have to the team. If you are the weaker player, you must understand and value your partner’s and your own roles and responsibilities.

If both of you are equal, then the team should follow the more classic approach of one partner setting up and the other putting away. You must evaluate your own particular strengths and

weaknesses and agree on which of you should play the right or left court. Sometimes such a decision is not that simple or clear-cut, in which case one or both of you must concentrate on making a strong physical and mental adjustment and developing compensatory tactics.

Off the court you and your partner should communicate to each other your ideas, feelings, and evaluations of each other’s and your opponents’ strengths and weaknesses, tactics, and strategy. Most

important, from this give-and-take dialogue you will develop a bond of empathy and support and an understanding of each other’s expectations. These expectations must be balanced. Both of you should contribute fully. If you do not achieve a measure of

harmony, effectiveness, and success satisfactory to both of you, then together you must review the situation and consider the

changes necessary to establish a better team. Sometimes the result may be a simple agreement to try for every shot, run down every ball, and never give up.

These suggestions may seem pedantic, but perhaps they can save a friendship for you, make your chance for exercise worthwhile, or ‘ win a world pro title for you.

Remember—you should enjoy doubles, not have to endure it.
