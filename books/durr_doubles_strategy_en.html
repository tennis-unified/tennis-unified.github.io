A third reason why doubles lagged behind is that, with the ex-

ception of a very few players like Billie Jean King, the superstars of

singles are not superstars of doubles. Singles and doubles are two

doubles player. On the other hand, a less well-known champion of

The influx of big money into professional tennis and the

tremendous surge in the popularity of tennis created the conditions

necessary to boost doubles to a better balance with singles.

Consider the days of amateur tennis of the past. Doubles was

considered the domain of weekend players—it was not real tennis.

In tournaments it was relegated to the back courts, and singles

scheduling took priority. Perhaps a few spectators would stop by

for some light entertainment.

Compare this with the situation today. Ever-increasing demand

for spectator tennis and ever-increasing available capital have

encouraged extending the tournament circuits into the bad

weather months. Enclosed arenas have been built—arenas with one

court. By necessity all play is set on the same stage. Voila! Doubles

has its audience; it is no longer set out of sight. You can witness the

drama of gut-busting, arm-stretching doubles—serves that bob like

elusive apples, shotgun volleys, feather-duster touch shots, angles

that stretch players parallel to the ground, lobs to the wall

returned with contorted backfiring, drop shots that tease an

opponent into the desperate effort to make the return before the

ball dies at his or her feet. And you love it!

Doubles players now have not only an enthusiastic audience and

more recognition than they had before, but also more financial re-

ward. Better prize money is being awarded. Grand Prix points for

the men and International series points for the women designate

top teams for extra compensation, and the Mixed Doubles Cham-

pionship on television in the spring has become a major winter

event.

In nonprofessional tennis doubles has become the answer to the

staggering demand for courts, a demand brought on by the tennis

boom. Many facilities—public and private—have eased court availability during the warm weather months by requiring doubles during prime time—a very palatable solution for tennis enthusiasts. If you live in an area where bad weather months prohibit playing

outdoors, doubles provides the answer for the otherwise prohibitive

cost of using indoor courts.

Just as more and more spectators enjoy watching doubles, so do

more and more players enjoy playing doubles. If you are a beginner, novice doubles allows you to concentrate on one discipline at a time—service, net, and so on. If you are an advanced player, you may still prefer one-on-one confrontation, but hopefully you are learning to appreciate the challenge of playing in a team situation and working for the power, placement, and movement that blend to make a doubles team.

Now that you see how the alleys of the doubles court increase the

possibilities of angles, surrounding playing area, crosscourt shots,

and flight time, you should become more aware of the role these

factors play in the tactics and strategy of doubles.

As I mentioned before, singles and doubles are two very different

games. Let us define their differences in terms of the strokes that

are the tools of both games. By understanding the possibilities and

limitations of each stroke, you will be better able to understand

how and why the tactics and strategy of using these strokes

separate one game from another.

Tennis popularity started to grow rapidly during the era of the serve-and-volley game. We have all been impressed with the

rocket-like serves of pros like Stan Smith, Arthur Ashe, and Roscoe Tanner. It is therefore understandable that many amateur players

consider power the key to a successful serve.

However, as I’ll now show, in neither singles nor doubles does the serve have to be powerful to be effective. Nor should the serve be the sole offensive weapon in either game. I have managed to

stay in the top ten among women players in the world for more

than fifteen years with a serve that Bud Collins has dubbed “pittypat.” It is so slow that in a fast-serve contest in Washington, D. C., my serve never moved the needle on the machine used to judge the

contest.

Consider first the serve in singles. There are three basic strategies in singles: serve-and-volley, backcourt, or an all-court mixture of

the two. Each strategy has a different serving requirement. The

following guidelines might help you to determine your strategy

and your service tactics according to the weaknesses and strengths

- If you see that your opponent needs a net target for a

successful return or can only reflex a ball back, do not play serve-

and-volley. This strategy will only make up for his or her

limitations rather than take advantage of them. A steady back-

court game and slow, twisting serves will frustrate him or her out of

the match.

* You may find that a hard, wide serve is an asset for a weaker,

less experienced opponent who desperately swings late and thereby

unintentionally passes you down the line. If you then change your

tactic to a one-half-speed serve, he or she will probably hit

everything out.

* If your opponent is weak-armed and only blocks balls back,

serve wide to either side. He or she will be stretched far and will

not have the strength for a successful return.

* If his or her footwork is poor and preparation slow, serve three-

quarter speed into the body.

* No matter what the quality of your opponent, keep him or her

off balance by varying the speed, twist, and spin of your serve.

* If you are losing, try deep, soft first serves to the backhand.

Unless your opponent is having even more success, keep up the

tactic. Over a period of time the pressure of your reduced errors

and the constant attack on his or her backhand will take its toll. Your opponent will have to work for each point and will lose

momentum. You will be forcing your opponent to win each point

instead of losing it yourself.

In doubles there is only one basic strategy: Get your first serve in and go to the net as fast as you can. You cannot afford to stay on

the baseline while your partner is at net. Such team-splitting gives your opponents the opportunity to close the net and gain the offen-

sive. See Diagram 6. Suppose you serve and do not follow your serve to the net. The receiver feels no pressure when he returns and approaches the net (Ri > Re). The receiving team now has a

united offensive position at the net. Each of them has two different easy-volley areas—a total of four easy-volley areas—open because

your team has neither a united offensive position nor a united de-

Although you use the big serve to get to the net in singles, you do

not use it to get to the net in doubles. Why not?

The Hard First Serve in Singles

In singles a fast, wide, or jamming first serve weakens your

opponent’s return and thereby allows you to move up and

maintain the offensive. You can take the first volley as far back as

between the baseline and the service line and still play the ball

safely to an opening in the court far enough away from the receiver

to allow you time to move even closer to the net.

More important, should your first serve be a fault, you need not

follow your second serve to the net. You have the option of waiting

for a strong return of your second serve at the baseline and lobbing

or passing if your opponent rushes to the net. You therefore do not

have the pressure of getting in your first serve and can afford to

strive for the big first serve.

The Hard First Serve in Doubles

In doubles, however, to gain the time to get into a strong

position at the net—an absolute must after your serve in doubles—

the same big serve must be at least 70 percent successful in

producing a weak return from the receiver.

Think what happens if both your serve and the receiver’s return

are hard and fast: Your netman’s time and ability to intimidate

the receiver with the threat of poaching is reduced. Your own time

to move to the net is reduced, and you must make your first

volley—probably a weak one—just inside the baseline. The re-

ceiver’s netman will probably intercept your weak volley, and your

netman will not have time to cover the interception. The receiver will close the net before you do, and your opponents will take

command of the net.

Also, in doubles your percentage of first serves in is critical. Unlike when serving in singles, you do not have the option of

staying back on the second serve. If you do stay back, you will face

the same set of circumstances I just described, and the receiver will

close the net before you do. In fact, with the second serve, the

receiver can take a bolder, more offensive stance. Knowing that

your second serve will not be a power serve, he or she will move a

step or two closer to the net. There he or she will be in a better

position to aim for wider angles and to perhaps test out your

netman. Again, the receiver has a better opportunity of closing the

net than you do.

The Most Effective Doubles Serve

If the hard, fast serve is not the most effective serve for doubles, what is? One approach to this question is to consider what returns

of serve are most effective in doubles and from there determine what serve will prevent or frustrate such returns.

In singles your opponent’s best return of your serve is one that is

deep and wide, thereby forcing you to move laterally and slowing your advance to the net. You therefore serve with all the slice, spin, and power you can muster in order to limit the receiver’s opportunity to pass you and make you move to the side rather

than up.

In doubles, however, your netman’s ability to cover half the court at the net reduces the possibility of effective lateral returns.

Your opponent’s best return is at your feet as you come in to the

net after serving—a very difficult shot to handle. To counteract the effectiveness of this kind of return, use a three-quarter-speed serve with some twist to allow yourself time to get in close enough for the net to actually protect your feet. The added twist on your serve may unbalance the receiver enough to weaken the return and thereby

give you more opportunity to make an effective volley. Billy Jean King says that my serve is so slow and well-placed that I’m stand-

ing at the net before she gets a chance to hit the return.

I discussed the return of serve briefly in the previous section, but let us try to define further the differences between the returns of serve in singles and doubles. Imagine now that you are the receiver and consider how to most effectively neutralize the advantage of

the server by your return.

Area of Return. As I pointed out earlier, the area of return in

singles is much less restricted than in doubles because you have only one opponent. You are able to return deep and wide and there-

by to pull the server to the side and impede his or her advance to

the net.

Stroke of Return. In singles you also have the time to execute a

normal groundstroke as a return. If your opponent comes to net after a

big serve, you have a good possibility of passing him or her with an

equally powerful groundstroke. If your opponent does not come to

the net (there is no pressure in singles to do so), then your strong

return at least will somewhat reduce his or her offensive potential.

In fact your return might feel so strong or well-placed that you can

follow your return to the net and thereby gain the offensive

position. Or you can stay at the baseline and use deep

groundstrokes to keep your opponent at the baseline until you feel

you have placed a ball so well that your opponent will not be able

to pass you if you come to the net. Once you come to the net,

however, you must try to win the point as soon as possible, because

it is difficult to cover the entire net yourself for more than one or two volleys.

In doubles as well as in singles, the server, because he starts play,

controls the offensive position. Your return should be an attempt

to neutralize that advantage. The key elements of an effective

return in doubles are placement and quickness.

Area of Return. Remember that your area of return is restricted

because the server’s netman can intercept returns on at least one half of the court. You must return, therefore, in the direction of the

incoming server. However, the return must be one that he or she

cannot easily volley away for a winner. A return at the server’s feet with a troublesome bounce or drop of the ball will frustrate and

weaken his or her ability to place a strong volley and thereby give

you a stand-off—another chance to play the ball and hopefully to

further reduce his or her initial advantage as server.

The most popular stroke to achieve this ideal return is the chip

shot. In this stroke you hit the ball softly and with underspin for

control. The ball drops quickly and dies on the bounce.

Stroke of Return. In singles you know that the server might move in

behind the serve and volley from approximately the service line. In

doubles you know that the server wz// move in and volley from well

within the service line. Also, in doubles you are more likely to move

to the net yourself after your return. You therefore cannot afford

the time, as in singles, to stand back and execute a powerful groundstroke with exaggerated backswing and followthrough as a return. Such exaggeration affords the server more time to move in

tighter to the net and gives you less time to follow your return to

the net. Instead, you must stand in as close as possible to receive and use a groundstroke return with abbreviated backswing and shortened, firm-wristed followthrough. The quickness of this kind of return

shortens the time the server has to get to the net and increases your

time to follow your return to the net.

Groundstrokes are basically for backcourt play, and are used in singles much more than in doubles. You keep your opponent back with deep corner-to-corner placements that barely clear the net.

In doubles you cannot afford the time to execute the full backswing and followthrough of baseline groundstrokes. Before you even recover from such a stroke, your opponents will be at the

net and will volley away your shot. To save time, you shorten your backswing and followthrough and take the ball above the net. To avoid or frustrate your opponent’s volley, you hit down at an

opponent’s feet or make a soft shot that dies just as it clears the net. You thereby force your opponent to hit up. Then you can make

the putaway shot down.

If, however, your team is forced to the baseline, you can use

groundstrokes as a defensive measure against your opponents at

the net. Hit these groundstrokes with extra topspin so that the ball

stays low, dips as it clears the net, and therefore is difficult for your

opponents to handle. To hit topspin, keep your racquet face

vertical and brush up—bottom to top—on the ball as it leaves your

racquet to give it spin in the direction it is traveling. The spin of

the ball makes the ball difficult to volley. And if it bounces, it

usually hops and gains pace.

Or, given the same situation of being at the baseline with your

opponents at net, you also can try a power play with

groundstrokes—that is, hitting two or three flat, hard balls to

intimidate your opponents into taking a step or two back from the

net, and then hitting a topspin shot that will drop at their feet.

The lob is primarily a defensive stroke in both singles and

doubles. By sending the ball high, you can gain time to recover

after being pulled out of position by a well-placed shot. Or you can

use the lob to open the net.

Consider the following possibilities of the lob in doubles:

* A good lob, by giving you the opportunity to move up as your

opponents retreat to retrieve, may reverse a situation where your

team is at the baseline and your opponents are at the net.

* A quick lob volley over your opponents’ heads may even catch 20

them flat-footed and turn out to be a winner for you. However, use

this tactic only if your opponents are so tight at the net that you

have relatively few options. When the ball is above the net, it is

always best to volley downward, but the lob volley is an occasional

answer for the ball below the net.

- A lob over the head of the server’s netman as a return of serve

can unbalance the net attack of the server and thereby give your

team the opportunity to reduce the server’s advantage.

In singles you must earn the net with placements that will not

allow your opponent to make passing shots. And once you are at

the net, you must win the point as soon as possible because it

becomes increasingly more difficult for you to cover the entire net

yourself once you have made your first or second volley.

In doubles you control the net as server by virtue of the starting

position of your netman. He is able to cover 50 percent of the net

before you even serve the ball, and if your serve is good, you can

close the other 50 percent almost before the ball reaches the

receiver. As in singles, once you have control of the net, you should win the point as soon as possible to prevent your opponents from

gaining a stand-off position that could possibly weaken your

advantage.

Dominating the net does not mean that you should limit your offense to power volleys. Like big serves, big net shots poorly

played often beget even faster returns that do not give you enough time to recover and prepare. Early interception and solid placement, not just power, are the keys to good net play. However, do not hesitate to hit out when you know you have a good setup. Even

so, always be prepared for a return.

The antithesis of the lob, and of comparable value, is the overhead. The overhead, or smash, requires a blend of power and placement. Like the serve, it is an offensive shot that, if played incorrectly, can produce a backlash rather than a winner.

In singles, placement, and not power, of the overhead is of primary importance. Because your opponent has his or her entire court to cover, you usually can win your point by simply hitting

the ball where he or she is not.

In doubles, because you must hit through two opponents, you

have less chance of placing the ball where someone is not. Power is

more important than placement. Also, because your opponents will

be fairly close to the net, a hard smash will reduce their reflex time.

The half-volley is a technique to handle a low ball that bounces

before a volley is possible, but not soon enough to allow a normal

groundstroke. The ball is taken at the bounce with no backswing,

but with a short, sweeping-up followthrough to direct and lift the ball.

In singles you half-volley down the line to stretch your opponent

wide if he or she is at the net and keep your opponent deep in the

corner if he or she is at the baseline.

In doubles you half-volley down the line, or if an opponent is

covering the line, you half-volley crosscourt to the feet of your other

opponent.

Remember that the half-volley is rarely a winner. It is a difficult

shot to execute, but if you are successful in simply playing it back,

you can hope to close in immediately and make the easier volley.

The approach shot is not a specific stroke. It is a groundstroke, a volley, or a half-volley taken inside the baseline and followed to the

net.

In singles, place your approach shot deep down the line. Because a down-the-line ball must have an appreciable angle of rise to clear

the net, a medium-pace ball will travel upward after clearing the

net. Your opponent not only will be stretched to reach the ball, but

also will have to withstand the power of a ball hit straight ahead. He or she will be forced into making a weak defensive shot or

attempting a low-percentage winner.

In doubles you should not go down the line on an approach shot. If you do, one of your opponents will close the line and volley away the rising ball. Instead, follow the general rule of a crosscourt approach shot fo the feet of your opponent. A medium-pace crosscourt shot usually has a gentle angle of rise so that the ball drops just as

it clears the net. Your opponent must hit up, and you have a good

chance of winning the point with a strong volley.

Occasionally the netman directly opposite you will anticipate

the crosscourt shot and will move from his or her position to intercept it. In this case a short, easy shot down the line may prove

quite effective.

The drop shot is effective in singles to make an outright winner,

to tire your opponent, to force a strong backcourt player forward to

play a weak net game, or simply to catch your opponent off

balance and set up an opening.

The drop shot in itself does not have the same effectiveness in

doubles that it does in singles. But if you modify it to a soft angle

shot, you increase its possibilities in doubles. Soft angle shots skim

the net and drop quickly so that your opponents have little

opportunity for making forceful plays. More important, an oppo-

nent is forced to hit up. If he tries to hit with power, the ball will

carry high enough above the net for you or your partner to volley it

away for a winner. Your opponent would be wise to answer your

soft, short angle with another soft, short angle.

My hope is that this review of strokes and of the differences in

tactics and strategy of using them in singles and doubles has made

it evident that strokes are your tools for building a good game. You

must learn to execute each stroke and shot separately before you

can attempt to progress to an integrated all-court game of either

singles or doubles.

In the following chapters I shall discuss the three main

components of the game of doubles—serve, return of serve, and

net—in much more detail and show how your mastery of the

strokes required for each component enables you to use the tactics

that will achieve a winning strategy.

Your service is most effective if you vary the speed and spin of

each serve and thereby keep the receiver off balance. To accom-

plish such differences in delivery, change your racquet grip and

your ball toss, not the speed of your stroke. Your racquet grip and ball toss determine the angle of your racquet face when it hits the ball, and it is the angle of your racquet face that determines how much of the power of your stroke will be distributed to velocity of

the ball and how much to spin of the ball.

There are three standard serves—flat, slice, and twist. A fourth

serve—the American twist—is a variation of the twist.

For the flat serve, use the forehand grip (usually the eastern or

shake-hands grip) and a forward toss.

The advantage of the serve is its speed, its potential for

overpowering the receiver.

For the slice serve, use the backhand grip and a side toss. The

ball curves sharply from right to left (right-hand service), and

continues to curve after it bounces, skidding low. See Diagram 9 on

The advantage of the slice serve is that the curve of the ball can

pull the receiver wide or can jam him or her with a difficult angle

to the body.

The slice serve has three disadvantages: (1) reaching laterally for

the side toss reduces your forward motion into the court, (2) net

clearance of the serve is limited, and (3) the receiver has a greater

angle of return if the serve pulls him wide but he still is able to

make the return.

The Serve and Control of the Net

Control of the net is the goal of both the serving and receiving teams, and the serve is extremely important in determining which

team will be able to close the net first.

As I pointed out in foregoing sections, the speed, slice, spin, and twist of the ball are not the only factors contributing to the effectiveness of your serve. Your serving position, the angle of your

serve, and your serving target are instrumental in not only eliciting weak returns, but also stretching the receiver laterally or otherwise

impeding his or her forward approach after the return so that you

can close the net before your opponent does.

How the Server Moves to the Net

Your forward movement to the net begins with the fol-

lowthrough of the serve itself. The downward swing or tail end of

the followthrough brings your shoulders and hips forward, and

your back foot steps out to balance your body for a continuous,

even acceleration. An erratic toss, an unrhythmic motion, a stiff

wrist, or overhitting will hamper the followthrough and impede

your approach to the net.

To take advantage of the acceleration and balance generated by

a good followthrough, stay low for easy change of direction, but

not so low as to impede speed. Take full strides, keep your

shoulders down for balance, and extend your hands to enable you

to maneuver your racquet quickly and to hit the ball as early as

possible.

When the receiver’s racquet starts forward, shorten your steps to

stutter steps—short, quick, forward-moving steps—to allow you to

move laterally quickly if necessary but to still keep your forward

momentum for early interception of the ball.

Continue moving while volleying to enable you to keep longer

control of the ball on your racquet and to close the net more

tightly rather than stopping, volleying, and starting up again.

The Role of the Server’s Netman

The effectiveness of your netman is also dependent upon your

serve. A good serve allows him to overplay his area—move beyond

his position—and intimidate the receiver. A poor serve may make

him a target, thereby limiting his intimidation and leaving you

with large lateral areas and hard returns to handle yourself. Given

average serves, your netman should play conservatively and rely

upon the advantage of your starting position as server to win the

points.

The key to effective service is to get your first serve in with a variety of speed, spin, twist, angle, and target that will probe and capitalize on the receiver’s weaknesses in movement, racquet preparation, footwork, and forehand and backhand returns.

Like the serve, the return of serve is a crucial segment of each point. The serving team is now vulnerable because the server has not yet closed the net, and the receiving team must get the ball

back in order to even play.

There seem to be two possible ways to play the return. One is to attack the vulnerability of the serving team; the other is to just get

the ball back. Actually, both of these are one and the same. Getting the ball back in itself attacks the serving team’s vulnerability, and getting it back consistently undermines the server’s

confidence.

However, do not confuse getting the ball back with playing it

safe. Playing it safe is a negative approach. If you are too

concerned with control, your stroke may become stiff; if you are

too intent on relaxing, your hand or wrist may become too limp; and if you are afraid of overhitting, you may tend to hold back on a full followthrough. Think more in terms of a positive approach—

of hitting out. You may make some errors, but you will be using a

natural stroke that eventually will work for you.

Taking the Ball in Front of Your Body

When you take the ball in front of your body, you gain three distinct advantages in return of serve: you can maintain your

balance, you can utilize your peripheral vision, and you can take

one or two steps closer to the net.

Only your stroke should influence the ball. A shift of weight or

incorrect muscle sequence at the moment of impact can upset the

return.

If such improper body use is a matter of habit, then your return

will be consistently erratic. Should this be the case, work with an

instructor or coach to break the habit and rebuild a basic

technique. Do not be afraid of change, of relearning in order to

improve. To make a correction, slow down the stroke and

intellectualize the muscle memory—that is, keep track of the entire

stroke until the new correction has been incorporated. If your

racquet moves more quickly than your mind, then the old muscle

memory re-introduces itself and the old error reappears.

Building up mental awareness takes as much practice as

developing physical abilities. In fact, mental development must be

in proportion to physical development. Both the amateur social

player and the professional player must maintain a certain level of

mental discipline in order to realize the complete physical dimen-

sion of their game. See Diagram 19.

The professional player’s level of mental involvement, of course,

is very high. On return of serve he should be able to concentrate on

such physical considerations as the execution of his return stroke,

where and how to place the return, and when and how to

approach the net. The beginner, on the other hand, is able to

concentrate only on getting the ball back by keeping it in front of

the body and using the simplest stroke possible.

If you are an intermediate or advanced player, you still must

concentrate primarily on the ball, but you probably have de-

veloped by now an automatic mental checklist to keep your return

stroke simple and effective. If you try to deliberately think out the

checklist, you probably will disrupt the natural flow of the muscle

memory that has been developed. If you wish to use such effects as

spin and speed, study them separately and use them only as

options, not as standard high-percentage returns.

Whatever you do, do not expend so much mental energy on the

idea of winning that you fail to concentrate on the moment-to-

moment play of the game.

Make Observations During the Warmup

You can prepare yourself mentally and physically to receive before the server even comes to the line by making the following observations during the warmup:

1. Are the balls new or old? Light or heavy?

2. Is it the first point of the third set? 3. Is the server left-handed or right-handed? 4. Does the server use a lot of spin, or are his or her serves flat?

5. Does the server have an easy motion? 6. Does the server have a backcourt stance or serve-and-volley

stance?

7. Where does the server toss—front, side, or back—to achieve

different serves?

8. Where does the server look when he or she is beginning to

serve? Before he or she serves?

9. What type of stance does the server have? Could he or she hit

a wide twist to the forehand, or does his or her body preclude that?

10. Does the server hold the racquet tight or loose? What type of

grip does the server use?

11. Is the server slow to warm up, or fast? Flashy or effective?

Relaxed or nervous?

It is definitely an advantage to be able to use the continental

grip for the return as well as the rest of the point. Because you can

use this grip for every stroke, you need not keep changing your

grip, and you can respond more quickly to the rapid exchanges in

doubles. One potential problem of the continental grip is overusing

your wrist. Keep your racquet head up, and if you use your wrist at all, incorporate it into the total stroke and do not create a separate

movement for it.

Your hands and shoulders are important in body position of

returning serve. Bring your shoulders forward so your hands have

room in front of your body to move without having to fight to get

around your body. Let your hands hang at mid-thigh.

Keep your legs comfortably apart—the width of a step—and turn

out your toes about 45 degrees. This stance allows you to step in

either direction without pivoting. Bend your knees and put your

weight on the balls of your feet for balance.

Preparation Between the Serve and the Return

Keep your eyes on the ball the entire time—while it is in the

server’s hand before the toss, while it is in the air during the toss,

while it comes down into your court and bounces up after it lands.

Sometimes the net tape catches your eye and distracts you from following the ball to its bounce. The ball seems to disappear, and

you blame such variables as the speed or spin of the ball or the

lighting of the court, but in fact you simply have not followed the ball. A good way to learn to see the seam of the ball when it’s in

flight.

Use an Abbreviated Groundstroke

Just before the wrist snap of the server, make a slight hop to

bring your weight to the balls of your feet. When you determine

the direction of the ball, keep your hands low, pivot your

shoulders, and make a short backswing. You will develop power by

meeting the ball with a solid forward swing and utilizing the power of the serve itself. During your forward swing, keep the ball on the

strings of your racquet long enough to impart direction, speed, and

spin. What you are doing, basically, is abbreviating (or condens-

ing) a groundstroke.

Do not worry about getting the lower half of your body—your hips and feet—around; you do not have enough time. Preparing your upper body—turning your shoulders, arms, and racquet as one unit—is sufficient to hit solidly through the ball. Your hips will turn slightly with your shoulders anyway, and your left foot eventually will step naturally with the followthrough. If your shoulders, hips, and feet cannot support the followthrough, then sheer arm muscle and mind will take over.

In fact, the most important element in preparation is your racquet. You hit the ball with your racquet, not your body. If your

racquet is not in hitting position, preparation of the rest of your

body means nothing. But if your racquet is in hitting position, you

can always hit the ball and muster as much body support as time

will allow. I have become famous for my unorthodox strokes, but most people don’t recognize that my racquet is always in the

proper hitting position no matter how awkward my grip or body

position might be.

Seventy-five percent of serving placements will be within one

step, especially if you intercept at a 45-degree angle. This angle of

interception not only limits the angle of the serve, but also keeps

your body weight into the ball. See Diagram 20.

Within a step and a half, you can reach 85 percent of serves, and within two steps, you can return 95 percent.

If you miss a return, it is probably because of not watching the

ball, overreacting, not completing your followthrough, or taking

too much backswing, rather than because of the speed or angle of

the serve.

Many good players return an opponent’s serve well with no

more than a jab. The jab offers a definite advantage to the

receiver—it eliminates the need for a backswing. You can take the ball on the rise and very soon after the bounce, sometimes almost like a half-volley. The racquet acts as a backboard, and you lend just enough forward swing for control. The result is that you use

the server’s own power against him or her by merely deflecting the

ball. The quick return so reduces the server’s approach time that

he or she must make the first volley from behind the service line,

and you are able to advance to the net more quickly.

However, the jab has some disadvantages. It can be inconsistent

on soft surfaces, it offers little possibility of variation, and it 1s difficult to master.

The chip shot, especially off the backhand, is one of the best methods of maintaining a consistent return. You achieve the chip by using an underspin followthrough.

With little backswing, take the ball on the rise. Instead of

holding your racquet perpendicular to the ground and contacting the ball at a 90-degree angle to produce a full rebound effect, lay back your racquet face to reduce the degree of rebound of the ball and increase the length of time you can keep the ball on your

racquet. Remember—the longer you keep the ball on your racquet,

the more control you will have.

Hit the high balls downward, because underspinning requires starting your racquet above the ball. Slide the low balls above the

net.

Comparison with the Jab Return

The chip return requires less precise timing than the jab return. In the jab return, both your body and your racquet are moving

forward, and thus you have two considerations.

The chip return, however, requires more backswing, and thus

your body moves forward after the stroke rather than during. You

have only the forward movement of your racquet to consider

before and during the stroke.

Both returns require your taking the ball on the rise and playing

it downward to the feet of the incoming server.

return increases the ball’s flight time and thereby allows you more

time to approach the net. If you can get to the net before or at the

same time as the server, the server’s initial advantage will be

neutralized.

move to the net: lobs, groundstrokes, soft angles, or, better yet, a

mixture of these.

The best defense is to mix shots constantly and thereby prevent

the net team from getting set and developing a unified attack.

Also, cut off the ball early whenever possible to force them to

recover quickly, thereby increasing your chances to play the ball

away from them without having to hit big shots. Trying to hit

passing shots or winners from the. baseline will defeat your

objective of trying to unbalance or force back the net team and

thereby gain access to the net.

When lobbing, always try to force your opponent to move back

to take the lob. As you can see in Diagram 28, the farther from the

net your opponent is, the fewer angles he or she can make (and

angles are the key to beating a baseline defense) and the less power

he or she can put into an overhead. If a momentary opening

develops, play a groundstroke with a soft angle that possibly will

catch your opponent away from the net before he or she can

recover from the overhead.

Play groundstrokes up the middle or at players. Alternating

power and extra topspin (to make the ball drop quickly) keeps

your opponents at the net guessing, limits the angles they can

make, and may even intimidate them into taking a step or two backward, especially if you put up a couple of lobs. Once the net

team retreats, you can exploit their new vulnerability and take the offensive.

Every team has weaknesses and strengths. The most important

time to exploit the weaknesses and neutralize the strengths is at the

return-of-serve segment of each point.

As you move closer to the net, your strokes require more

quickness and less power. Thus, at the baseline you use your hand,

shoulders, and step to achieve sufficient power and control for your

groundstrokes. When you return or make an approach shot, only

your hands and shoulders are necessary for power and control. And

when you are close enough to the net to volley, only your hands are

the essential factor.

In the volley your racquet itself rebounds the ball. Your hands, with only a short forward motion, control the shot. If you try to

create power with backswing and full followthrough, you will be wasting energy and time. A backswing requires extra time, the

extra time allows the ball to drop before you hit it, and a full

followthrough takes away from your time to prepare for the next volley. The best way to reinforce this action at the net is during practice to put a headband around your upper arms to force your elbows together so that you cannot swing but only punch the

volley.

To examine the volley further, let us isolate the four positions in doubles—the server, the receiver, the server’s netman, and the

receiver’s netman—and see what their roles are in relation to the

net.

When you serve, you try to achieve two results: maximum flight time of your serve so that you can approach and get in tight at the

net, and a limited return from the receiver that you or your

netman can volley away easily at the net. When you approach, you

try to volley the receiver’s return. The closer you are to the net and

the more quickly you make this first volley, the less time the

receiver has to recover and return the volley. If the receiver’s return

is high, you can play your first volley through the opposing

netman. If the return is such that you must make a low or deep

first volley, play it crosscourt and then move forward to close the

angle. If you or your partner do not win the point by the second

volley, your serving advantage will be neutralized.

Sometimes your body speed as you approach the net acts as a hitting force, and you run through the shot. Or you build up so

much speed that you do not have time to prepare your racquet or

change direction. To temper such loss of control, keep in mind the following procedure for the first volley.

When you approach after your serve, run only as fast as maintaining your balance will allow. Shorten your stride to stutter

steps as the receiver hits the return. When you make your first volley, reach through the ball so that your stroke, not your body

speed, controls the ball. Angle the face of your racquet for

underspin and control. After hitting the first volley, keep moving

forward for a better angle and a better chance to complete the

close of the net by the second volley. Shorten your steps to

maintain balance, control body speed, and permit easier change of

direction. Throughout the approach, keep your hands as far

forward as possible so that you can reach for the ball without your

body interfering. However, do not drop your racquet head below

your hand, or your wrist will lose its firmness. Always try to get

your hand below the ball by bending your knees and bringing your

shoulders forward.

The second volley usually determines the point. Up to now your

team has maintained its offense and thwarted the attempts of the

receiving team to gain control. Now is the time to win the point.

However, do not put the second volley into a category separate

from the flow of the preceding or following shots. In theory it may

be a pivotal play, but in practice it is just another shot that you

play to obtain the best results. Do not break up the rhythm of your

play or impose another psychological pressure on yourself by

considering the shot special. It is more important for you to play

each shot as well as possible and in support of the next. Such

consistency is an attack in itself, because the opposing team knows

it cannot rely on your errors to get points, but rather must earn them.

As receiver you have two goals when you return: to limit the

time the server has to get to the net by cutting off the angle of the

serve and taking the ball on the rise, and to make a low or difficult

return with enough angle to increase the flight time of the ball and

to pull the server away from the middle of the court.

Once you make your return, you try to thwart the advance of

the server by returning his or her first and second volleys and thereby obtaining a stand-off that eventually will neutralize the

server’s initial offensive advantage. Perhaps you can create an

opening for your own approach to the net by mixing speeds,

lobbing, or using any other such means to frustrate the serving

team. Unlike the server’s clear-cut tactic of serve-and-volley, your

tactic as receiver is to constantly probe to find the best way to get

to the net and disrupt the server’s offensive attack.

Even if you do not make the best possible return, your single

most important task is to make a return. If you are forced to make

an error, make it by hitting too deep rather than hitting too short

and into the net. There is always the possibility of the other team’s

playing it back.

As the server’s netman your role when your partner is approaching is to put pressure on the receiver by threatening to poach, poaching, or covering angles. Do not overplay so much that you constantly leave openings; the suggestion of movement is often

more effective than the actual poach.

Your partner, the server, can help keep your position offensive by serving to the receiver’s weaknesses, but you

must .be ready to cover all angles of service.

As the receiver’s netman you have two responsibilities before you settle in at the net: to cover the center in case the server’s netman poaches crosscourt, and to cover the alley in case the server makes his or her first volley down the line. Once the ball passes the server’s netman, you focus on the server and try to intimidate his or

her approach. If your partner, the receiver, is returning well, you may start tight at the net to pick off the server’s first volley.

However, if the receiver is returning weakly or the server is serving well or the receiver’s opportunity to come to the net is somehow

limited, then you start farther back in proportion to the weakness of the return. Whatever the reason, you must so position yourself

that you and your partner can develop a united attack.

Sometimes on the second serve you can stand as close as possible

to the center service line to psychologically reduce the serving area. You can afford to overplay this way because the second serve

usually brings a strong return that forces a weak volley from the

server. However, if the receiver returns weakly, you may be caught ~

in an overcommitted position. Also, you must be careful not to be

hit by the ball; if the ball touches you, the point goes to the server.

In fact, this is one reason you do not attempt this tactic on the first serve; the server could aim his or her first volley at you and win the

point.

Once all four players are at the net, everyone is on equal ground. All the advantages and disadvantages of starting positions disap-

pear, the separate considerations change to team considerations, and the points will be lost more by errors than by planned strategy

of attack. Balls will be played a bit too high or too wide, and the

opposing team will try to capitalize on such advantages. The key at

this point is to play advantages until an opening develops and then

to play the opening. If the first opening does not work, go back to

playing the percentages; with patience you will win the point.

Guidelines for Playing the Net

Although playing advantages is the general rule-of-thumb for

playing the net, there are several guidelines for you to follow when you and your partner are at net.

* When you play the net, you are completely committed to an

offensive position. And such commitment requires your making anticipated, well-played volleys rather than guess-and-swing swats

at the ball.

* The first person who can get to the ball takes the ball. You do

not have time to wait for the ball or discuss the pros and cons of

who should take the shot. Exceptions to this basic rule will stand

out, and you can change play accordingly.

* Be aware of possible responses in order to develop anticipation. Whether your opponent plays the ball to you or your partner, stay

ahead of the possibilities and maintain a constant awareness of the

ball. We have discussed some likely strategies, and you should count on just such shots from your opponents. Sometimes the

unexpected happens, but the percentage of such shots nullifies

their effect.

* Regardless of where your opponent plays the ball, reach out

your hand and lean your body toward the ball. This reaction gets

you in rhythm with the play, prepares you for any occurrence, and

encourages continual flow to the ball rather than jerky stop-and-

start responses. Also, reaching for and leaning toward the ball

keeps the ball in front of you instead of to the side, because your body, or at least your shoulders, are turning when your hand reaches.

- Do not turn your head independently to follow the ball. Because turning the head is a slow movement, your eyes must keep refocusing and the ball goes through blind spots. Leaning and moving your shoulders gives you sufficient peripheral vision to see your opponents.

the point.

We have discussed the basic net shots—volley, overhead, and covering the overhead. Now let us touch upon some of the basic

net tactics.

Poaching—crossing in front of your partner at the net and taking his shot—is perhaps the most dramatic net tactic. However, it often

creates more problems for your team than for your opponents, and

some of the problems have nothing to do with the success or failure

of the tactic itself.

There are definite times to poach, and both you and your partner must be ready to handle the response. You poach only if you can make a better shot than your partner to benefit the overall

position of the team: to take advantage of a setup (when your

opponent has been forced to make a defensive, weak, or obvious shot); to reduce pressure on your partner; to cut off an angle; or to

limit the time for the other team to get in position for a setup. Once you poach, you must continue to cross, and your partner

must go behind you and cover the vacated area. You cannot stop and return to your original position. For more discussion of

poaching see pages 100-103.

The lob volley also can be an effective tactic at the net, but it not only is precarious and difficult to execute, but also can turn you

into a target. You must be careful how you hit it as well as when

you play it.

Never play the lob volley from above the net; you will be

sacrificing an offensive shot for a defensive one. Also, play it when

there is no alternative. For example, if you are making a desperate

effort to reach a low shot and your opponents have closed the net,

your only hope is to go over them. Or if an angle pulls a netman

wide and he or she returns low and races to close the net again,

then a lob is perfect to take advantage of his or her overcommitted

forward movement. A lob also can take advantage of wind, sun,

lack of confidence, slow reaction, or a disheartened opponent.

One final word about the lob volley: always play it so that the

Opposition must move back, not stand still or move forward.

Hitting or Jamming an Opponent

Hitting or jamming an opponent when you are at net is an

effective tactic. When the ball is below the net, angle the ball to an

opening or to the body of an opposing netman, specifically to his or

her forehand hip (a difficult shot to get back). You hit at your

opponent not with the intention of hurting him or her but of

making that opponent step back and thereby creating an opening

for a better angle. Or, inversely, you may jam an opponent after

you have hit an effective angle that has put him or her off balance.

Whenever you are at the net and there is any question as to

where you should play the ball, play it low to the middle. A shot

down the middle always has plenty of court available to it,

confuses the opposition as to who should take the shot, and limits

the angles of return, thereby producing returns that are easy to

cover.

Take the Shot as High as Possible

A basic rule-of-thumb for playing the net is to take the ball as

high as possible. Sometimes your opponent will hit a drop shot—

the ball drops over the net and stays below net level even after it

bounces—and your response may be another drop shot. However,

do not make the mistake of not taking the ball when it is above net

level. Similarly, if the ball has dropped below the net level, let it bounce and come back up after its bounce before you hit it so that you can make a driving shot rather than having to play an angle or

defensive shot.

A commitment to offensive play is the key to playing the net. You might have to back away or play defensive shots when the opposition has a stronger offensive position, but at no time is your

position defensive—it is always offensive.

Let us examine the first building block mentioned above— percentage shots. Knowing why, when, and where to make a particular shot is as important as knowing how to make the shot. You determine the why, when, and where of a shot by its percentage of success. I have already discussed and evaluated

percentages of effectiveness of certain shots in previous chapters, but you must make your own analyses and thereby sharpen your

awareness of various possibilities and situations.

To evaluate the percentage of success of a shot, consider three

elements of equal value: the difficulty of the shot, the effect of the

shot, and the possible response of the opponent. For example, a

shot that is very easy to make might rate a 33 percent, but it may

have only a medium effect of 15 percent. If it allows the best

possible opportunity for the opponent, it merits 0 percent. Added

together (33 + 15 + 0), the shot’s chance of success is 48 percent.

Also, as difficult as it may be, you must separate the effect of the

shot from the response of the opponent to make an accurate

evaluation of the shot. The effect of the shot does not necessarily

control the response. For example, see Diagram 36, and assume

you are the server’s netman. To evaluate the effectiveness of your

Learning to play percentages requires developing an awareness and discipline that eventually becomes second nature. Although

this learning is not very exciting at first, once accomplished, it improves the quality of your play and the ease with which you handle tough situations and maintain a mental edge. Playing

percentages also simplifies play for you. If you cannot assess situations and possibilities, you can become so confused that your

strokes are hesitant, you let opportunities go by, and you and your

partner both converge on the ball and then let it pass.

Another building block of the game is anticipation. Anticipation

is too often considered a talent for guessing correctly. A better definition is awareness of ball-controlling factors. Developing such

awareness is like working on any other part of your game. You

must learn to break down and then reassemble actions and results

so that you can understand individual influences as well as their

role in the overall picture.

For the return of serve, you prepare prior to and during the toss,

and you anticipate from such indicators as score, glance of the

server before his or her toss, the position of the server on the

baseline, whether the server is right-handed or left-handed, the

direction of the wind, the success of previous serves, the server’s

grip, the condition of the balls, and the past history of the server’s overall play, especially his or her first volley. After impact, the

speed, angle, spin, arc of flight, and target of the ball all give you

information for the response.

For all other strokes, your anticipation has two parts: you prepare during the flight of the ball to your opponent, and you

respond to the return of the ball by your opponent. During the first

part you must consider the distance your opponent must move to

get to the ball and then get back in position, the amount of

backswing he or she uses for the stroke, his or her balance during

the shot, the angle of the racquet head as he or she swings through

the ball, the position of the ball in relation to his or her body, the

balance of the opposing team, the balance of your own team, and

the best percentage response. Once your opponent hits the ball,

your focus should be on the ball, not on your opponents. Evaluate

your best position of interception—the point from which you will

play back the ball. If your opponent has made a good shot, your

only possible response may be defensive. However, never accept

your position of interception—choose it. Always try to take the ball

at your best advantage, not your opponent’s.

After you have watched the ball and analyzed the possibilities

and have selected your point of interception, what’s missing? The

stroke, and often players do just that—miss the stroke. You must

ready your racquet when the ball is leaving your opponent’s

racquet. It must be in position to hit as the ball crosses the net, whether or not the rest of your body is ready. If there is not enough

time to get your body ready as well, at least you can bring the

racquet forward on the chance of getting back the ball. Perhaps

the best definition of anticipation is having your racquet ready all

the time.

Being ready all the time also means communicating with your

book; some use a wink or a look or a behind-the-back gesture. Betty Store and I played together for years and could make moves automatically without any signaling and could compensate for

is another very important building block. The score not only

indicates the count, but also your focus—whether you should probe

tactics.

plan, are the foundation of the momentum of your attack. You

maintain that momentum by recognizing the pivotal importance
