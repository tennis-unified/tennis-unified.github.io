Tennis is war. Players enter into a battle of annihilation worthy of any pair of boxers, any pair of knights of old who rode each other down atop charging warhorses. The sport is as aggressive an encounter as can occur between two persons who remain alive and on speaking terms at the end of the confrontation.

What else is tennis but an extended attack upon your opponent? With each stroke, you are trying to do something to him: make him run until he can run no more; jam him so that he cannot swing; trick him; rush him; throw off his timing;

break his game; defeat him. A tennis match is an assault conducted in a realm of sporting allegory that allows the participants to coexist once the combat is over, even to enjoy that combat while it is underway. The winner of a tennis match leaves the court strewn with the wreckage of his opponent’s game. It is only outside the confines of the match that peace is maintained, by the traditional handshake and all it implies.

Tennis is a wonderful game because it allows our competitive instincts to come raging forth, but in an athletic situation that will not injure us, a situation so devised that human society can welcome the display of these instincts rather than strive to suppress them. The rules of tennis give the player a territory to defend, and offer him the opponent’s territory and tennis game to attack through the medium of a moving tennis ball.

The etiquette that surrounds tennis is one of the most distinctive aspects of the game, and for years—before the recent tennis boom—it caused non-players to consider tennis a sissy sport. That etiquette is so thick because of the total aggressiveness which is at the very heart of the game. Players are directed to be always courteous, truthful, pleasant, and observant of the rules, while their games are left free to hack and chop at each other with brutal intensity.

Various players are known for their blazing tempers, but such attention to the personal behavior of a player is quite beside the point of a tennis match: it is the player’s tennis game which is conducting the assault, and personal behavior is relevant only to the degree that it helps or hinders the execution of the opponent’s tennis game. ‘Murder most foul” is what any tennis match is about; ‘“‘match” implies this and the murder that occurs is that of one player’s game by another.

Watch any tennis match between good players. | mean really watch it. No matador could go in over the horns and thrust for the heart with more exuberance than the great players go about their business of winning. Think back to the Outstanding matches of the recent past, ones featuring Borg,

Connors, Nastase, Ashe, Rosewall or Laver among the men,

Evert, King or Court among the women. In every one of the matches featuring these players—in fact, in any properly played tennis match—the winners went for the kill relentlessly.

The victories scored in topflight contests come as the result of a long process during which the winners create the most devastating tennis game possible for themselves, and the courage, determination, confidence and physical strength to test that game against any other in the world. Before they go on the court, the winners of these matches energize themselves by insisting inwardly that nothing will stop them from victory—least of all the tennis games of their opponents—and then they proceed to demolish those opposing games as efficiently and relentlessly as they possibly can, within the rules of tennis.

The weapon these players use in their assault against their opponents is not so much their racket or the tennis ball as the tennis game which directs each of these. The great players have internalized a complete set of automatic responses to any court situation which may occur in.a match, acquiring these responses through years of learning, practice, and competition. Because of these automatic responses, the world’s best players will tend, without thought, to hit the best possible shot in the most effective way out of any situation in which they find themselves:

it is this ability which makes them the world’s best. They have absorbed, over the years, a complete understanding of the angles and percentages open to them on any shot, and they routinely use this knowledge with deadly and unthinking efficiency to beat down their less able opponents.

It is a reasonable thing for anyone who plays tennis to decide that they would rather win than lose when they play.

Winning, after all, is fun—usually more fun than losing—and since someone has to win, it may as well be you. The very fact of keeping score implies a competitive situation, and it is this competitive clash that makes tennis so enjoyable.

If you decide that you would like to improve your game in order to win more often then you must strive to develop at least some of the automatic responses that make the best players so formidable. You must learn how to produce the most effective strokes possible. Then you must hone those strokes and your understanding of how to use them, through practice and play, to the point that your tennis game will prove superior to the games of your opponents.

The goal of this book is to serve as a whetstone to sharpen the cutting edge of your game, to give you a weapon equal to the assaults you may face on the tennis court. A principle of stroke production—topspin—is presented, with suggestions on how to go about learning it, and how you should use your strokes in competitive situations.

Topspin is the single most prevalent characteristic in the attacking games of all the top players in the world today, and you simple cannot hope to compete efficiently without it. The competitive aspect of tennis is a joyous reality, but you will be unable to partake fully of that reality until you develop a weapon—your tennis game—that is capable of dealing with those of the competitors you find around you. Topspin can help you create such a weapon.

Turn on the television and listen for five minutes to almost any well-played tennis match; you are sure to hear some reference to topspin. Topspin is as ubiquitous in topflight tennis today as long pants and skirts were 50 years ago. Topspin allows the pros to hit the hardest shots they possibly can and still keep those shots in the court. Topspin guides the delicate passing shot, letting it sneak over the net and then dip down before the opponent can close on it. Topspin is what allows adventuresome pros to hit full-drive lobs that still manage to come down inside the opponent’s court before bounding over the back fence. Watch film clips of the great players hitting groundstroke winners. Those winning shots were hit with topspin.

Unfortunately, it is possible for a beginner to take dozens of lessons from a variety of tennis pros and never hear a word about topspin. If the neophyte hears about topspin at all it will probably be through television or in conversation with other beginners, who use the term as though it describes some impossibly difficult and secret technique better players employ to keep beginners at bay.

In fact, the student should hear about topspin before he or she ever hits a ball. It is virtually impossible to hit a tennis ball without imparting some sort of spin to it, so that spin might as well be topspin. The strokes most pros teach—‘‘Turn, step, swing forward and up to a nice high finish”—are meant for topspin. But if they never say that the aim on the ground strokes is to roll the ball forward as it travels down the court, the student may never realize that this is the goal. He may never master topspin.

Topspin on the ground strokes, and a variation of it on the serve, is the best way to propel a tennis ball from one end of a court to the other in an effective, aggressive, and consistent manner. This is true no matter what your level of play...

With topspin, you have access to the greatest possible area of your Opponent’s court. And you can deliver the ball to all points of that area with power, if you wish to use it. But topspin shots need not necessarily be hit at a thousand miles an hour. Gentle shots hit with topspin can also be productive as the ball dips and dives and sneaks past your opponent. Topspin produces an efficient shot, requiring a minimum of effort to produce a well-hit, accurate ball, and even medium-paced swings will produce excellent results. No matter what your level of play, a drop of topspin added to the brew of your strokes will improve the flavor of your tennis game.

Topspin is not the easiest skill to learn. If it were, many more of the 30 million Americans who play tennis would be outstanding. But players who do not use topspin are, for the most part, hoping that their opponents will miss. Players who have learned the art of topspin will have the firepower to make their opponents miss. And once you have topspin in your arsenal of shots, you will discover that you not only possess an enhanced shotmaking capability, but also a new psychological perspective on the game which players without topspin do not, and cannot, have.

Before going on, we must arrange for some common terms when talking about a moving tennis ball. Looking down the court, what | call the back of the ball is what we see as it approaches. The front of the ball is around the other side, and it will lead the way down the court after we hit the ball.

This viewpoint from behind the court is different from what a player would actually see as he hits the ball, since at that instant the player would be standing to the side of the ball, rather than behind it. Actually, we are looking at the ball the way the racket strings would see it just before impact. These points on the ball will remain the same regardless of how the ball is spinning.

Now we are going to shift our viewing position to the side, so that we see what a player would see when hitting the ball, but the labels remain the same, as though we were still viewing the ball from the rear of the court.

If you could strike the ball absolutely flat on the back, with the force of the racket moving on a perfect line through the exact center of the ball, you would produce a true “‘flat” shot.

It would go away from you without any spin whatsoever, in a straight line, with only gravity pulling it down.

If you lower the point of contact from the back of the ball to a point down toward its bottom, but still have the force of impact moving directly through the center of the ball, you would again produce a “‘flat” shot. But this one would sail up into the air in a high parabola before gravity brought it back to earth.

Topspin is a combination of parts from each of these actions. To produce topspin, you hit the back of the ball, as in the first example, with a vertical racket face. But the racket (and the force of impact) moves at an angle, upward and forward, as in the second example. The result is a ball that is “topped,” the line of force now passing above the center of the ball.

A ball stroked in this manner will leave the racket in an upward direction because the line of force drives it that way. But it will also be spinnning—rolling forward—as the ball travels away from the hit, and this spin will cause the ball to dive back toward the court. This, then, is all there is to topspin. By lifting the racket face forward and up through the ball while hitting, and at the same time keeping that racket face fairly vertical, you produce a shot that at first rises into the air, and then dives back toward the court. And these are excellent characteristics to have on your ground strokes, since they must get over at least three feet of net before plunging back into your opponent’s court. With topspin on your shots you can drive the ball high over the net for safety while hitting it as hard as you can, and still bring that ball safely down into the opposite court. In effect, the user of topspin plays against an opponent whose court slants back and up from the net, offering a target like a wall. No other spin can produce such a target, and underspin (the opposite of topspin) in effect drops the opposing court down away from you, offering the most difficult target of all.

The reason the ball behaves the way it does when you hit it with topspin was first explained by Daniel Bernoulli in 1738. With 150 years of forethought, Bernoulli knew that tennis players would want to know why spin affects a tennis ball the 14 way it does, so he devised his theorem: the pressure in a fluid decreases as its velocity increases. Air is a fluid, and when a tennis ball is hit down the court it gives a velocity to the air passing by. If the ball is also spinning as it flies forward, it drags a film of air along with it, thus creating different air speeds on different sides of itself. The result is a difference of pressure on the ball from the air it passes through, and that pressure causes the vall to dip in flight. If you hit with topspin, you are asking air pressure to help you drive the ball back down toward the court, while if you hit with underspin, you are creating a pressure that will help hold the ball up.

The amount of. topspin applied to a ball depends on how the ball is struck. For example, if the racket’s line of force strikes the ball at a steep angle, the amount of topspin will be greater.

If the racket is swung on a flat plane through the hit, climbing only a little while the ball remains on the strings, a ‘‘flatter”’ shot (with less topspin) is produced. If the climb of the racket is very steep, much more topspin results. The shot will climb and dive more sharply, with less forward momentum. There is a range of possibility in the relationship between the amount of force moving the ball down the court and the amount that generates topspin: these forces vary according to how the racket drives through the ball. The opposite of topspin is underspin. Many players who pick up a racket and take a swing at the ball for the first time often hit down at it, and underspin is the result. The racket face is opened—tilted up toward the sky to get the ball over the net— and it slides under the bottom of the ball, producing backspin.

(The line of force from the impact passes below the center of the ball.) With a little practice, the players who hit this way will adjust their racket faces correctly, and the underspin shots will begin to land in the court.

There is a danger with underspin, however. If you don’t hit the underspin shot too hard, you can clear the net by a comfortable margin and still have this ball land in the court as well.

Putting backspin on the ball makes it want to climb against gravity—air pressure holds it up—but if you hit gently enough the climb dies and gravity pulls the shot back into the court. As you become more and more aggressive, however, and begin to hit harder, you will find that you have to bring your underspin shots lower and lower toward the net in order to keep them from sailing long. But this will increase your chances of hitting the net.

Even the underspin shots that do make it into play have drawbacks. The shot not only tends to sail instead of dipping back toward the court, but also it tends to be slower than if it were hit with topspin.

Because of these characteristics, a player at net has a fraction of a second longer to intercept an underspin passing shot. And if the man at net does get to the shot, the ball will be higher above the net than if it had been “topped.”’

In other words, underspin shots will bother an aggressive opponent less than topspin ones. With topspin, you can dip the ball down on your opponent’s side of the net, forcing him to volley up. You can hit sharper angles successfully as well, because the topspin shot will dip down toward the line, while the underspin shot keeps on sailing.

Of course, no player hits every single ground stroke with topspin. There are some situations where you would choose to hit with underspin, such as when you hit a backhand approach shot down the line to your opponent’s forehand, keeping the ball low, or when you want to drop a ball just over the net, bringing your opponent in. There are other times, as when you face such a big serve that you can do nothing other than block or chop it back, when topspin gives way to simply getting the ball into play. But in all situations except those where you actually need underspin (i.e., that backhand approach) topspin would produce better results if you could manage to employ it. In other words, the more you can train yourself to hit with topspin, the better results you will have.

Memorable moments come in tennis when a player instantly analyzes the unfolding situation, intuitively perceives how best to attack the opposition, and then produces that attack flawlessly. There is no thought; there is only a reaction to the situation at hand.

Such moments happen in an instant, but they are the end product of enormous amounts of careful learning and intensive, thoughtful practice. One must learn tennis before one plays it, after all.

It is important that you understand clearly how a person learns, because only you can teach yourself tennis. A common mistake is believing that any tennis pro or instructional book can teach tennis to you. They can show you the best way to go about learning, and the best strokes to devote that learning process to. However, you must understand the strokes, you must put in the hours, you must guide your body and you must strive to develop your strokes to their greatest potential.

Good tennis players move and hit effortlessly because everything they do happens automatically, without interfering conscious thought. These players have moved beyond the mechanics of hitting. They can devote themselves fully to the question of where to hit the ball rather than how to hit it. For the most part even that “‘where” is not thought out, but rather is a grooved, instinctive reaction to situations faced and solved a thousand times before. The good players play automatically. Their conscious minds may be offering encouragement or making plans between points, but mostly their minds are quiet while play is going on.

Can you play in this automatic way, similar to the world’s top players? Yes, without any question at all. The human body has an amazing talent for doing complex sets of motions automatically. And, happily, our bodies are very bright at learning new sets of motions, if you will just help it to learn in a reasonable way. If you can learn to tie your shoes, sign your name or drive your car, then surely you can learn to stroke a tennis ball correctly. The only things needed are time, understanding and application.

Different persons learn tennis at different rates, and sometimes one person may require varying amounts of time to master the individual strokes. Often, the reason for such varied learning rates is the amount of data students have in their minds about how. to move their bodies. Some people have never engaged in ball sports involving hand-eye coordination, and these people take longer to learn such a sport than people who have developed these skills. The non-players not only have to absorb the specifics of tennis, they must also learn a large amount of more general knowledge about how their bodies perform.

The best student | have ever taught, in the sense of how quickly she picked up the strokes, was a young girl of 16 who had studied ballet for over 10 years. | explained each stroke and demonstrated them in turn. Then | asked her to try. She reproduced them all, perfectly, on the very first try. This girl, who had played almost no tennis before in her life, advanced in 10 minutes to a point many people never reach in years. It was quite an experience.

The source of this young woman’s astounding performance lay in her training for ballet: because of those years, Jane had a beautiful control of all of her body, from the top of her head to the tips of her toes.

Most of us, unfortunately, have not had this kind of training. Our bodies are not fully under our command. Additionally, they are not programmed to learn new physical motions quickly and efficiently, as a dancer’s body is. We face not only the task of learning what the tennis strokes are, but also the problem of getting our bodies to produce the strokes when we finally comprehend them.

For most people, learning a physical sport such as tennis takes time—time to understand what the sport and its techniques are all about, and time to train the body to produce the required movements, smoothly and automatically. That time can be cut dramatically, however, if the student understands the learning process.

Two analogies come to mind to describe the learning process in tennis. One is that of an empty computer filling itself with data, the other is of somebody starting to cut a board with a Saw.

If you are new to tennis, your mind is like a computer without any data specifically relating to tennis on its tapes. You may have a good deal of information concerning how you go about moving your body, perhaps a fair amount on catching, throwing and hitting things. Or, if you play tennis but have developed faulty strokes, your computer has a great deal of material about tennis in it, but portions of that information are incorrect, and ineffective. In either case, you must work to gather correct data on how to produce effective tennis strokes, as well as information, in the second case, on why the strokes you have developed are incorrect. With this information in hand, you can set about printing a new control tape, through practice, where the motions of your body match your information about sound stroke production.

Now for the second analogy. When you begin to saw a piece of wood, the blade sometimes skitters over the top of the board, moving aimlessly from side to side. But if you make a determined effort to hold the saw on the proper spot, you get the cut started. After that, the blade stays in its own groove.

“Grooving” a tennis stroke follows a similar sequence. At first, the instructions to your brain go skittering about over its surface, producing almost random body motions. This continues until you make an effort to narrow things down, to control your stroke, and imprint on your brain the correct motion you want your body to perform. As the correct motion is re-peated, the groove in your mind deepens, and as that happens, it becomes easier and easier to repeat the motion smoothly and accurately. After sufficient repetitive practice, the motion becomes yours, something that you can produce automatically.

Unfortunately, an old, ineffective habit can have just as deep a groove as a correct, effective one. No matter how much you may wish to change this stroke, as soon as you have a ball coming at you in play you will go on “‘automatic”’ and define your swing by that old groove. Until you do the work to create a new groove, the old groove is the only one you have. To replace an old stroke with a new one it is necessary to create a new groove at least as deep as the old one, so that when you go on automatic in the face of an oncoming shot, you will produce the correct set of motions rather than the wrong set. Many players can quickly duplicate proper strokes under ideal practice conditions, but far fewer apply themselves to the repetitive practice routines that are needed to make a stroke automatic under all conditions, including play.

Luckily, you can form a new groove with less time and effort than it took to create the old one. As soon as you hit a single ball in an easier, more effective way, you will feel it instantly, and positive feedback will speed the learning process greatly. Correct tennis strokes feel good, almost effortless, and if you can hit just one, you are well along the road toward more correct play. If you can hit one shot correctly, you can hit a million. You can then deepen that groove through repetition. When you hit that first ball correctly you will have located the spot for the new groove in your mind.

Learning a new stroke also requires relaxation. Be easy. Don’t panic. No one is asking you to throw out your old strokes (bad as they may be) and reduce yourself to naked impotence in the face of your tennis enemies. Undoubtedly, while you are acquiring your new weaponry, it will be those old strokes that continue to do battle for you. Think instead in terms of adding new strokes to your repertoire. You must relax about the learning process—welcoming new ideas about how to produce tennis strokes and enjoying what your body does with those ideas—because you can’t learn if you are filled with stress.

And | will announce at this point one hateful but unavoidable truth: You are going to miss a lot of shots before you are done. You are going to miss every time you try something new.

You are going to flub shots you hit perfectly against the backboard or ball machine when you try to show them off to your spouse, tennis friends, or worst of all, your tennis enemies. You are going to have embarrassing moments, “‘off’’ days, and times when you are going to wish you’d never tried the game. In a word—you are going to lose. All of these bad things are going to happen to you, without fail. They have happened to everybody, even to Laver, Connors, Borg and Evert. All of the world’s great players missed before they began making the shots for which they are famous. But they all persevered through the low points of their tennis education. So can you.

In short, you are allowed to miss while learning. In fact, you are expected to miss. Very quickly, though, if your persevere, you will emerge on the far side of the learning process with strokes that are effective, and very much under your control.

Learning a new stroke requires thought, then thought and practice, then practice and play. You must prepare yourself to sneak up on a new stroke one step at a time.

1) You must understand what you are trying to do. |n the case of a topspin forehand, for example, you must realize exactly what you are trying to produce: a shot with a forward roll on the ball. Through reading this book, or instruction from a pro, or through watching and talking with other players, you must gain an understanding of the sort of stroke that produces topspin. When you watch good players play, really watch, so that you will see how clean and neat the required stroke actually is.

2) Get your body to duplicate the stroke you can now visualize in your mind. Standing anywhere, but preferably away from the tennis court, alone, and in front of a mirror, you must practice the swing until your racket duplicates exactly the motion you now know to be correct for the forehand.

In this second stage of learning, with a mirror or reflective window for reference, you are working to get in tune with your body, to realize exactly how the correct stroke motion will feel.

3) Apply your correct stroke to a ball. Toss a ball up with your left hand, underhand. It should bounce to your right, far enough forward so that it will still be out ahead of you after you pivot and step forward. (You can make it simpler by standing sideways to the net to begin.) Toss the ball high enough so it will bounce twice, in the same place, while you get ready. Then use your correct stroke when the ball is at the top of its second bounce, about waist high or a bit lower. Having hit the ball, don’t worry about whether or not you’ve hit it well. At this point, you are just trying to produce a good swing, and to see the effect it has on the ball.

Having hit the ball, you learn about your swing by observing the results accurately. How did the ball spin as it flew away from you? This indicates whether or not you have hit with topspin. Was the flight of the ball too high—did it go long out of the court? Or too low—did it go into the net? This tells you how open or closed your racket face was. Did the ball feel easy on the strings as you stroked through it, or did it clunk off the racket or turn the racket in your hand? This is the signal for whether or not you hit the ball in the middle of the strings.

Having noted all this information, try the stroke again; and again. Try to produce smooth, flowing stokes that carry the ball nicely over the net, but if something goes wrong don’t panic or criticize yourself. You are simply gathering data. The information will come flowing in if you observe it accurately, and it will come from mishits as well as fine drives.

4) Concentrate on keeping the shape of your stroke correct, and apply it to a moving ball. Have someone toss balls to you gently, so you can hit the balls easily. They should be tossed underhand, high enough so they will bounce and set up nicely for your stroke. Always use the correct swing—a complete, full one—even if you miss the ball with it at first. At this stage, hitting the ball over the net still isn’t as important as learning to produce the correct, topspin-producing swing in response to an approaching ball. You must assume the ready position in the middle of your opponent’s possible angles. This is the key to court positioning!

If your opponent contacts the ball at the center of his baseline, a ready position behind your own center mark would be quite correct. Standing there, you will be equally ready to get any of his possible shots. But if your opponent is hitting from either his forehand or backhand sidelines, for example, and you are standing at the center of your court, you are now quite out of position. Your opponent can hit down the line—the sideline defines one of his possible angles—or wide crosscourt, conceivably hitting to about where your service and sideline meet. If you stand in the middle of your court you are much closer to the shots down the lines than you are to the equally possible crosscourts. You are out of position.

To be ready you must position yourself in the middle of your opponent’s possible angles. Have somebody stand at the other end of the court and move from one side to another as though they are hitting shots. After each change of position look at the court, and figure out what angles your opponent could reasonably hit. Mark each side of the angle by placing balls in line with your baseline. Then stand midway between the two balls. This is the correct position to assume during play. Notice that as your opponent moves further to the side of his court, you will have to move further, but not as far to the opposite side of your court, in order to split in half the area you must cover.

Of course, in actual play your opponent may not be able to hit one or the other of his possible shots. If you discover this to be true (you learn by watching your opponent carefully during the warmup and first few points, to see what he can and can’t hit), you can slightly favor the side he consistently hits to. But don’t get trapped into trying to guess, out of habit. Good players command all the shots, and can hit to any point of your court they want to. Against better players, your end of the court will suddenly seem much larger as they consistently hit to the side you are not covering properly. So train yourself to split the possible angles before they hit.

Also notice how the idea of the “middle of the possible angles” helps determine your proper position at net. Have you ever noticed how, when the pros are at net, they will often stand off to one side of the court or the other, rather than over the center line? They are simply setting up in the middle of their opponents’ possible angles.

Imagine yourself at net, playing an opponent hitting from his right (forehand) sideline, in the backcourt. If you stand in the middle of your opponent’s possible angles you will find yourself to the left of your centerline. Yet you actually have your whole court covered. The point at which your opponent can get the ball by you the quickest is down your backhand line. But you are standing closer to that side when you are in the middle of the possible angles, so you don’t need as much time to reach your opponent’s shot on that side. You have left more court open on your forehand side, but you have a bit more time to reach a shot there because it must travel a longer distance before it gets past you. You are asking your opponent to “thread the needle’? when you position yourself correctly in the middle of his possible angles while at net, and very often he will prove unequal to the task.

Finally, face your opponent when you are getting ready.

Your feet should be perpendicular to the line that splits your opponent's possible angles. On service return, for example, many people stand with their feet on or lined up with their baseline. Yet the serve will be coming at them from an angle across the net, not straight down the court. Consequently, these players are cramping their stroke on one side in getting ready this way.

So, you have your ready position and are standing in the middle of the possible angles. The ball approaches, and now you have to return it.

You must move from the ready position the instant your eyes tell you where your opponent’s shot is going. If the ball comes toward you, so that you don’t have to run for it, the proper preparatory sequence is: turn, step, stroke. Turn toward the side the ball is coming toward, pivoting on the foot nearest the line of flight of the ball, with the knees slightly bent; step forward, parallel to the ball’s line of flight, so your weight is well into the shot; stroke through the ball. The turn gets your racket back and your body around, enabling you to produce the stroke.

You should be turned before your opponent’s shot from the backcourt crosses the net. You can check this whenever your opponent hits a shot into the net: is your racket back?

Also, make sure that you pivot on the foot nearest the bail, rather than stepping back with it: you want to attack the ball, not retreat as it comes at you.

On the turn, your shoulders and arms shifting as a unit do most of the work of getting the racket back. From the ready position, facing forward, you will find that your racket will have covered half of its entire backswing if you simply turn your shoulders to one side or the other. The backswing continues smoothly as you step forward, and then with your forward foot set you are ready to hit. At no time, on any stroke,

Should your arm be waving about at your side without help from your upper body to get it ready.

Your forehand turn does not move your body as far around as does the turn on the backhand. On the backhand you want to turn edge-on to the ball, as though you are standing sideways to fight a duel, your right shoulder pointing at the ball as it approaches. If you were to turn as far on the forehand, you would find yourself hitting around your own body, since on the forehand your arm is on the side away from the net.

When you turn, on either the forehand or the backhand, the racket should point back toward the back fence at the end of the backswing, but no further. Try standing with your back right against a fence, as though you were hitting along the line of the fence. Put your racket back. Your backswing and your follow-through will be just right when limited by the fence you are standing against.

At first, while you are learning, you will turn and with a straight backswing point the top of your racket back and slightly down toward the back fence as quickly as possible, move into the hitting position, and then stroke. Later, as you become more used to hitting the ball, your stroke will become smoother and more continuous, often incorporating a long, looping motion from start to finish (for timing), the racket flowing through the spot where it points back toward the back fence rather than being put there at the very start. In either case, you must turn as quickly as you can, as soon as you determine where your opponent’s shot is going.

Your step comes a moment after the turn, and it is your fine tuning for getting into position. If the ball is coming just right your step will be forward, your knee bent, parallel to the ball’s line of flight. The phrase “‘step into the ball’ refers to this step parallel to the ball’s line of flight.

With experience, you will know how long your racket is and how much space you must allow between your body and the ball’s line of flight. If the ball is coming a bit too close for comfort, you can step forward and slightly to the side. If it isa bit too far away, you can step toward its line of flight to get within reach.

If the ball is going to be really out of reach, then instead of just turning on your pivot foot (the foot nearest the ball’s line of flight) you must begin stepping toward where the ball is coming, with the pivot foot, to get into position. After that, you Step forward to the ball’s flight with the other foot, as usual.

Many players have a great deal of difficulty with balls that come right at them. These are easily handled, however, if you use a smooth response resembling a dance step. For the ball right at you, you take what would normally be your pivot foot and step around behind your other foot with it, away from the ball’s line of flight. Then you step forward with the other foot and there you are, after just two steps. You will never learn to do this, however, if you don’t practice it. Stand on a court, or in your home, and pretend that shots are coming right at you. Practice the footwork until you can perform all the movements smoothly and automatically.

On volleys the action is quicker but the principles are the same. Here it is important to step with the foot that is on the opposite side of your body from the ball’s line of flight. You should hardly turn your upper body on the volley, and you will use up too much time if you make any move with the foot nearest the ball. If you get in the habit of stepping incorrectly for simple shots with the foot nearest the ball, you will tend to do the same on balls that come wide, instead of crossing over. If this happens, you won’t have the “‘reach’”’ that you would have if you automatically stepped across your body with the other foot. On the volley, except on very wide balls over six or eight feet away from you, you should move only one foot once, the foot away from the flight of the ball, to reach the shot.

For balls right at you, you still step only once, but forward and to the side away from the ball, rather than just forward or forward and toward the ball. At the same time, you reach far forward with your racket, your arm slanting across your body on the forehand, out to the side of your body on the backhand, so that your racket face meets the line of flight of the ball.

One important point to be stressed is that you do not want to be moving with the step when you hit, on either groundstrokes or volleys. You want your front foot firmly planted on the ground to anchor you as your upper body produces the correct stroke. The proper sequence is: turn, then step, then hit.

Once you’ve put your weight out on your forward foot you have supplied yourself with energy for the hit. Now all you have to do is apply it, through your stroke. If golfers could get additional yardage by lunging or running forward past the ball, they certainly would. Yet they do nothing of the sort; they work to keep their heads in place throughout their swing. The same is true of baseball batters: they step forward, then swing. It is the same with tennis strokes: you gain nothing if you try to combine your step and stroke at the same time.

When you actually have to run to reach a shot you still must move in an easy, controlled manner. Ideally, you want to run to within stroking distance of the line of flight of the ball, then step forward parallel to that line of flight, so that you can stroke the ball normally. If the run doesn’t work out naturally and easily for you at first—most people do this correctly without any thinking or instruction—try running through the correct movements without a ball coming at you. Start the run by stepping with the foot nearest the ball—your pivot foot on normal shots—and take an odd number of steps to get within reach of the ball. You will arrive beside its line of flight on the proper foot this way (right foot for a forehand, left foot for a backhand). You are then ready to step forward with your other foot in the proper manner.

When you find yourself running for a ball, pretend your running legs and the top of your body are separate entities. Turn your shoulders and take the racket back at the same rate, with the same relaxed motion and timing, as you would when simply turning and stepping forward for a shot. Do all this while your legs are covering the court for you. When you are hurrying for a shot, very often you will intercept the ball at the moment you should hit it. If your racket isn’t back by then, your run will have been useless. Also, make sure that the effort your legs are making to get you to the ball does not get into your stroke as well: you still want to take a relaxed, correct swing.

Whenever you do have to run hard to get to a ball in the backcourt, your safest reply generally will be a deep crosscourt.

It is an easier shot to hit safely because you hit over the lowest part of the net into the greatest area of court. Also, you will have less work to get into position for your opponent’s next shot, in the middle of his possible angles, than if you hit down the line.

If you are running hard after a shot and can’t pause and step along its line of flight before you hit, don’t worry. Just produce the best stroke that you can. You would like to be fast enough to get into position and step along the line of flight of the ball before hitting, but if you can’t, then just go ahead and swing, and try to be faster the next time.

Whenever you have been run wide on the court, and you decide to play a crosscourt in response, be sure to make an extra effort to hit up through the ball. A very common ailment on this sort of crosscourt is to hit the ball into the top of the net, so counteract that by hitting slightly higher into the air than you may think is necessary. After investing the effort you have to get to the ball, it is a shame not to keep it in play, and as you will soon discover, that little extra bit of lift on the ball will cause it to clear the net nicely.

Once you have hit your shot, you still have to get back into position for your opponent’s next stroke. Instead of running back into position, however, you want to slide or skip sideways to get back, your body facing forward as you move, your feet not crossing while they slide you to one side or the other. The reason for this is that you must not turn and run back to your ready position, or even cross your feet, because your opponent may hit before you get back to the proper spot in the middle of his possible angles. He may hit behind you, crossing you up. If you slide back into position while intently watching the flight of your shot, you will be ready to move to either side.

You are still not done with body movement once you are back into the proper spot to wait for your opponent’s next shot. Most good players wait for a return by bobbing up and down in the ready position, on the balls and toes of their feet. This keeps their weight in motion so that it is easier to move for the next ball. The bobbing is done in place, with the feet coming just slightly off the court.

Control of the ball depends on several factors: where the ball

Strikes the racket strings; the angle of the racket face at contact;

the direction in which the strings travel during the hit (i.e., how much topspin you apply); how fast your racket moves during contact and where that contact occurs as the ball moves past you.

To hit a tennis ball well, you must hit it in the center of your racket, in its “Sweet spot.’’ When the ball collides with the racket at this point, the strings stretch and rebound uniformly, resulting in the greatest control possible. If you hit off-center on any stroke, the racket will wobble and turn in your hand. The rebound on such a hit will not be uniform, and the resulting shot may be far worse than your stroke warrants.

When you do hit off-center, you will feel a “‘clunk”’ between racket and ball. This should signal you that it is time to relax your grip and arm, watch the ball carefully to the point of contact, and strive to hit the ball with the exact center of your racket face. You don’t want to tighten your grip, as that will deaden the racket and your feel for the ball, and will do little to help you hit with the center of the strings.

On the ground strokes (and the volley), the angle of your racket face controls how high or low the ball will go after you hit it. An “open’’ racket face (strings are slanted upward) is going to cause the ball to go higher into the air than a “‘closed”’ racket face (strings facing down). In general, you will make most of your strokes with your racket face slightly open from the vertical, as your shots must climb up and over the net before dropping down again. You control this open or closed element with your wrist, by rotating it slightly one way or another. You do not control your racket face by changing your grip! To control the height of your shot over the net, turn your wrist the small amount needed, before you hit the ball. Once set, however, your wrist should not change through the stroke.

All of this may sound quite complicated, but it really isn’t once you get the idea by trying a few strokes with different face “‘settings.’’ Go ahead. Open your racket face way up and hit a ball too high. Then close it and hit the ball into the bottom of the net (using the correct stroke to do each). Experiment with opening and closing your racket face, so you can learn what sort of adjustments are needed for which particular shots. Just make sure you hit with topspin at the same time.

No single setting can be used for all strokes. You will need all sorts of settings, and the different shots that result, while playing. If your opponent is in the backcourt, you will drive your topspin ground strokes with a fairly open racket face, so that the ball will clear the net by perhaps four feet, carrying deep into your opponent’s court. (Depth is controlled more by racket face opening and the shape of the swing than by how hard you hit the ball.) If your opponent comes into net, you will close the racket face slightly from its previous setting, thus keeping the ball low over the net and forcing your opponent to volley up. The swing will stay the same, but the face setting will change. If you should desire to lob, you will open the face way up, and stroke up through the ball at a steeper angle.

There is a range of possibility here. You can close the racket face and just clear the net, or open it way up to hit the shot much higher and deeper. You must strive to gain control over the complete range, and make your control automatic through practice and play.

In the above, we talk about changing the flight of the ball by changing the angle of the racket face, while keeping the shape of the swing the same. It is important to realize that you can also change the flight of the ball by varying the amount of topspin you put on it. In this case, you keep the angle of the racket the same, but change the direction that it takes through the ball. On one side of the spectrum of possibility, you may lift a slightly open racket face so abruptly up the back of the ball that you put a tremendous amount of topspin on it, but it will hardly go forward over the net because you haven’t driven through it. The other end of this range is to hit through the ball with a nearly flat swing, causing the ball to bullet away with very little topspin on it at all. You haven’t changed the racket face, but you have radically changed the flight of the ball by driving that face through the ball in two different directions. (In the first example, the line of force imparted by the racket barely clips the ball, passing well above its center, while in the second, the force goes almost through the ball’s center.)

Proper topspin lies between these two extremes, with your racket brushing up the back of the ball to put topspin on your shot, but also hitting through the ball enough to drive your shot forward over the net. Just as there is no one setting for the racket face on your strokes, so there is no proper amount of topspin: you will use topspin to do many different things with the ball, and you must experiment to discover how much topspin is needed to produce different results.

Still another aspect of the stroke which must be under your control is how fast you swing the racket through the stroke. Invariably, in the interests of hitting harder, starting students swing the racket faster and faster, and then wonder why their shots have almost no control—and often, no speed—as a result. To exert control on the ball, the racket must remain in contact with the ball long enough for compression between the ball and the strings to take place, and then for both to rebound smoothly.

If you swing too fast, this compression doesn’t happen properly, and far worse shots—light, flighty ones—take place than if you had swung at a slower speed. It is also harder to judge the amount of topspin you are putting on the ball when swinging really fast. | To hit harder, you must concentrate on taking a long step forward, in order to get your weight solidly into the shot. You must hit the ball squarely in the middle of the strings, and you must employ a long, flowing swing rather than any sort of jerky, violent one in order to make proper contact with the ball. Once you are really secure in all this, you can begin trying faster swings for still more pace, but now you will know when the swing becomes too fast for control, and can keep it at a more manageable speed. If you wait until the ball is almost to you before you start your swing, you will have to swing very fast to make contact at all. If you feel you are doing this, concentrate

On starting your swing sooner, but go through it more slowly so that you still meet the ball correctly. You will “carry”’ the ball longer this way, and produce better shots.

With our discussion of the angle of the racket face, we described how control is exerted over the vertical direction the ball takes after the hit. To hit the ball accurately, you must also control the left and right direction.

The horizontal direction of your shots will be properly determined by where you take the ball as it passes by you. If you make contact with the ball early—out ahead of you, beyond the normal distance at which you want to hit it—you will tend to hit the ball crosscourt. As you reach forward, your racket face will stop “looking” down the court and begin to face off to the side more and more. If you take the ball further back than this, right at the point where the strings face directly down the court, you will hit your shot straight down the court. And if you take the ball still further back, you will tend to hit it wide.

Actually, to hit in different directions you want to swing at the ball with exactly the same swing all the time. You want to keep your wrist and arm constant throughout the swing. Don’t slap your wrist or poke out your elbow to change the direction of your shots. What does change is your stance in relation to the ball coming toward you, and where you hit that ball on its line of flight past you. Although under playing conditions you may find yourself forced to change your swing to change the direction of your shots, you would hit far more secure, solid shots if you changed your stance instead.

Select a line on the court and pretend that a ball is traveling directly down that line toward you. Take up your stance for stroking, already turned toward whichever side you wish, and put out your racket so that its strings face directly down the line. If you made contact with a ball exactly here, it would travel right back the way it came.

Now, to hit a crosscourt shot, don’t move your arm or racket at all, but instead move your back foot an inch or two closer to the line, your front foot an inch or two further away from it, turning your stance in the new direction you want the ball to travel. Keep your racket face positioned directly over the line along which our imaginary ball is coming, but note how it now intersects that line at an angle, at a point a few inches ahead of where it was placed to hit directly down the line. If you swing exactly as you normally would, but intercept the ball at this new point, you will hit the ball crosscourt.

To hit wide, toward the side opposite the crosscourt, you must move your front foot closer to the line, your back foot further away from it, again turning your body in the new direction. Now the racket will cross the line a bit further back than it did when you hit down the line, and the ball will be driven wide.

In practice, think of giving yourself more room from the ball to hit down the line or wide from the center of the court than you need on crosscourts. (Most novices hit crosscourt because they tend to get too close to the ball’s line of flight. If they were to try to hit straight down the court from this too-close stance, they would hit the ball on the throat of the racket, or even on their hands.) The important idea to grasp is that there is no need for any sort of poking or jerking with the racket in order to drive the ball in different directions. You want to use your normal stroke, and simply change your stance to allow that same stroke to drive your shots where you want them.

In effect, by changing your stance you are changing the point of contact between your strings and the ball. On the forehand, for example, you would want to hit the ball directly at

With these details of horizontal control in mind, along with those on vertical control, you are now equipped to make very accurate evaluations of the shots you hit in practice. If you can’t accurately diagnose what you are doing wrong on a missed shot, it will be hard for you to make the necessary correction on the next one.

If you hit the ball into the net, for example, you should think about your racket face, and try to open it further on the next shot. If your shots are going wide to the right on the forehand or to the left on the backhand, you are hitting the ball late, taking it too far back beside your body, and you should try to swing a moment sooner. If you find yourself hitting solidly, but your shots are landing short in the opponent’s court despite your opening up your racket face, perhaps you are hitting with too much topspin. Swing on a flatter plane through the ball to carry it long. If your shots are going out over the baseline, use more topspin to bring them back down.

The most common mistakes that students make while learning a stroke are those of hitting off the center of the strings, hitting with too open or closed a racket face, putting too little or too much topspin on the ball, swinging too fast, or making contact with the ball too early or late as it passes by. The interesting thing is that all of these ailments have nothing to do with any sort of mistake in the shape of the stroke itself. Students may have perfect strokes and not know it because of the poor shots that result from these problems.

The hope is that you will realize just how few things can go wrong with a stroke, learn to identify the characteristics associated with each of these common mistakes, and then work to correct those mistakes. In each case the correction is easy if the problem is only properly identified.

If you are to hit well with topspin on the ground strokes, you must have a clear image of the sort of shot you are trying to make—an idea of its shape—as well as an understanding of the mechanics involved. With topspin, you should think of lifting the ball up and out toward the top of the fence behind your opponent, rather than of trying to hit it out toward the top of the net or in some sort of direct line toward a point in your opponent’s court.

Often, when novices struggle and fight to get topspin on their strokes, they have little success. Their minds tell them that they must hit out and through the ball toward some point in their opponent’s court in order to make the ball go there. On your ground strokes, on both deep drives and shorter shots, your topspin strokes will not have a flat contour to their flights over the net, but rather quite a curved appearance from your racket into the far court. You can vary the degree of this curve (through racket face and the degree of topspin imparted), but you want some sort of curve to exist on your shots in order to bring them back down into the far court.

The stroke with the most curved appearance would be the lob. The lob is a simple shot to hit, much simpler than most people at first try to make it. And it works nicely when a bit— or a great deal—of topspin is added to the shot. To lob, all you have to do is open up your racket face, and swing your racket up through the ball on a steeper climb than you would use on the ground strokes. Many people try to poke the lob up into air with an abrupt, hurried jab, and that just doesn’t work.

The point of ball contact on the lob is down near its bottom, rather than at its back. By varying where you hit the ball, you vary the sort of lob produced. An offensive lob is low. It is usually hit with a lot of topspin, and is designed to clear your

Opponent’s upstretched arm and racket by only three or four feet when he is at net. The idea behind this lob is to get the ball over your opponent quickly, on a shallow plane, so that he cannot catch up with it in the backcourt. A defensive lob is much higher, and it is hit to give you time to get back into play from some sort of difficult position. Usually, the defensive lob is hit out of desperation, and it is thrown up into the air with any sort of swing you can manage, topspin or underspin.

The lob is a very important shot to have. It provides you with a whole new means of getting the ball past your opponent, and also a means to say “time out’’ whenever things get a bit difficult for you. (In play, you will want to lob over your opponent’s backhand side if possible: if he should reach it, he will have more trouble hitting it back effectively off the backhand.) Try to remember to lob offensively a time or two early ina match. If you are successful, your opponent will tend to stay further back from the net, and your passing shots will then have more room to get by him.

A very common problem on the ground strokes is that of taking the racket too far back behind the body on the forehand, and wrapping it too far around the body on the backhand. This occurs because players have the mistaken idea that such an enlarged swing will give them more power, or simply because they are unaware of either where their rackets are going or where they should go. In fact, to produce your best shots, especially in fast play, you want to employ a compact swing, with your elbows kept close to your body, and the racket well under control on the backswing.

After learning to hit properly with topspin against balls at waist height, many novices still have great difficulty realizing that they can apply the same stroke to both lower and higher balls.

For low balls, you must bend your knees as well as step far forward to get your weight down, and then swing smoothly and bravely with a full swing from below the ball to above it. Obviously, you won’t be able to get the racket as far below a low ball before stroking up through it—the ground is in the way.

On balls that come high over the net and are obviously going to bounce high as they pass you, there are four possible responses. You can move back in your court, so that the ball will pass its high point after the bounce and drop down before you hit it. If you do this, make sure that you get turned and move back fast enough to give yourself time to step forward again, getting your weight properly into the shot. You will probably hit your most solid shots off high balls if you follow this pro-cedure properly, but this is the worst choice of the four. By moving back you will give up three or more yards of distance between you and your opponent, meaning that no matter how forceful a shot you hit, your opponent will have extra time to remain in command of the point by moving in on or cutting off your effort. After all, it takes extra time for the ball to cover that distance you have moved back, and it must cover it twice, going down the court and coming back.

If you stand your ground, the ball will be at your shoulders or higher as it passes you. Doing this, they consistently hit the ball in the net. What they don’t realize is that a three-foot difference in height for the point of impact makes little difference in relation to a 78-foot-long tennis court. A proper topspin stroke will still work perfectly well on the higher ball, and will still bring the ball back down in fair territory.

While big, powerful serves usually get most of the attention in tennis, in terms of speed and power the world’s great serves must take a back seat to the world’s greatest service returns. When executed brilliantly, as by Jimmy Connors or Bjorn Borg, the poor server simply supplies the power for the return these players fire back. No matter how huge a serve they face, you will see these players, and all the other good ones, stepping forward to get their weight into the shot, then swinging cleanly through the ball for driving topspin returns.

The server in tennis is said to have the advantage because he controls the first hit of the point. He can try to fool or overpower his opponent with that first hit. He gets two chances at this planned assault, and he can advance to net as soon as he has hit his serve. The receiver, on the other hand, must wait for the serve to bounce before he can hit and attempt to advance to the net as well.

If you simply chip the ball back low at the server’s feet, i.e., punch the ball back with a short, downward, chopping motion, a competent server will consistently manage to hold serve against you. He will routinely play a good enough shot off your return to remain in command of each point. In order to win against the server you must take the advantage away from him. You must learn to hit out on your returns. You must—you guessed it—learn to hit your service returns with topspin! Anything less will not put enough pressure on a good player to produce errors.

The service return should employ a much more compact swing than that used on a normal ground stroke: things are happening too fast for you to do anything more. On normal ground strokes, you will see players looping their rackets for timing. With a full, looping backswing of your arm, throw a tennis ball from behind your baseline, overhand, like a pitcher (but with your feet on the ground). Throw it down the length of the court. Now grasp another ball and instead of throwing it forward, throw it up at an angle into the air, so that it passes high over the net as it travels down the court.

With this second throw you have come close to duplicating the proper motion for a forceful, dependable serve.

If you threw the balls with a full pitching motion, you dropped your throwing hand down past your side, then back toward the back fence. At this point you bent your arm at the elbow and cocked your wrist so the ball was two or three inches behind your right ear. You then brought your elbow whipping around and extended it and your arm as you turned your shoulders, snapping your wrist forward and throwing the ball. If you replace the ball with your tennis racket and perform roughly the same action, you will have a good service motion.

The serve uses the snap of your wrist and the extension of your elbow to accelerate an already moving racket head up through the ball, which you have carefully placed in its path.

How hard your serve is depends on how fast you are able to accelerate your racket head, as well as on the usual elements of timing and how accurately you hit the ball in the middle of the strings. How well you direct your serve depends on how well you control your racket face, and also on how well you impart a variation of topspin to the ball.

As on the ground strokes, you must learn to hit up past the back of the ball if you are to have any sort of consistent, hard service. Because of the overhand nature of the serve and the design of the human body, you will not be able to put absolutely vertical topspin on the ball. But you can learn to send the racket up past the ball, the racket striking the back of the ball before traveling off the ball’s upper, right-hand ‘‘corner.” Getting the ball to spin this way will improve your serve more than any other spin, because you are commanding the ball to come back down toward the court after it clears the net.

Every single good server in tennis today, man or woman, hits toward the upper right-hand area of the ball on their serve (or toward the upper left-hand corner if they are left-handed). These players may look as though they are hammering the ball down toward their opponent’s court, but this is an illusion. If the top players were to let go of their rackets just as they made contact with the ball, those rackets would not fly down toward the court. They would go sailing up into the air and off down the court toward the right-hand fence. What makes it look as though the server is pounding the ball down toward the court? He reaches full extension of his arm just after hitting up through the ball, and at that point centrifugal motion takes over, pulling the arm and the server’s body forward and down.

The impact on the serve is up past the ball, however, and not down toward the court. As on the ground strokes, the angle the racket takes on its drive up and forward through the ball may vary from shallow to steep, but in every good serve the racket is climbing. Even the “cannon ball’’ serve is not flat—it would go into the court so seldom as to be useless. Instead, it has enough forward spin on it to give it the characteristic topspin curve down into the service court. A good second serve has much more spin on it because it is hit with less forward motion through the ball, with much more brushing up by the ball—and this allows a far greater clearance and margin for error over the net.

But it too is hit according to the same principle, with the racket face snapping up past the back of the ball and off toward the upper right-hand area.

Serving is similar to pitching in baseball, in that you want to get the ball to the target but never give the opposition anything that they can hit well against. You must practice the serve until you have control of the motion, and then you must practice it some more, until you have complete control over where you place it in your opponent’s court. Just as your wrist gives you vertical control on the ground strokes, by opening and closing the racket face, so does your wrist (by turning the racket face) give you left-to-right control on the serve. To serve to one corner of your opponent’s court you do not change your stance, nor do you place the toss in a different spot. You simply turn your wrist one way or the other, slightly, as it snaps the racket head up into the ball. With practice this will become automatic, so that you can think where you want the ball to go and your body will automatically direct it there.

When you are playing, you must not try to clobber the ball on serve before you have learned control to handle it. There is nothing sillier than seeing powerful men or women blast their first serves consistently out of the court or in the net, then use a little patty-cake shot to get the second ball into play. If this is the case with you, take some of the steam off your first serve and strive for control, and then add a little of that steam to your second serve so it isn’t a powder puff. As somebody once said, you are only as good as your second serve. So work on it.

Most good players exert just as much energy and swing the racket just as fast on the second serve as they do on the first.

But they brush up by the ball much more on the second serve, hitting through it far less. This puts much more spin on the ball, so it clears the net by a safer margin while still falling into play.

Good servers also make sure the second serve lands deep in the court, to keep their opponent back as much as possible. And of course, the second serve should go to the backhand.

Although you may only be as good as your second serve, it is better to never use it, getting the first serve in instead. As soon as you miss your first serve, you may notice your opponent breathing a sigh of relief as he steps forward to clobber your second serve. To avoid his increase in confidence and aggression you must get your first serve in, and you might consider hitting a second serve, first, to do this. If you are not putting 70 per cent of your first serves in, you are not serving properly and you should do something about it—such as getting the first serve in no matter how slowly you have to hit it.

When serving, and as an aid to getting your first serve in, do not try to always hit the ball as hard as you can. You will miss too many serves, and you will tire just as your opponent begins to get used to the consistent fast speed. Good players serve quite firmly, but they always keep a little of their service speed in reserve. This enables them to change the pace, and to blow the ball by their opponent when they need to.

Finally, practice the serve as much as you possibly can, even when it seems to be going well. Your goal is to groove it so solidly that you can hit it smoothly and accurately not only when you are loose and relaxed, but also when you are tight and it is match point. When you get tight, your serve will go off faster than any other stroke, and the only means you have to prevent that from happening is practice. A powerful, accurate serve continually under your control will be one of your most valuable assets, but you will have to work to establish that control.

The overhead is directly related to the serve. It borrows a portion of the service motion to deal with balls that your opponent lobs over your head. The secret to a good overhead is position: you must move until the ball drops through the area where you would toss the ball on the serve. In this way you will be able to hit the ball as solidly as possible, exactly as though you were serving from very close to the net. Because of this, overheads are wonderful shots to hit. You get to serve a second time, even after the point is underway.

Since you are usually much closer to the net than on serve, you can hit down toward the court. You don’t want to hit too steeply down toward the court, however, unless you mean to bounce the ball clear over the back fence. If you do hit down steeply, you come dangerously close to the net, and if you don’t hit the ball over the back fence your opponent may be able to hit an overhead back at you. Instead, try to angle your overhead off toward the corners, or deep into the backcourt. You have such a positional advantage that you should have no trouble winning the point through placement, and you must refrain from over-hitting. Far too many overheads are missed because of a great mania that grips almost everyone; they try to pulverize the shot.

If the lob should catch you in the backcourt, or force you there, you must change your plans somewhat. From here the net is just as dangerous as on the serve, and you are not in control of the toss. Instead of blasting the ball, or even expecting to win the point outright, you should try to hit the ball well over the net and deep to your opponent’s backcourt, simply playing it back firmly instead of trying to blast it. If your opponent comes in to the net, however, you may as well blast, because if you don’t he will volley your weak shot away for a winner. The volley is the karate chop of tennis; the short, explosive punch that finishes a well-played point. It is the shortest and most compact of all tennis strokes, yet one of the most powerful strokes when properly timed and executed. It is employed best when you are close to the net, taking advantage of that commanding position. And the stroke is hit with backspin, the opposite of our beloved topspin.

There are three reasons for this. The first is that you can produce backspin (for control), with a short, quick, downward punching motion with the racket, making contact with the ball well out in front of you. Second, the underspin shot will shoot off your strings and stay low in your opponent’s court, tending to skid and forcing him to hit up to you if he gets to the ball at all. (Topspin, which is hard to hit with control against a fly ball, tends to cause the ball to bounce higher, and you don’t want that when you are in at net. Your opponent has a better chance of passing you when he hits off a high bounce.) Finally, if you punch the racket face forward and down the back or slightly under the ball, you can hit forceful shots back over the net from below your side of the net, allowing you to cope with the best dipping shots your opponent can produce.

Try hitting a spin second serve, making sure that you get it deep in the service square to the backhand, and you will find yourself able to get much further forward for a more effective first volley.

You must practice all the elements of the serve-and-volley game—the accurate, forcing serve, the controlled first volley or half-volley, the decisive final volley from close to the net, and the overhead. And then you must practice the entire sequence as a whole.

Once you are in at the net, you must learn to differentiate between those balls you can hit winners off of, and those you must play back deep or wide, to keep control of the point. At times, you may find your opponent as close in to the net as you are. When this happens, you must keep the ball low and wait for him toyhit a high shot which you can then pound past him or down at his feet. In fast exchanges close to the net, try to hit your volleys at your opponent’s right hip, or else down at his feet, as these are the toughest spots to volley from effectively. In such a situation the paramount concern is to watch the ball: you don’t have time to watch anything else. Also, you must snap back into the ready position after each volley, so that you will be ready to go instantly in either direction for the next ball. Don’t try to guess—be ready! Far too many novices don’t get back to a good neutral ready position, but wait for the next shot slightly favoring the side from which they have just hit. This is a disaster in any sort of quick exchange.

The only way to learn the proper speed of reaction and readiness on the volley is to stand in with someone else and volley back and forth over the net as quickly as you both possibly can. Keep the ball going, and force yourself to hit and get ready faster and faster.

A very useful shot in head-to-head confrontations at the net, or when you have brought your opponent in to the net witha drop shot, is the lob volley. This shot is produced with the short, punching volley motion, but you open the racket face way up, to pop the ball up into the air over your opponent’s head. This forces him to turn and run back for the shot, leaving you in command at the net. The lob volley is a wonderful shot—except when you hit it too low, setting up your now-smiling opponent with a killing overhead. The lob volley, like the drop shot, is a grandstand shot, one that is a great deal of fun to get away with, but also one that you don’t want to try on a crucial point unless you have total confidence in your ability to hit it.

It is a shot that you need to practice before you try it out on your local tennis enemies.

Finally, some tennis players experience a great deal of fear when they find themselves close to the net, facing their opponent’s attack. The easiest way to overcome such fear is to practice your volley, and the proper way to deal with balls that come right at you. If you watch the oncoming ball continuously, you will be able to protect yourself with the racket. With practice, your reaction time will improve, and you will be able to volley that dangerous ball back effectively. However, you must remember that the key word here is practice.

To learn tennis, you must set up a regular practice schedule for yourself. This practice time should be separate from your playing time, and you should go out onto the court in quite a different frame of mind from when you play.

In practice, your goal is to repeat the correct stroke in the correct direction enough times so that it will become natural and automatic. Any stroke, no matter how bad, will feel “natural” if you hit it enough times. So you must make sure that you are practicing the correct strokes. In practice, you will use your conscious mind to form and control the strokes you hit. After sufficient practice, you will be able to relax mentally and produce the same strokes, simply and automatically, when you play. Practice has to be a regular, repetitive effort, because it is only through such repetition of the correct movements that those movements become grooved into your mind.

However, correct tennis strokes do not automatically make you a good tennis player. They give you the means to become one. Armed with your good strokes, you must seek out the best possible play you can find, so that you can learn to use those strokes in effective patterns of play. There are many levels to the game of tennis, and just as you feel you’ve got the last one licked, the next looms ahead. Proper tennis practice, however, goes beyond hitting tennis balls with a racket. Good tennis requires a body able to meet the demands of play. If you are to play well, you will want to devote some time to getting your body into condition.

Sit-ups are important, to give your torso the strength to twist and reach and bend throughout a long match. Kangaroo jumps—knees coming up to your chest as you jump in place— aid in building leg strength and leaping ability. Do them in units of 10-20 at a time, several times a day. A prolonged series of crossover steps, where you stretch as wide as you can to one side, then recover without moving your pivot foot if you can, then go wide to the other side, help you with your speed and reach at the net. Jumping rope improves your agility and ability to take the quick little steps that are needed for proper positioning in tennis.

In any of these sorts of exercises, it is better to do lots of small units of them throughout the day or week, rather than putting in one huge workout that kills you. Many people find that a mild routine of weight lifting and exercising is beneficial. Use a 10- or 15-pound weight, preferably one in each hand, and do three sets of 10 motions once or twice a day. This will increase your shoulder, arm and wrist strength, keep your muscles in general tone, but avoid the bulking of muscle that is common when major efforts are put into lifting truly heavy weights.

Some therapists believe that regular lifting of light weights will aid in preventing or reducing the effects of tennis elbow by conditioning the whole arm to the impact of the strokes.

Many people in the world could play far better tennis than they do, with exactly the same equipment they currently have, if they were just confident in their abilities. Doubles is combat conducted on a team basis. The great joy of doubles arises from the successful combination of two players, through astute generalship, into a winning team. It often takes years for a doubles team to reach its full potential, as each player learns what to expect from the other, and also learns how to play in such a way as to mesh his abilities with those of his partner.

Let’s begin by talking about court position. In the forehand court, the proper place from which to serve is only slightly further out from the center of the court than you would serve in singles, a matter of three or four additional feet. Serving from this position rather than from far out by the sideline, gives you a better shot at your opponent’s backhand corner. You also collapse the angles of return open to your opponent, allowing your partner at net to stand safely out toward the middle of his half of the court. From his position your partner can pick off your opponent’s attempts at returning the ball back to you. If you serve properly to the backhand corner of the forehand court, your partner at net can safely stand as far out as midway between the center line and the singles sideline and still cover the alley. (He can cover the alley by just stepping over with his right foot and sticking out his racket.) At the same time, by standing out so prominently, your partner at net gives the opponents something to think about, especially since he can also cover most of the middle of the court by just stepping across the other way.

Your netman thus becomes a real threat. Too many netmen stand carefully with one foot on the singles sideline, and for all the good they do there, they might as well go sit down and watch. If you serve from wide on the forehand, however, you will tend to serve to your opponent’s forehand, and your partner will be pinned to the alley. So don’t do it.

As for serving to the backhand court, the rule about standing in close to your center is a little less strict. You probably will have to stand out two or three additional feet—so that you are midway between the center mark and the singles sideline—in order to get your serve past your partner at net and still wide to your opponent’s backhand. You don’t want to stand too far wide here either, as again you offer your opponent easier angles of return by your netman.

By serving from closer in toward the center mark than many people normally do, you collapse the angles that are open to your opponents, and their returns must now come closer to your partner at net. The whole point of doubles is to hit your shots in such a way that your opponents’ replies will end up going to you or your partner at net.

After your serve, though, you must remember to run forward on a line more toward the net person than toward the receiver, in order to cut off the open area of your court. In other words, you serve and then move in toward the middle of the receiver’s possible angles into your half of the court, so that you can easily reach whatever he returns.

For good doubles, you must get your first serve in, and you must serve it to your opponents’ backhands. At times, of course, you may vary the serve wide to a forehand, in order to keep the opposition honest about where they stand to receive, but your standard serve for doubles must be to the backhand.

In doubles, a fairly hard first serve placed carefully in the backhand corner is a far better delivery than a slugger’s blast, which may not be as accurate. A serve of slower pace allows you to get further in toward the net if you come in behind your serve, the careful placement to the backhand allows you to control the point, and because of that placement you will know what sort of a return to expect. If your serve should accidently slip off to the forehand, you will suddenly be facing quite different angles of return, and may find yourself quickly in trouble.

Please let me stress this. If you serve to the forehand in doubles, or miss your first serve consistently, you are being stupid! You are yelling to the world that you haven’t a clue about how to play winning doubles. Serve to the backhand consistently, on the other hand, and get your first serve in all the time, and you will be known as a doubles hero. You will win.

The serves don’t even have to be particularly hard, they just have to be directed to the backhand.

In doubles, the team that holds the net generally controls the point. For this reason, you’ve got to train yourself not only to follow your serve in, but also your return of serve. To gain command at the net, where you can hit down at your opponents, you must be able to follow your serve in, and your return as well. To do this, you must develop a very good, accurate first volley, so that you can takea low ball at or just inside your service line, and volley it back to the receiver, keeping it low and away from the netman. To produce such a volley, you must learn to get your body as low and as forward as possible, so that you can reach out for that first volley.

As a receiver, you must gain control over your return, so that you can hit the ball hard, but also low and away from the netman, back toward the server. You will also want to get to the net after returning, so you must learn to take the serve on the rise and move in right behind it. If you can learn to hit a topspin return with consistency and control, it is by far the most forcing shot possible. But it also requires that you take up more of a set stance to hit, and you may find it useful to occasionally reach out and simply volley the serve back—hit it with an abrupt, downward, under-cutting punch, as you move forward toward the net. (You can take the ball more at your side with this stroke and be closer in toward the net when you hit.)

The hardest return of all is the backhand crosscourt from the forehand court. The serve will tend to curve into you, yet you must get it back crosscourt to keep it away from the netman. | think it is quite easy to return with topspin off the forehand in either court, and off the backhand in the backhand court, but this return is tougher. Here you might consider chipping the ball regularly—that short, punching volley motion—or else realize that you must move very well to get far enough away from the curving ball to hit it back crosscourt with topspin. There is a tradition in doubles that the better player blithely assigns the forehand court to the weaker player, but if you want to do well as a team, you had better work to upgrade that weaker'player’s ability on this difficult return, through practice.

The game of doubles is won at the net. You and your partner should spend a lot of time volleying for quickness, both of you standing within the service line and keeping the ball going, snapping yourselves back to the ready position between shots, moving forward for even faster volleying as your ability improves. You must work to keep all your volleys /ow over the net, so that in play the opposition will be forced to volley up to you. You and your partner can also practice starting in the backcourt and working your way to net, volleying aggressively as you advance. To make such a drill more realistic, come in on a diagonal, just as you would in actual play.

The lob volley can be very useful for getting your team out of a jam at net. The idea is to take a volley from your opponents, and instead of punching it back at them, open up your racket face while punching under the ball, thus pitching the ball up over them. The lob volley depends on surprise for its success, and great accuracy on your part in judging just how high to get it up to clear your opponents. It can be a very good shot, except when it doesn’t work, in which case it often is very bad: you will have a tennis ball for lunch.

The change-of-pace shot, usually a serve hit at a much slower speed than normal, can be very effective in doubles. This slow serve allows the server to get in much closer to the net, it gives the server and netman time to switch sides if they so choose. And the slower serve usually proves very difficult to hit: the receiver must adjust his timing radically to hit the ball, yet he must manage a more accurate shot than usual to keep the ball away from two netmen and still in the court. Such a change-of-pace shot can have devastating effects on your opponents, not only because it tends to throw off their timing, but also because it gives them still one more thing to worry about.

Some of the prime ingredients in good doubles, then, are consistency of shot off all strokes, the ability to serve accurately to the backhand, an equal ability to keep the ball low over the net, the timing and willingness to poach, and inventiveness in giving your opponents problems to worry about. Beyond these points, however, lie one or two less obvious but still important facets of the game of doubles.

To play good, winning doubles, you and your partner must be able to talk over whatever problems come up. For example, if you are serving too often to the forehand or not keeping the ball low enough on volleys, or standing in an incorrect position on service return, your partner must be able to let you know about it, and know that his telling you won’t throw off your game, or make you angry at him. You must even be able to suggest to one another that as a team you change your entire style of play, if what you are doing isn’t winning the match. If your opponents are just too strong for you to overpower them at serve-and-volley, then you must try standing back, as a team. If they seem to thrive on hard hitting, you must switch to soft shots. If staying back or hitting in various ways isn’t working, you’ve got to be able to work out a plan to try something else. Non-communicative doubles teams, teams where each player isn’t certain that his partner is firmly on his side no matter what is happening, are usually non-winning teams.

Perhaps the most important element in playing good doubles is this, that you remember that you are playing doubles. Your partner is your partner, not an enemy to be abused when he misses a shot. He is going to miss shots—as are you—and you mustn’t suddenly turn on him, to let him know that the lost point and possibly soon-to-be-lost match are all his fault. Such behavior is stupid in the extreme—but all too common, especially in the juniors—because instead of being supportive, you are acting in just such a way as to cause your partner to miss more shots. The old saying about cutting off your nose to spite your face certainly applies here.

The name of the game is doub/es, and in this form of tennis you face the common enemy with your partner. It goes without saying that each of you is going to try as hard as you can to play as well as you can. When you walk out on the court to play doubles, you do so as a team, and you must accept whatever happens on the court as a team.

In tennis, points combine to make games, the games collect into sets, sets form matches, and enough matches make a tournament. There is usually an ebb and flow in the course of a good tennis match, first one player and then the other taking command. In tennis, a player doesn’t have to win all the points or games in order to emerge the victor. In fact, he can win less than half of them. But almost invariably the winner of a close match will have a better understanding or “feel” for the rhythm of the ongoing game than his opponent.

If you are to win at tennis, singles or doubles, you must develop this feel for the course of your matches. In this way, you will be able to ride the changes of momentum without despair, and seize the final victory. You must learn which strokes need to be hit hard for winners and which need to be carefully controlled; you must learn which points are the most valuable, demanding your greatest effort during each game; you must learn to recognize which games you have to have; and you must gain experience in sensing when to fight without quarter to hold a set and when to let one go in order to fight harder in the next.

All of these involve rhythm, the sense of what to do when, and while you can be made aware of these aspects of tennis through being told, you will absorb this knowledge only through constant play, and through thinking about that play. “‘Learn from your mistakes” is a thundering cliche, but that does not lessen the truth of the phrase.

A tennis point is very much like an extended dance between you and your opponent, or a complex fight with the blows delivered through the moving tennis ball. You have something you should be doing every moment that the point is underway: preparing to receive or deliver a shot, hitting that shot, moving into proper position to await the next shot, etc. And if you are to win the point you must do all these things better than your opponent.

As you gain experience, you will become more and more aware of the shape of the court, the dynamics of the game, and what shots you can (and cannot) hit with safety. For example, a down-the-line shot may be a great winner, unless your opponent gets to it. If he does, he has a much better chance of volleying a winner crosscourt against you than if you had hit your first shot crosscourt. If you hit the crosscourt, he will be forced to hit much more parallel to your opposite sideline if he chooses to hit in that direction. You have to move a shorter distance to reach your opponent’s best possible shot if you force him to volley off a crosscourt, so therefore that shot is safer. By all means hit winners down the line, but realize that if you don’t hit a winner, you’re in trouble.

In the course of play, you will also identify the worst possible shot that you can make, other than not getting the ball into the court, and you will work very hard to avoid that shot. This worst possible shot is the ground stroke that you hit from wide off one side or the other from your backcourt toward your opponent’s stroke on the same side. In other words, you hit from wide on your forehand toward his backhand in the center of the court, or from wide on your backhand side toward his forehand in the center of the court. Ever notice how, whenever a good player plays a lesser player, the lesser player’s court seems suddenly to be much bigger, the lesser player forced to run from corner to corner to corner? Watch again, and you will see the player with less ability hitting what I’ve termed the “worst possible shot’’ over and over and over again. Hit the ball crosscourt after a run, and you won't have to run so hard in the immediate future.

If you are to be successful at tennis, you must follow a two-headed course of action: you must not make a monster out of your Opponent, and you must always do the worst possible things imaginable to that opponent.

Taking the negative side first, you must not make a monster out of your opponent. You must not serve to their forehands, you must not hit back toward the middle when you are running wide, you must not throw up short lobs. You must not allow backcourt players to stay in the backcourt or net lovers to camp out at net, you must not stand back on slow, high-bouncing serves, and above all else, you must not encourage your opponent by showing temper or discouragement. In sum, you must not help your opponent to win in any way; you must not sabotage yourself into a loss. All of this seems perfectly obvious, but in nine matches out of 10 the players ignore this advice. The cure against making your opponent a monster is to think during a match. Between points, which you will play automatically, you’ve got to think about what is going on, how you are playing, why your opponent is making those marvelous shots and what sort of shots you hit previously to set your opponent up. All too often, players submerge themselves so much in their matches that they have no idea what is going on, and hours later may wake up to wonder ‘“‘Why in the world didn’t | lob once to keep him off the net?” “‘Hey, did | hit all my serves to his forehand?” or something equally dreadful.

The positive side of the competitive situation in tennis might be summed up by saying you must always do the worst possible things imaginable to your opponent every time you hit the ball.

And just hitting the ball back into your opponent’s court is not a bad start. If you can hit the ball deep to his backhand, that will be even more horrible. And then follow that with a drop shot, and then a lob, and then perhaps a topspin drive to the other side, and you will really make your opponent feel awful.

And that is the idea. From the first ball you hit in a match, you want to do something to make your opponent wish you would stop. (But remember, hitting the ball 200 miles an hour— but out—is not something that will bother any opponent.)

Remember the last time you saw a good player competing against a lesser opponent? You may have noticed how every time the good player hit the ball, he did something with it. The better player never ‘‘just aims for the court.’’ Instead, he exerts control over the ball, gained through practice and endless play, to hit shots which will force his opponent to work hard to return the ball. In any match, the superior player is superior exactly because he is able to produce more difficulty for his opponent than his opponent can produce for him.

Tennis is very similar to the game of chess. In both, you must think in terms of position and the geometry of the playing area, you must mount well-prepared attacks, you must keep an overall view of how the entire game is progressing, and you must think in order to defeat the changing plans of your opponent. And in both games it is utterly disastrous to make a move or hit a stroke that does nothing for you, that invites your opponent to launch an attack of his own rather than be forced to defend against yours.

A good way to plan an attack for yourself in general, without seeing any particular opponent, is to think of every shot you enjoy receiving, and then resolve never to hit such shots to your Opponents, think of every shot you hate receiving, and dish out plenty of those. Having some sort of plan when you go out to play is almost always better than having no plan at all, even if your plan is wrong. A strategy gives your body a clear course of action to follow, as well as improving your attention to the match. Your game will often be much sharper as a result.

Once on the court, you must mold your battle plan to your particular opponent. He may, for example, be left-handed. To hit to this player’s backhand, you will have to hit toward his forehand corner. You will have to serve wide to the forehand, and so on.

Even with right-handers you will want to examine each player you come up against, both when he is playing other players and during your own warmup. At this time you should try to answer various questions: does your opponent run well from side to side? Up and back? If not, force them to do what they are weak at doing. Is their volley penetrating, or does it fall short? If short, bring them to net, give them lots of volleys to hit, and pass or lob them off their short responses. Is one or the other of their ground strokes faulty? If so, pound that stroke, and lob them at net so they must stay back and take that pounding. Are they very consistent? Break their rhythm by varying the length, direction and pace of your shots.

One can go on and on with these questions, but the idea behind them is to think about your opponent during the course of your warmup and match, and figure out what you can do to him that will be effective.

The goal of all your thinking—and all your play—is to find means of encouraging your opponent to miss while not doing so yourself. By far the greater number of points in tennis are lost by somebody missing than are won by somebody hitting a brilliant winner.

Unfortunately, that method will not bring much success once you have moved up a few notches. Against better players, you must not miss. But you also must hit an offensive enough shot that your opponent cannot take command of the point with an attacking shot of his own. In order to win, you must learn to attack your opponent, through shots that are forceful, wide away from him, deep right at him, or very short at net, and if he manages to deal with these, then you must attack some more. Don’t miss, but as soon as you decide to “play safe,” or to “protect your lead,’ you are well along the way to the loss of a set or an entire match. You must learn to attack behind forceful and consistent shots. If your shots have one of these characteristics but not the other, then go back to the practice court.

Your attack must be a balanced one, combining consistency, accuracy and even delicate touch with raw power. A well-timed drop shot or accurate lob can be just as devastating as the blistering passing shot. An ideal goal to strive for is the ability to hit the ball so hard and so accurately that no opponent can return it. But before you get to that ideal state, you are going to have to at least pass through more normal realms of performance, where you will be forced to deal with your opponent rather than simply smack balls past or through him. For this reason, you must strive to master every aspect of the game in order to jerk and yank and tug your opponent from one point to another, from one mistake to the next.

Your attack in tennis can take many forms. Just as efficient play in tennis is almost a model of Zen paradox—you’ve got to try like the devil, but remain relaxed at all times—so too must the nature of your attack remain relaxed and flexible. Physically, you cannot play efficiently by tightening every muscle in your body, or by hitting every ball at full power. And mentally, too, you must remain relaxed and flexible, able to vary your game plan rather than seize on one system no matter what happens. Of course, if the system you seize on wins for you, and continues to win, stick with it. But if you begin to lose, you must give up one system of attack for another, willing to do whatever is necessary—within the confines of proper tennis—to grab victory from your opponent.

You must play enough to gain a sense of what shot Is appropriate for what situation. If you can win a point with a ground stroke hit at a certain pace, use that pace, and don’t try to hit it harder than necessary. On the overhead, hit it just as hard as you need to win the point, not harder in order to impress the gallery. That also increases your risk of missing. Keep your hardest shots in reserve, for when you really need them, and in that way surprise your opponent with them. Any winner is by definition the correct shot for that situation, but often the delicate soft shot is a more infuriating—and safer—shot than the blasted one.

In a tennis point, talking of the ‘‘appropriate shot”’ is just another way of saying that you must develop “rhythm.” You must gain a sense of the right shot for every situation, and use it, rejecting any other sort of shot, or pace that doesn’t fit the situation. Often, people will stroke two or three balls up and down the middle of the court during a point, then suddenly bash the ball away, attempting a winner. They miss, of course, because they haven’t followed the proper rhythm that every tennis point demands. Winning a tennis point is an act similar to that of landing a fish. Only here the line that brings in your quary is one made of time, and your prize is the point. If you play well, utilize all the area of the court and do something with the ball each time you hit it, you remove time from your opponent little by little. Finally, he is unable to get properly set to hit the next shot accurately—and you put away the result—or else he is not able to reach your shot at all.

In tennis, everything during the course of a point will tend to either add up for you, or else add up against you. That is, if you serve deep rather than short to your opponent’s backhand, you give yourself a bit more time to move toward the net, while asking your opponent to deal with a more difficult shot on his

(probably) weaker side. Having moved a bit further in toward the net, you will be able to volley his weaker reply a little bit higher in the air, a little bit closer to the net. This allows you a better, more angled, shot than if you were hitting from further back and lower down. And such a shot will land further away from your opponent, travel by him at a faster pace and allow him less time to get ready. He will have to run harder over a longer distance to reach your shot, and probably will not hit quite as good a shot as he would have off a nearer one. This gives you a still easier ball to deal with, and by then you'can hit a shot that takes away his time entirely: he won’t be able to get it, and you have reeled in your fish.

Pressure in tennis is what you apply to your opponent when, point after point, you force him to deal with a difficult shot every time you hit the ball. To produce pressure, you cannot play hard and well on some of the points. You must play hard and well on all of them. Do this enough, play all the points accurately and hard enough, and you will discover that almost every Opponent you face will eventually crack. Pressure can build for your opponent not only through the severity of your attack, but also through your manner: if you just attend to business, always, carry yourself as a winner would, and never get angry or in some other way distract yourself from the work at hand, your opponent will never see any hopeful signs that you may be on the verge of cracking. And he will be that much closer to doing so himself.

Pressure is a cumulative thing. It will add up through a match, wearing an opponent down, often turning a 6-0 first set winner into a third set loser. Above all else, you must learn never to give up On a match, no matter how poorly you are playing. Time after time, especially among the juniors, you will see players decide that they are not playing up to their personal expectations, and then obviously “tank” a match, when in fact they could win if they just gave their best, and applied as much pressure as possible despite playing at less than their best.

Accepting defeat before your opponent has forced it on you is stupid. It is insulting to your opponent, who should have to earn his win. But far more importantly, it is insulting to the tennis game you have labored to create. You will have off days, but that doesn’t mean that they have to be losing days as well.

Don’t waste a match just because you think you are playing poorly. Stick in there, give your game a chance, put as much pressure On your opponent as you possibly can, and make the effort to see if your less-than-perfect play might not still be good enough to win. Often, you will win through such effort, but you will never discover this unless you play as hard as you can, no matter how your game is performing, right up to the final point.

If your Opponent should win under such conditions, when you have tried as hard as you can, then fine. Congratulate him.

You know that you gave it your best, you have the satisfaction of knowing that you didn’t give up under trying conditions, your game is stronger for the workout against adversity, and you will play better next time. For goodness sake, don’t play badly and give your opponent the match on a platter besides.

Make him earn the match, make him prove he can beat your game even on an off day. It may turn out that your opponent can’t beat you, and that he will crack under the pressure you produce simply by not giving up. For that matter, by trying as hard as you can, your game will many times come back into focus, and begin functioning properly again, making the win that much easier. But this will never happen if you give up before play is ended. Who is to lose and who is to win in a tennis match is never decided for certain until the last point has been played and finished.

Attitude can decide if any particular player is to become a winner or loser at tennis. The moment negative thoughts creep into your mind, even on particular points—‘‘God, | hope | don’t double fault,” “‘l hope | don’t miss this return”—you have moved a good distance toward the loss you want to avoid. It is as though your playing mind, only half listening to your conscious mind, picks up the words ‘‘double fault” or ‘“‘miss this return,” takes them for command, and produces the horrid event.

On the other hand, positive thoughts such as, “This first serve is going in” or “I’m going to belt this return down the line no matter where he puts the ball,” can work miracles for your tennis game.

The most aggravating form of negative thinking for a tennis pro, or for anyone watching someone play whom they want to do well, is negative body posture, an ailment distressingly common among juniors. The slumped shoulders, the hanging head, the racket head smacked down into the court as a player walks back toward the baseline after losing a point, all of these do your opponent almost as much good as eight consecutive service aces. You are on the court to play, not to perform an exercise of self-sabotage. Fake the upright carriage, the quick walk back to get ready to win the next point, if you must. You may discover yourself much more positive about the point to come. As an added benefit, you have increased the pressure on your opponent, because he will begin to worry that you don’t get upset when he wins a point. Your goal whenever you go onto the tennis court is to play the best tennis you can, not to produce an impromptu performance out of a Greek tragedy.

Tiny variations in skill separate most winners from most losers, and separate touring pros from those players just below them. The actual differences may be small, but they can represent years of difference in training and dedication, and very decisive differences in scores between better and lesser players. There are many levels of tennis, and each aspiring player must attend to his homework faithfully in each of those levels if he hopes to graduate on to the next. And no player ever graduates without confidence and a positive attitude.

It is very important, however, that you don’t try to vault up several levels of play when you find yourself confronted with an opponent considered better than you. Many players try for such brilliant winners in these cases that they miss shot after shot, and never find out just how good their opponent actually is.

You must try to get the most out of your game, of course, but remember that you aren’t going to serve like a Tanner or return like a Connors until you have put in the years of effort they have. Until that time, simply serve and return and play the way you can, and ask your opponent to prove that he can deal with that level of play—the best you can actually produce—rather than any far higher level of play that you are not capable of sustaining. |

An important concept to understand—no matter what your level of play—is that of ‘“‘“momentum” in a tennis match. Time and time again, in everyone’s career, there will be matches where you win the first set easily, only to drop the second as though you had never held a racket before. The culprit here is not your play, but your inability to hold onto momentum.

When you have dominated a set against a player of comparable ability, and won it easily, you must realize that you stand at a point of potential weakness. You have reached the goal that you've been striving for, and it is all too easy for your mind to say, with a sigh of relief, ‘Well, I’ve got it at last, that’s done.”

With these words it is you who may be done! Your opponent has gained nothing—he is well along the way to being shoved out of the match entirely—while you have gained everything, as far as the set is concerned. So your opponent is going to begin to fight with everything he has to stay alive in the next set, at the very moment you begin to let up. The result is often one of those strange 6-0, 0-6 reversals.

If you are aware of how all this can happen, however, you will harden yourself to fight for those first few games at the start of any second set after you’ve won the first, as though it is you who were down. By doing this, you present as firm a barrier to your opponent’s progress as ever.

If your opponent should win the second set, it is now his turn to be at a point of potential trouble: He has now reached a goal, the second set, and it is now A/s mind which may sabotage him with thoughts of success. It would be easier to avoid these difficulties by insisting that you play your best, firmest, most consistent and unforgiving tennis during those first two or three opening games of the second set, after you’ve won the first set, to make sure that you take the second as well. But if that second set should slip away, do wake up, realize that the momentum will shift back to you if you just force it to, and make your counterattack at the start of the third. Often the momentum will swing back, and you will redeem your lapse in the second set by still winning the third, decisive set.

Another way of saying all this is: never let a beaten opponent back into a match through the mistake of slackening your attack. Why risk losing? And realize that you can slacken your attack before a match is even begun, by underestimating your opponent, or by foolishly listening to nice people who tell you “Oh, you won't have any problem with that player, you’re a cinch to win.” | Whatever you do, always keep in mind that any opponent can always get back into a match until the last point is played.

You must also learn that on important points you absolutely must not hope that your opponent will lose them, you must insist on winning them yourself. If you do not attack purposely on important points—serve into the body and come to net, for example—your opponent may not lose them to you, and you may never win.

If you have read this far—through the mysteries of topspin, the intricacies of the serve and volley, and the various suggestions | have made for improving your game—you are obviously interested in pursuing tennis excellence. Something about the game has captured your attention, and held it beyond the initial stages of purchasing a racket and some balls and seeing what the sport is all about. You have discovered that it isn’t quite as easy as it looks, yet something keeps you involved.

Even though you play tennis against an opponent across the net, you are also involved in a very intense confrontation with yourself. First, you must train yourself to play, and then you must gain control of your physical and emotional self in order to play the best tennis you can.

Pogo said in his famous line, ‘‘We have met the enemy, and he is us.’’ Nowhere is this more true than in tennis. But what joy if you can meet this enemy and overcome him. How splendid it is to discover that you can concentrate for a whole match, or that you can exert yourself successfully; that you can keep from getting angry, or tight, or scared on crucial points, and instead actually respond effectively and aggressively at these moments. If you do gain a degree of control over your enemy self—and you surely will, with any involvement in the sport— you will reap many of the same returns of self-awareness and self-expansion that are offered as reward to pursuers of more exotic disciplines, such as Yoga or meditation.

But beyond these rewards lie the still greater ones held in that single word so often frowned upon while violently pursued in our society: winning! It is enormous fun to compete with someone, and through the exertion of your abilities and self-taught talents, beat them. There is no question within a tennis match what each player is trying to do: each is trying to win. And each player accepts the aggressiveness of the other, because he is trying to return that same aggressiveness.

Tennis is so rewarding, however, that you may even lose a match and still feel that you have won. If you play up to your limits, and perhaps forge beyond them for a short time, there is no shame in losing to a better player. In short, tennis is rewarding for anyone who plays it (no matter what their level of play), as long as they give their best, and strive to explore their potential in the game.
