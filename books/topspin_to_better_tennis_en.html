Tennis is war. Players enter into a battle of annihilation worthy of any pair of boxers, any pair of knights of old who

rode each other down atop charging warhorses. The sport is

as aggressive an encounter as can occur between two persons

who remain alive and on speaking terms at the end of the confrontation.

What else is tennis but an extended attack upon your opponent? With each stroke, you are trying to do something to him: make him run until he can run no more; jam him so that

he cannot swing; trick him; rush him; throw off his timing;

break his game; defeat him. A tennis match is an assault con-

ducted in a realm of sporting allegory that allows the partici-

pants to coexist once the combat is over, even to enjoy that

combat while it is underway. The winner of a tennis match

leaves the court strewn with the wreckage of his opponent’s

game. It is only outside the confines of the match that peace is

maintained, by the traditional handshake and all it implies.

Tennis is a wonderful game because it allows our competi-

tive instincts to come raging forth, but in an athletic situation that will not injure us, a situation so devised that human society

can welcome the display of these instincts rather than strive to

suppress them. The rules of tennis give the player a territory to

defend, and offer him the opponent’s territory and tennis

game to attack through the medium of a moving tennis ball.

The etiquette that surrounds tennis is one of the most distinctive aspects of the game, and for years—before the recent

tennis boom—it caused non-players to consider tennis a sissy sport. That etiquette is so thick because of the total aggressive-

ness which is at the very heart of the game. Players are directed

to be always courteous, truthful, pleasant, and observant of the

rules, while their games are left free to hack and chop at each

other with brutal intensity.

Various players are known for their blazing tempers, but

such attention to the personal behavior of a player is quite beside the point of a tennis match: it is the player’s tennis game

which is conducting the assault, and personal behavior is relevant only to the degree that it helps or hinders the execution

of the opponent’s tennis game. ‘Murder most foul” is what any

tennis match is about; ‘“‘match” implies this and the murder

that occurs is that of one player’s game by another.

Watch any tennis match between good players. | mean really watch it. No matador could go in over the horns and

thrust for the heart with more exuberance than the great

players go about their business of winning. Think back to the Outstanding matches of the recent past, ones featuring Borg,

Connors, Nastase, Ashe, Rosewall or Laver among the men,

Evert, King or Court among the women. In every one of the matches featuring these players—in fact, in any properly played tennis match—the winners went for the kill relentlessly.

The victories scored in topflight contests come as the result

of a long process during which the winners create the most devastating tennis game possible for themselves, and the cour-

age, determination, confidence and physical strength to test

that game against any other in the world. Before they go on

the court, the winners of these matches energize themselves

by insisting inwardly that nothing will stop them from vic-

tory—least of all the tennis games of their opponents—and then they proceed to demolish those opposing games as efficiently

and relentlessly as they possibly can, within the rules of tennis.

The weapon these players use in their assault against their

opponents is not so much their racket or the tennis ball as the

tennis game which directs each of these. The great players have

internalized a complete set of automatic responses to any court situation which may occur in.a match, acquiring these responses

through years of learning, practice, and competition. Because of

these automatic responses, the world’s best players will tend,

without thought, to hit the best possible shot in the most effective way out of any situation in which they find themselves:

it is this ability which makes them the world’s best. They have

absorbed, over the years, a complete understanding of the

angles and percentages open to them on any shot, and they

routinely use this knowledge with deadly and unthinking efficiency to beat down their less able opponents.

It is a reasonable thing for anyone who plays tennis to

decide that they would rather win than lose when they play.

Winning, after all, is fun—usually more fun than losing—and

since someone has to win, it may as well be you. The very fact of keeping score implies a competitive situation, and it is this

competitive clash that makes tennis so enjoyable.

If you decide that you would like to improve your game in

order to win more often then you must strive to develop at

least some of the automatic responses that make the best

players so formidable. You must learn how to produce the most effective strokes possible. Then you must hone those strokes

and your understanding of how to use them, through practice

and play, to the point that your tennis game will prove superior

to the games of your opponents.

The goal of this book is to serve as a whetstone to sharpen

the cutting edge of your game, to give you a weapon equal to

the assaults you may face on the tennis court. A principle of stroke production—topspin—is presented, with suggestions on

how to go about learning it, and how you should use your

strokes in competitive situations.

Turn on the television and listen for five minutes to almost

any well-played tennis match; you are sure to hear some reference to topspin. Topspin is as ubiquitous in topflight tennis

today as long pants and skirts were 50 years ago. Topspin allows

the pros to hit the hardest shots they possibly can and still

keep those shots in the court. Topspin guides the delicate pass-

ing shot, letting it sneak over the net and then dip down before

the opponent can close on it. Topspin is what allows adventure-

some pros to hit full-drive lobs that still manage to come down

inside the opponent’s court before bounding over the back fence. Watch film clips of the great players hitting ground-

stroke winners. Those winning shots were hit with topspin.

Unfortunately, it is possible for a beginner to take dozens of

lessons from a variety of tennis pros and never hear a word

about topspin. If the neophyte hears about topspin at all it

will probably be through television or in conversation with

other beginners, who use the term as though it describes some

impossibly difficult and secret technique better players employ

to keep beginners at bay.

In fact, the student should hear about topspin before he or

she ever hits a ball. It is virtually impossible to hit a tennis

ball without imparting some sort of spin to it, so that spin

might as well be topspin. The strokes most pros teach—‘‘Turn,

step, swing forward and up to a nice high finish”—are meant for topspin. But if they never say that the aim on the ground

strokes is to roll the ball forward as it travels down the court,

the student may never realize that this is the goal. He may

never master topspin.

Topspin on the ground strokes, and a variation of it on the

serve, is the best way to propel a tennis ball from one end of a court to the other in an effective, aggressive, and consistent

manner. This is true no matter what your level of play...

With topspin, you have access to the greatest possible area of your Opponent’s court. And you can deliver the ball to all

points of that area with power, if you wish to use it. But top-

spin shots need not necessarily be hit at a thousand miles an

hour. Gentle shots hit with topspin can also be productive as

the ball dips and dives and sneaks past your opponent. Topspin

produces an efficient shot, requiring a minimum of effort to

produce a well-hit, accurate ball, and even medium-paced swings will produce excellent results. No matter what your level of play, a drop of topspin added to the brew of your strokes will

improve the flavor of your tennis game.

Topspin is not the easiest skill to learn. If it were, many more of the 30 million Americans who play tennis would be outstanding. But players who do not use topspin are, for the

most part, hoping that their opponents will miss. Players who

have learned the art of topspin will have the firepower to make their opponents miss. And once you have topspin in your

arsenal of shots, you will discover that you not only possess an enhanced shotmaking capability, but also a new psychological

perspective on the game which players without topspin do not,

and cannot, have.

Before going on, we must arrange for some common terms

when talking about a moving tennis ball. Looking down the court, what | call the back of the ball is what we see as it approaches. The front of the ball is around the other side, and it will lead the way down the court after we hit the ball.

This viewpoint from behind the court is different from what

a player would actually see as he hits the ball, since at that

instant the player would be standing to the side of the ball,

rather than behind it. Actually, we are looking at the ball the way the racket strings would see it just before impact. These

points on the ball will remain the same regardless of how the

ball is spinning.

Now we are going to shift our viewing position to the side, so that we see what a player would see when hitting the ball, but

the labels remain the same, as though we were still viewing

the ball from the rear of the court.

If you could strike the ball absolutely flat on the back, with

the force of the racket moving on a perfect line through the exact center of the ball, you would produce a true “‘flat” shot.

It would go away from you without any spin whatsoever, in a

straight line, with only gravity pulling it down.

If you lower the point of contact from the back of the ball

to a point down toward its bottom, but still have the force of

impact moving directly through the center of the ball, you

would again produce a “‘flat” shot. But this one would sail up

into the air in a high parabola before gravity brought it back

to earth.

Topspin is a combination of parts from each of these actions. To produce topspin, you hit the back of the ball, as in the first

example, with a vertical racket face. But the racket (and the force of impact) moves at an angle, upward and forward, as in

the second example. The result is a ball that is “topped,” the

line of force now passing above the center of the ball.

A ball stroked in this manner will leave the racket in an up-

ward direction because the line of force drives it that way. But

it will also be spinnning—rolling forward—as the ball travels away from the hit, and this spin will cause the ball to dive back toward the court. This, then, is all there is to topspin. By lifting

the racket face forward and up through the ball while hitting,

and at the same time keeping that racket face fairly vertical, you produce a shot that at first rises into the air, and then

dives back toward the court. And these are excellent char-

acteristics to have on your ground strokes, since they must get over at least three feet of net before plunging back into your opponent’s court. With topspin on your shots you can drive the

ball high over the net for safety while hitting it as hard as you

can, and still bring that ball safely down into the opposite court. In effect, the user of topspin plays against an opponent

whose court slants back and up from the net, offering a target

like a wall. No other spin can produce such a target, and underspin (the opposite of topspin) in effect drops the opposing

court down away from you, offering the most difficult target

of all.

The reason the ball behaves the way it does when you hit it with topspin was first explained by Daniel Bernoulli in 1738. With 150 years of forethought, Bernoulli knew that tennis

players would want to know why spin affects a tennis ball the 14

way it does, so he devised his theorem: the pressure in a fluid

decreases as its velocity increases. Air is a fluid, and when a ten-

nis ball is hit down the court it gives a velocity to the air passing by. If the ball is also spinning as it flies forward, it drags a film of air along with it, thus creating different air speeds on differ-

ent sides of itself. The result is a difference of pressure on the

ball from the air it passes through, and that pressure causes the vall to dip in flight. If you hit with topspin, you are asking air

pressure to help you drive the ball back down toward the court, while if you hit with underspin, you are creating a pressure that will help hold the ball up.

The amount of. topspin applied to a ball depends on how the

ball is struck. For example, if the racket’s line of force strikes

the ball at a steep angle, the amount of topspin will be greater.

If the racket is swung on a flat plane through the hit, climbing only a little while the ball remains on the strings, a ‘‘flatter”’

shot (with less topspin) is produced. If the climb of the racket

is very steep, much more topspin results. The shot will climb

and dive more sharply, with less forward momentum. There is a

range of possibility in the relationship between the amount of force moving the ball down the court and the amount that

generates topspin: these forces vary according to how the racket

drives through the ball. And the player must gain control of

The opposite of topspin is underspin. Many players who pick

up a racket and take a swing at the ball for the first time often hit down at it, and underspin is the result. The racket face is

opened—tilted up toward the sky to get the ball over the net—

and it slides under the bottom of the ball, producing backspin.

(The line of force from the impact passes below the center of the ball.) With a little practice, the players who hit this way will

adjust their racket faces correctly, and the underspin shots will

begin to land in the court.

There is a danger with underspin, however. If you don’t hit

the underspin shot too hard, you can clear the net by a comfortable margin and still have this ball land in the court as well.

Putting backspin on the ball makes it want to climb against

gravity—air pressure holds it up—but if you hit gently enough

the climb dies and gravity pulls the shot back into the court. As you become more and more aggressive, however, and begin to

hit harder, you will find that you have to bring your underspin

shots lower and lower toward the net in order to keep them

from sailing long. But this will increase your chances of hitting

the net.

Even the underspin shots that do make it into play have drawbacks. The shot not only tends to sail instead of dipping

back toward the court, but also it tends to be slower than if it

were hit with topspin.

Because of these characteristics, a player at net has a fraction of a second longer to intercept an underspin passing shot. And

if the man at net does get to the shot, the ball will be higher above the net than if it had been “topped.”’

In other words, underspin shots will bother an aggressive opponent less than topspin ones. With topspin, you can dip the

ball down on your opponent’s side of the net, forcing him to volley up. You can hit sharper angles successfully as well, be-

cause the topspin shot will dip down toward the line, while

the underspin shot keeps on sailing.

Of course, no player hits every single ground stroke with topspin. There are some situations where you would choose to hit

with underspin, such as when you hit a backhand approach shot down the line to your opponent’s forehand, keeping the ball

low, or when you want to drop a ball just over the net, bring-

ing your opponent in. There are other times, as when you face such a big serve that you can do nothing other than block or

chop it back, when topspin gives way to simply getting the ball

into play. But in all situations except those where you actually need underspin (i.e., that backhand approach) topspin would

produce better results if you could manage to employ it. In other words, the more you can train yourself to hit with topspin, the better results you will have.

Memorable moments come in tennis when a player instantly analyzes the unfolding situation, intuitively perceives how best

to attack the opposition, and then produces that attack flaw-

lessly. There is no thought; there is only a reaction to the situa-

tion at hand.

Such moments happen in an instant, but they are the end product of enormous amounts of careful learning and intensive, thoughtful practice. One must learn tennis before one plays it, after all.

It is important that you understand clearly how a person learns, because only you can teach yourself tennis. A common mistake is believing that any tennis pro or instructional book

can teach tennis to you. They can show you the best way to go about learning, and the best strokes to devote that learning

process to. However, you must understand the strokes, you must put in the hours, you must guide your body and you must

strive to develop your strokes to their greatest potential.

Good tennis players move and hit effortlessly because everything they do happens automatically, without interfering conscious thought. These players have moved beyond the mechan-

ics of hitting. They can devote themselves fully to the question of where to hit the ball rather than how to hit it. For the most

part even that “‘where” is not thought out, but rather is a grooved, instinctive reaction to situations faced and solved a

thousand times before. The good players play automatically. Their conscious minds may be offering encouragement or

making plans between points, but mostly their minds are quiet while play is going on.

Can you play in this automatic way, similar to the world’s top players? Yes, without any question at all. The human body

has an amazing talent for doing complex sets of motions automatically. And, happily, our bodies are very bright at learning

new sets of motions, if you will just help it to learn in a reason-

able way. If you can learn to tie your shoes, sign your name or

drive your car, then surely you can learn to stroke a tennis ball

correctly. The only things needed are time, understanding and application.

Different persons learn tennis at different rates, and some-

times one person may require varying amounts of time to master the individual strokes. Often, the reason for such varied

learning rates is the amount of data students have in their minds about how. to move their bodies. Some people have never en-

gaged in ball sports involving hand-eye coordination, and these

people take longer to learn such a sport than people who have

developed these skills. The non-players not only have to absorb

the specifics of tennis, they must also learn a large amount of

more general knowledge about how their bodies perform.

The best student | have ever taught, in the sense of how quickly she picked up the strokes, was a young girl of 16 who

had studied ballet for over 10 years. | explained each stroke and demonstrated them in turn. Then | asked her to try. She reproduced them all, perfectly, on the very first try. This girl, who

The source of this young woman’s astounding performance

lay in her training for ballet: because of those years, Jane had a

beautiful control of all of her body, from the top of her head to the tips of her toes.

Most of us, unfortunately, have not had this kind of training. Our bodies are not fully under our command. Additionally,

they are not programmed to learn new physical motions quickly

and efficiently, as a dancer’s body is. We face not only the task of learning what the tennis strokes are, but also the problem of

getting our bodies to produce the strokes when we finally com-

prehend them.

For most people, learning a physical sport such as tennis

takes time—time to understand what the sport and its tech-

niques are all about, and time to train the body to produce the

required movements, smoothly and automatically. That time

can be cut dramatically, however, if the student understands

the learning process.

Two analogies come to mind to describe the learning process

in tennis. One is that of an empty computer filling itself with data, the other is of somebody starting to cut a board with

a Saw.

If you are new to tennis, your mind is like a computer without any data specifically relating to tennis on its tapes. You

may have a good deal of information concerning how you go

about moving your body, perhaps a fair amount on catching,

throwing and hitting things. Or, if you play tennis but have developed faulty strokes, your computer has a great deal of

material about tennis in it, but portions of that information are

incorrect, and ineffective. In either case, you must work to

gather correct data on how to produce effective tennis strokes,

as well as information, in the second case, on why the strokes

you have developed are incorrect. With this information in

hand, you can set about printing a new control tape, through

practice, where the motions of your body match your information about sound stroke production.

Now for the second analogy. When you begin to saw a piece of wood, the blade sometimes skitters over the top of the board,

moving aimlessly from side to side. But if you make a determined effort to hold the saw on the proper spot, you get the

cut started. After that, the blade stays in its own groove.

“Grooving” a tennis stroke follows a similar sequence. At first, the instructions to your brain go skittering about over its

surface, producing almost random body motions. This con-

tinues until you make an effort to narrow things down, to con-

trol your stroke, and imprint on your brain the correct motion you want your body to perform. As the correct motion is re-

peated, the groove in your mind deepens, and as that happens,

it becomes easier and easier to repeat the motion smoothly and accurately. After sufficient repetitive practice, the motion be-

comes yours, something that you can produce automatically.

Unfortunately, an old, ineffective habit can have just as deep a groove as a correct, effective one. No matter how much

you may wish to change this stroke, as soon as you have a ball

coming at you in play you will go on “‘automatic”’ and define your swing by that old groove. Until you do the work to create

a new groove, the old groove is the only one you have. To re-

place an old stroke with a new one it is necessary to create a

new groove at least as deep as the old one, so that when you go

on automatic in the face of an oncoming shot, you will produce

the correct set of motions rather than the wrong set. Many

players can quickly duplicate proper strokes under ideal practice conditions, but far fewer apply themselves to the repetitive

practice routines that are needed to make a stroke automatic

under all conditions, including play.

Luckily, you can form a new groove with less time and effort

than it took to create the old one. As soon as you hit a single

ball in an easier, more effective way, you will feel it instantly,

and positive feedback will speed the learning process greatly. Correct tennis strokes feel good, almost effortless, and if you

can hit just one, you are well along the road toward more cor-

rect play. If you can hit one shot correctly, you can hit a million. You can then deepen that groove through repetition. When you hit that first ball correctly you will have located the

spot for the new groove in your mind.

Learning a new stroke also requires relaxation. Be easy. Don’t panic. No one is asking you to throw out your old strokes (bad

as they may be) and reduce yourself to naked impotence in the

face of your tennis enemies. Undoubtedly, while you are

acquiring your new weaponry, it will be those old strokes

that continue to do battle for you. Think instead in terms of

adding new strokes to your repertoire. You must relax about

the learning process—welcoming new ideas about how to pro-

duce tennis strokes and enjoying what your body does with

those ideas—because you can’t learn if you are filled with stress.

And | will announce at this point one hateful but unavoid-

able truth: You are going to miss a lot of shots before you are

done. You are going to miss every time you try something new.

You are going to flub shots you hit perfectly against the back-

board or ball machine when you try to show them off to your

spouse, tennis friends, or worst of all, your tennis enemies. You

are going to have embarrassing moments, “‘off’’ days, and times

when you are going to wish you’d never tried the game. In a word—you are going to lose. All of these bad things are going to

happen to you, without fail. They have happened to everybody,

even to Laver, Connors, Borg and Evert. All of the world’s great players missed before they began making the shots for

which they are famous. But they all persevered through the low

points of their tennis education. So can you.

In short, you are allowed to miss while learning. In fact, you

are expected to miss. Very quickly, though, if your persevere, you will emerge on the far side of the learning process with

strokes that are effective, and very much under your control.

To be ready you must position yourself in the middle of your opponent’s possible angles. Have somebody stand at the other

To hit a tennis ball well, you must hit it in the center of your racket, in its “Sweet spot.’’ When the ball collides with the racket at this point, the strings stretch and rebound uniformly, resulting in the greatest control possible. If you hit off-center

on any stroke, the racket will wobble and turn in your hand. The rebound on such a hit will not be uniform, and the result-

ing shot may be far worse than your stroke warrants.

When you do hit off-center, you will feel a “‘clunk”’ between

racket and ball. This should signal you that it is time to relax your grip and arm, watch the ball carefully to the point of contact, and strive to hit the ball with the exact center of your

racket face. You don’t want to tighten your grip, as that will

deaden the racket and your feel for the ball, and will do little to

help you hit with the center of the strings.

On the ground strokes (and the volley), the angle of your racket face controls how high or low the ball will go after you hit it. An “open’’ racket face (strings are slanted upward) is

going to cause the ball to go higher into the air than a “‘closed”’

racket face (strings facing down). In general, you will make

most of your strokes with your racket face slightly open from

the vertical, as your shots must climb up and over the net before dropping down again. You control this open or closed

element with your wrist, by rotating it slightly one way or another. You do not control your racket face by changing your

grip! To control the height of your shot over the net, turn your

wrist the small amount needed, before you hit the ball. Once

set, however, your wrist should not change through the stroke.

All of this may sound quite complicated, but it really isn’t once you get the idea by trying a few strokes with different face “‘settings.’’ Go ahead. Open your racket face way up and

hit a ball too high. Then close it and hit the ball into the bottom of the net (using the correct stroke to do each). Experi-

ment with opening and closing your racket face, so you can

learn what sort of adjustments are needed for which particular

shots. Just make sure you hit with topspin at the same time.

No single setting can be used for all strokes. You will need all

sorts of settings, and the different shots that result, while play-

ing. If your opponent is in the backcourt, you will drive your topspin ground strokes with a fairly open racket face, so that

the ball will clear the net by perhaps four feet, carrying deep

into your opponent’s court. (Depth is controlled more by racket face opening and the shape of the swing than by how hard you hit the ball.) If your opponent comes into net, you

will close the racket face slightly from its previous setting, thus

keeping the ball low over the net and forcing your opponent to volley up. The swing will stay the same, but the face setting will

change. If you should desire to lob, you will open the face way

up, and stroke up through the ball at a steeper angle.

There is a range of possibility here. You can close the racket face and just clear the net, or open it way up to hit the shot

much higher and deeper. You must strive to gain control over the complete range, and make your control automatic through

practice and play.

In the above, we talk about changing the flight of the ball by

changing the angle of the racket face, while keeping the shape of

the swing the same. It is important to realize that you can also

change the flight of the ball by varying the amount of topspin you put on it. In this case, you keep the angle of the racket the

same, but change the direction that it takes through the ball. On

one side of the spectrum of possibility, you may lift a slightly

open racket face so abruptly up the back of the ball that you

put a tremendous amount of topspin on it, but it will hardly go forward over the net because you haven’t driven through it. The

other end of this range is to hit through the ball with a nearly flat swing, causing the ball to bullet away with very little top-

spin on it at all. You haven’t changed the racket face, but you

have radically changed the flight of the ball by driving that face through the ball in two different directions. (In the first exam-

ple, the line of force imparted by the racket barely clips the ball,

passing well above its center, while in the second, the force goes almost through the ball’s center.)

Proper topspin lies between these two extremes, with your

racket brushing up the back of the ball to put topspin on your shot, but also hitting through the ball enough to drive your shot forward over the net. Just as there is no one setting for the

racket face on your strokes, so there is no proper amount of topspin: you will use topspin to do many different things with

the ball, and you must experiment to discover how much top-

spin is needed to produce different results.

Still another aspect of the stroke which must be under your control is how fast you swing the racket through the stroke. Invariably, in the interests of hitting harder, starting students

swing the racket faster and faster, and then wonder why their

shots have almost no control—and often, no speed—as a result. To exert control on the ball, the racket must remain in contact

with the ball long enough for compression between the ball and

the strings to take place, and then for both to rebound smoothly.

If you swing too fast, this compression doesn’t happen properly,

and far worse shots—light, flighty ones—take place than if you

had swung at a slower speed. It is also harder to judge the amount of topspin you are putting on the ball when swinging

really fast. | To hit harder, you must concentrate on taking a long step forward, in order to get your weight solidly into the shot. You

must hit the ball squarely in the middle of the strings, and you must employ a long, flowing swing rather than any sort of jerky, violent one in order to make proper contact with the ball. Once

you are really secure in all this, you can begin trying faster

swings for still more pace, but now you will know when the

manageable speed. If you wait until the ball is almost to you before you start your swing, you will have to swing very fast to

make contact at all. If you feel you are doing this, concentrate

On starting your swing sooner, but go through it more slowly so

that you still meet the ball correctly. You will “carry”’ the ball

longer this way, and produce better shots.

takes after the hit. To hit the ball accurately, you must also

control the left and right direction.

The horizontal direction of your shots will be properly determined by where you take the ball as it passes by you. If you

make contact with the ball early—out ahead of you, beyond the

normal distance at which you want to hit it—you will tend to hit the ball crosscourt. As you reach forward, your racket face will stop “looking” down the court and begin to face off to the

side more and more. If you take the ball further back than this,

right at the point where the strings face directly down the court,

you will hit your shot straight down the court. And if you take

the ball still further back, you will tend to hit it wide.

Actually, to hit in different directions you want to swing at

the ball with exactly the same swing all the time. You want to

keep your wrist and arm constant throughout the swing. Don’t

slap your wrist or poke out your elbow to change the direction of your shots. What does change is your stance in relation to

the ball coming toward you, and where you hit that ball on its

line of flight past you. Although under playing conditions you

may find yourself forced to change your swing to change the direction of your shots, you would hit far more secure, solid

shots if you changed your stance instead.

Select a line on the court and pretend that a ball is traveling directly down that line toward you. Take up your stance for

stroking, already turned toward whichever side you wish, and

put out your racket so that its strings face directly down the

line. If you made contact with a ball exactly here, it would

travel right back the way it came.

Now, to hit a crosscourt shot, don’t move your arm or racket

at all, but instead move your back foot an inch or two closer to

the line, your front foot an inch or two further away from it,

turning your stance in the new direction you want the ball to

travel. Keep your racket face positioned directly over the line

along which our imaginary ball is coming, but note how it now

intersects that line at an angle, at a point a few inches ahead of

where it was placed to hit directly down the line. If you swing exactly as you normally would, but intercept the ball at this

new point, you will hit the ball crosscourt.

To hit wide, toward the side opposite the crosscourt, you

must move your front foot closer to the line, your back foot

further away from it, again turning your body in the new direc-

tion. Now the racket will cross the line a bit further back than

it did when you hit down the line, and the ball will be driven

wide.

In practice, think of giving yourself more room from the ball

to hit down the line or wide from the center of the court than you need on crosscourts. (Most novices hit crosscourt because they tend to get too close to the ball’s line of flight. If they

were to try to hit straight down the court from this too-close stance, they would hit the ball on the throat of the racket, or

even on their hands.) The important idea to grasp is that there

is no need for any sort of poking or jerking with the racket in order to drive the ball in different directions. You want to use

your normal stroke, and simply change your stance to allow that same stroke to drive your shots where you want them.

The most common mistakes that students make while learn-

The volley is the karate chop of tennis; the short, explosive

punch that finishes a well-played point. It is the shortest and

most compact of all tennis strokes, yet one of the most powerful strokes when properly timed and executed. It is employed

best when you are close to the net, taking advantage of that

commanding position. And the stroke is hit with backspin, the opposite of our beloved topspin.

There are three reasons for this. The first is that you can produce backspin (for control), with a short, quick, downward

punching motion with the racket, making contact with the ball

well out in front of you. Second, the underspin shot will shoot

off your strings and stay low in your opponent’s court, tending

to skid and forcing him to hit up to you if he gets to the ball at

all. (Topspin, which is hard to hit with control against a fly ball, tends to cause the ball to bounce higher, and you don’t want

that when you are in at net. Your opponent has a better chance of passing you when he hits off a high bounce.) Finally, if you

punch the racket face forward and down the back or slightly under the ball, you can hit forceful shots back over the net from below your side of the net, allowing you to cope with the best

dipping shots your opponent can produce.

Moving in on the bounce of the ball brings us to the subject of the half volley. This stroke is badly misnamed, as it is not

a volley at all, but rather a very abbreviated ground stroke. The

half volley is used to hit the ball just after it bounces, when the

idea of taking the ball on the rise is carried to an extreme. It is normally used on your way to net, when your opponent hits a ball that lands too far in front of you to volley, yet is too close for you to swing normally.

To produce a half volley, you must get your weight down as

close to the ground as possible, keep your eyes glued to the ball

and swing with a flat or only slightly rising stroke, two or three feet in length. You should hit the ball just after it bounces,

using a firm wrist and your short swing to hit it back deep into your opponent’s court. The shot demands care, since you are

below the net and fairly close to it. You cannot hit too hard, as you must not only get the ball up to clear the net, but also back down into the opposite court. So your goal is to get a difficult shot back over the net and deep into your opponent’s court.

To learn the half volley, try standing at midcourt, turning,

ball is not moving briskly toward you, but it can give you some

idea of the sort of brief ground stroke that will enable you to

get such a ball back into play. Then have your practice partner either throw or hit balls so that they bounce almost beside you,

and try to duplicate your effort.

Proper tennis practice, however, goes beyond hitting tennis

balls with a racket. Good tennis requires a body able to meet the demands of play. If you are to play well, you will want to devote some time to getting your body into condition.

Most good players run two or three miles several times a

week, to build endurance. They will also drill for speed on the

court. One good exercise consists of running a race against your

practice partner. Each of you starts opposite one another,

Standing on the baselines of the court, halfway between the

center mark and the sidelines. Holding your rackets, you each

run from your starting point in toward the center strap, touch

the net with your racket, then backpedal along the other side of a giant ‘‘V”’ back to the baseline. Repeat the entire ‘“V”’ 10

Once or twice a day, working as hard as you can for speed, you

“little more’ beyond exhaustion that makes you stronger.

Sit-ups are important, to give your torso the strength to twist and reach and bend throughout a long match. Kangaroo

reach at the net. Jumping rope improves your agility and ability

to take the quick little steps that are needed for proper posi-

Doubles is combat conducted on a team basis. The great joy of doubles arises from the successful combination of two players, through astute generalship, into a winning team. It often takes years for a doubles team to reach its full

potential, as each player learns what to expect from the other,

and also learns how to play in such a way as to mesh his abilities

with those of his partner.

Let’s begin by talking about court position. In the forehand court, the proper place from which to serve is only slightly further out from the center of the court than you would serve

in singles, a matter of three or four additional feet. Serving from this position rather than from far out by the sideline, gives you

a better shot at your opponent’s backhand corner. You also

collapse the angles of return open to your opponent, allowing your partner at net to stand safely out toward the middle of his

half of the court. From his position your partner can pick off your opponent’s attempts at returning the ball back to you. If

you serve properly to the backhand corner of the forehand

court, your partner at net can safely stand as far out as midway

between the center line and the singles sideline and still cover the alley. (He can cover the alley by just stepping over with his

right foot and sticking out his racket.) At the same time, by standing out so prominently, your partner at net gives the opponents something to think about, especially since he can also cover most of the middle of the court by just stepping

across the other way.

Your netman thus becomes a real threat. Too many netmen

stand carefully with one foot on the singles sideline, and for all

the good they do there, they might as well go sit down and

watch. If you serve from wide on the forehand, however, you

will tend to serve to your opponent’s forehand, and your part-

ner will be pinned to the alley. So don’t do it.

As for serving to the backhand court, the rule about standing

in close to your center is a little less strict. You probably will

have to stand out two or three additional feet—so that you are

midway between the center mark and the singles sideline—in

order to get your serve past your partner at net and still wide to

your opponent’s backhand. You don’t want to stand too far

wide here either, as again you offer your opponent easier angles

of return by your netman.

By serving from closer in toward the center mark than many

people normally do, you collapse the angles that are open to your opponents, and their returns must now come closer to

your partner at net. The whole point of doubles is to hit your

shots in such a way that your opponents’ replies will end up going to you or your partner at net.

After your serve, though, you must remember to run forward

on a line more toward the net person than toward the receiver,

in order to cut off the open area of your court. In other words, you serve and then move in toward the middle of the receiver’s

possible angles into your half of the court, so that you can easily reach whatever he returns.

For good doubles, you must get your first serve in, and you

must serve it to your opponents’ backhands. At times, of course,

you may vary the serve wide to a forehand, in order to keep the opposition honest about where they stand to receive, but your

standard serve for doubles must be to the backhand.

In doubles, a fairly hard first serve placed carefully in the backhand corner is a far better delivery than a slugger’s blast, which may not be as accurate. A serve of slower pace allows you to get further in toward the net if you come in behind your serve, the careful placement to the backhand allows you to con-

trol the point, and because of that placement you will know

what sort of a return to expect. If your serve should accidently slip off to the forehand, you will suddenly be facing quite different angles of return, and may find yourself quickly in trouble.

about how to play winning doubles. Serve to the backhand consistently, on the other hand, and get your first serve in all the

time, and you will be known as a doubles hero. You will win.

The serves don’t even have to be particularly hard, they just

have to be directed to the backhand.

the advantage is truly on your side. As soon as you miss your first, and have to serve a second serve, your opponents will

rather than simply smack balls past or through him. For this reason, you must strive to master every aspect of the game in order to jerk and yank and tug your opponent from one point

to another, from one mistake to the next.

Your attack in tennis can take many forms. Just as efficient play in tennis is almost a model of Zen paradox—you’ve got to try like the devil, but remain relaxed at all times—so too must

the nature of your attack remain relaxed and flexible. Physical-

ly, you cannot play efficiently by tightening every muscle in your body, or by hitting every ball at full power. And mentally, too, you must remain relaxed and flexible, able to vary your

game plan rather than seize on one system no matter what

happens. Of course, if the system you seize on wins for you,

and continues to win, stick with it. But if you begin to lose, you

must give up one system of attack for another, willing to do whatever is necessary—within the confines of proper tennis—to

grab victory from your opponent.

You must play enough to gain a sense of what shot Is appro-

priate for what situation. If you can win a point with a ground stroke hit at a certain pace, use that pace, and don’t try to hit it

harder than necessary. On the overhead, hit it just as hard as you need to win the point, not harder in order to impress the gallery. That also increases your risk of missing. Keep your

hardest shots in reserve, for when you really need them, and in

that way surprise your opponent with them. Any winner is by definition the correct shot for that situation, but often the deli-

cate soft shot is a more infuriating—and safer—shot than the

blasted one.

(To illustrate this idea of using the appropriate shot, | can’t

help but think of an important match | saw in the 1965 US Intercollegiates. One player, with match point against him, ran for a ball, hit a short lob, then slipped, fell, and skidded under a

bench at the side of the court. His opponent, standing very

close to the net, took a full windup, hit the ball as hard as he

could at the open court, missed his shot by an inch, and went on to lose the entire match.)

In a tennis point, talking of the ‘‘appropriate shot”’ is just an-

other way of saying that you must develop “rhythm.” You

must gain a sense of the right shot for every situation, and use it, rejecting any other sort of shot, or pace that doesn’t fit the situation. Often, people will stroke two or three balls up and

down the middle of the court during a point, then suddenly

bash the ball away, attempting a winner. They miss, of course,

because they haven’t followed the proper rhythm that every

tennis point demands. Winning a tennis point is an act similar to that of landing a fish. Only here the line that brings in your

quary is one made of time, and your prize is the point. If you

play well, utilize all the area of the court and do something with

the ball each time you hit it, you remove time from your oppo-

nent little by little. Finally, he is unable to get properly set to hit the next shot accurately—and you put away the result—or else he is not able to reach your shot at all.

In tennis, everything during the course of a point will tend to either add up for you, or else add up against you. That is, if you

serve deep rather than short to your opponent’s backhand, you

give yourself a bit more time to move toward the net, while

asking your opponent to deal with a more difficult shot on his

(probably) weaker side. Having moved a bit further in toward

the net, you will be able to volley his weaker reply a little bit

higher in the air, a little bit closer to the net. This allows you a better, more angled, shot than if you were hitting from further

back and lower down. And such a shot will land further away from your opponent, travel by him at a faster pace and allow

him less time to get ready. He will have to run harder over a

longer distance to reach your shot, and probably will not hit

quite as good a shot as he would have off a nearer one. This

gives you a still easier ball to deal with, and by then you'can hit

a shot that takes away his time entirely: he won’t be able to get it, and you have reeled in your fish.

Pressure in tennis is what you apply to your opponent when, point after point, you force him to deal with a difficult shot

every time you hit the ball. To produce pressure, you cannot

play hard and well on some of the points. You must play hard

and well on all of them. Do this enough, play all the points accurately and hard enough, and you will discover that almost

every Opponent you face will eventually crack. Pressure can

build for your opponent not only through the severity of your attack, but also through your manner: if you just attend to

business, always, carry yourself as a winner would, and never get angry or in some other way distract yourself from the work

at hand, your opponent will never see any hopeful signs that you may be on the verge of cracking. And he will be that much

closer to doing so himself.

Pressure is a cumulative thing. It will add up through a match, wearing an opponent down, often turning a 6-0 first set

winner into a third set loser. Above all else, you must learn

never to give up On a match, no matter how poorly you are playing. Time after time, especially among the juniors, you will

see players decide that they are not playing up to their personal expectations, and then obviously “tank” a match, when in fact

they could win if they just gave their best, and applied as much

pressure as possible despite playing at less than their best.

Accepting defeat before your opponent has forced it on you

is stupid. It is insulting to your opponent, who should have to

earn his win. But far more importantly, it is insulting to the

tennis game you have labored to create. You will have off days,

but that doesn’t mean that they have to be losing days as well.

Don’t waste a match just because you think you are playing poorly. Stick in there, give your game a chance, put as much

pressure On your opponent as you possibly can, and make the

effort to see if your less-than-perfect play might not still be

good enough to win. Often, you will win through such effort,

but you will never discover this unless you play as hard as you can, no matter how your game is performing, right up to the final point.

If your Opponent should win under such conditions, when you have tried as hard as you can, then fine. Congratulate him.

You know that you gave it your best, you have the satisfaction of knowing that you didn’t give up under trying conditions,

your game is stronger for the workout against adversity, and you will play better next time. For goodness sake, don’t play

badly and give your opponent the match on a platter besides.

Make him earn the match, make him prove he can beat your

game even on an off day. It may turn out that your opponent

can’t beat you, and that he will crack under the pressure you

produce simply by not giving up. For that matter, by trying as

hard as you can, your game will many times come back into focus, and begin functioning properly again, making the win

that much easier. But this will never happen if you give up before play is ended. Who is to lose and who is to win in a tennis

match is never decided for certain until the last point has been

played and finished.

Attitude can decide if any particular player is to become a winner or loser at tennis. The moment negative thoughts creep

into your mind, even on particular points—‘‘God, | hope | don’t double fault,” “‘l hope | don’t miss this return”—you have

moved a good distance toward the loss you want to avoid. It is

as though your playing mind, only half listening to your conscious mind, picks up the words ‘‘double fault” or ‘“‘miss this re-

turn,” takes them for command, and produces the horrid event.

On the other hand, positive thoughts such as, “This first serve is going in” or “I’m going to belt this return down the line no

matter where he puts the ball,” can work miracles for your

tennis game.

The most aggravating form of negative thinking for a tennis pro, or for anyone watching someone play whom they want to

do well, is negative body posture, an ailment distressingly com-

mon among juniors. The slumped shoulders, the hanging head,

the racket head smacked down into the court as a player walks back toward the baseline after losing a point, all of these do your opponent almost as much good as eight consecutive service

aces. You are on the court to play, not to perform an exercise of self-sabotage. Fake the upright carriage, the quick walk back

to get ready to win the next point, if you must. You may dis-

cover yourself much more positive about the point to come. As

an added benefit, you have increased the pressure on your

opponent, because he will begin to worry that you don’t get up-

set when he wins a point. Your goal whenever you go onto the tennis court is to play the best tennis you can, not to produce

an impromptu performance out of a Greek tragedy.

Tiny variations in skill separate most winners from most losers, and separate touring pros from those players just below

them. The actual differences may be small, but they can repre-

sent years of difference in training and dedication, and very

decisive differences in scores between better and lesser players. There are many levels of tennis, and each aspiring player must attend to his homework faithfully in each of those levels if he

hopes to graduate on to the next. And no player ever graduates

without confidence and a positive attitude.

It is very important, however, that you don’t try to vault up several levels of play when you find yourself confronted with an

opponent considered better than you. Many players try for such

brilliant winners in these cases that they miss shot after shot,

and never find out just how good their opponent actually is.

You must try to get the most out of your game, of course, but

remember that you aren’t going to serve like a Tanner or return like a Connors until you have put in the years of effort they

have. Until that time, simply serve and return and play the way you can, and ask your opponent to prove that he can deal with that level of play—the best you can actually produce—rather

than any far higher level of play that you are not capable of sustaining. |

An important concept to understand—no matter what your

level of play—is that of ‘“‘“momentum” in a tennis match. Time

and time again, in everyone’s career, there will be matches where

you win the first set easily, only to drop the second as though you had never held a racket before. The culprit here is not your

play, but your inability to hold onto momentum.

When you have dominated a set against a player of compar-

able ability, and won it easily, you must realize that you stand

at a point of potential weakness. You have reached the goal that

you've been striving for, and it is all too easy for your mind to say, with a sigh of relief, ‘Well, I’ve got it at last, that’s done.”

With these words it is you who may be done! Your opponent

has gained nothing—he is well along the way to being shoved

out of the match entirely—while you have gained everything, as

far as the set is concerned. So your opponent is going to begin

to fight with everything he has to stay alive in the next set, at

the very moment you begin to let up. The result is often one of

those strange 6-0, 0-6 reversals.

If you are aware of how all this can happen, however, you will harden yourself to fight for those first few games at the start of any second set after you’ve won the first, as though it is you

who were down. By doing this, you present as firm a barrier to your opponent’s progress as ever.

If your opponent should win the second set, it is now his

turn to be at a point of potential trouble: He has now reached a

goal, the second set, and it is now A/s mind which may sabotage

him with thoughts of success. It would be easier to avoid these difficulties by insisting that you play your best, firmest, most consistent and unforgiving tennis during those first two or three opening games of the second set, after you’ve won the first set,

to make sure that you take the second as well. But if that

second set should slip away, do wake up, realize that the mo-

mentum will shift back to you if you just force it to, and make

your counterattack at the start of the third. Often the momentum will swing back, and you will redeem your lapse in the second set by still winning the third, decisive set.

Another way of saying all this is: never let a beaten opponent

back into a match through the mistake of slackening your attack. Why risk losing? And realize that you can slacken your

attack before a match is even begun, by underestimating your opponent, or by foolishly listening to nice people who tell you “Oh, you won't have any problem with that player, you’re a cinch to win.” | Whatever you do, always keep in mind that any opponent

can always get back into a match until the last point is played.

You must also learn that on important points you absolutely

must not hope that your opponent will lose them, you must insist on winning them yourself. If you do not attack purposely

on important points—serve into the body and come to net, for example—your opponent may not lose them to you, and you

may never win.

You must train yourself to have as your goal the winning of

all the sets of a match, not just the winning of “‘a set,’’ or even

worse, just the desire to ‘‘make a good showing.” Unless you

really decide that you want to win, unless you insist that you

will win, you probably won’t. On the other hand, if you decide

—and get your body and mind and tennis game to really understand that you insist—on winning all the points, all the games, and all the sets you play, then you will stand a very good

chance of winning all the matches you are involved in.

If you have read this far—through the mysteries of topspin,

the intricacies of the serve and volley, and the various sugges-

tions | have made for improving your game—you are obviously

interested in pursuing tennis excellence. Something about the

game has captured your attention, and held it beyond the initial

stages of purchasing a racket and some balls and seeing what the sport is all about. You have discovered that it isn’t quite as easy

as it looks, yet something keeps you involved.

Even though you play tennis against an opponent across the

net, you are also involved in a very intense confrontation with yourself. First, you must train yourself to play, and then you

must gain control of your physical and emotional self in order

to play the best tennis you can.

Pogo said in his famous line, ‘‘We have met the enemy, and

he is us.’’ Nowhere is this more true than in tennis. But what joy if you can meet this enemy and overcome him. How splen-

did it is to discover that you can concentrate for a whole match,

or that you can exert yourself successfully; that you can keep

from getting angry, or tight, or scared on crucial points, and

instead actually respond effectively and aggressively at these

moments. If you do gain a degree of control over your enemy

self—and you surely will, with any involvement in the sport—

you will reap many of the same returns of self-awareness and self-expansion that are offered as reward to pursuers of more exotic disciplines, such as Yoga or meditation.

But beyond these rewards lie the still greater ones held in that

single word so often frowned upon while violently pursued in our society: winning! It is enormous fun to compete with some-

one, and through the exertion of your abilities and self-taught talents, beat them. There is no question within a tennis match

what each player is trying to do: each is trying to win. And

each player accepts the aggressiveness of the other, because he

is trying to return that same aggressiveness.

Tennis is so rewarding, however, that you may even lose a

match and still feel that you have won. If you play up to your limits, and perhaps forge beyond them for a short time, there is

no shame in losing to a better player. In short, tennis is reward-

ing for anyone who plays it (no matter what their level of play),

as long as they give their best, and strive to explore their potential in the game.
