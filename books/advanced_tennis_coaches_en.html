The book Advanced Tennis for Coaches, Teachers and Players by Gundars Tilmanis, helps to fill a void that has long existed in tennis textbooks. The book is particularly

relevant now because of the tremendous growth of tennis as a recreational sport, not just for children, but also for adults of all ages.

Previously, most all textbooks were directed to the young. The principles of learning propounded in these textbooks were all saturated with instruction by rote. This method

of instruction is in no way acceptable to the new adult player. He needs to understand what he is about to learn. This means that a very intelligent approach must be used to make a tennis convert of this newcomer to the game.

Gundars Tilmanis’ book answers this need extremely well by giving the coach and player a real understanding of the mechanics involved in playing tennis. The credibility

of the book is enhanced by the excellent illustrations and photographs demonstrating the techniques of the world’s greatest players, and also those of the neophytes.

The portion of the book dealing with the introductory ball skills for the young is good, but of particular interest is the presentation of a successful method of group teaching. The author shows susbtantial inventiveness in presenting a large number of routines to facilitate group teaching. These routines follow a logical pattern in an

ever-increasing degree of difficulty. Any coach who would take his pupils through the regimen of stroke, practice, and play is assured of a much-improved tennis team.

The tactics on singles and doubles are practical and the common temptation to make it an exercise in geometry has been avoided. The author’s clear understanding of the

game and his ability to relate it make his book particularly valuable for players of every level of tennis.

For the coach in charge of developing a tennis team, the author provides not only a practical routine, but also a method of self-improvement. The coach in charge of

introducing tennis is shown a sensible, systematic way of taking the pupil through the different stages of tennis development. For the coach in charge of recreational tennis, the group teaching methods presented are invaluable in keeping all the students

productively occupied at all times.

For the coach of outstanding players, the book has many very useful hints interspersed with the general instruction. Tactical hints presented for team situations will prove

equally valuable on a one-to-one teaching basis. The attentive coach will find something

of value, regardless of which skill level he is teaching. I heartily recommend this book.

DENNIS VAN DER MEER President Van der Meer Tennis University

1. The Biomechanical Principles Involved in Tennis

My first contact with hitting occurred when I was four years old, at the family dinnner table. My mother sat

on my left, my father on my right. At this stage of my

career I was notorious for slurping soup, licking knives and so on (habits which incidently, you pick up again

once you leave home). I used to receive hits behind the

ears for these slurps.

My father used to hit me behind the right ear, my mother behind the left. As the months rolled by, I noticed in the mirror one morning that my left ear stood out a little further than my right. This surprised me,

since my father was the bigger of the two.

Next mealtime, I slurped on purpose and then observed closely. Sure enough, my, father was forced to

hit me left-handed and had a very cramped hitting style

due to some cupboards just behind him.

My mother had a longer backswing, her arm was straight at impact point and I noticed a slight wrist flexion and a long follow through—and a big smile on

her face.

IDEAS TO PONDER OVER WHEN USING

1. Far too often tennis coaches simply model players along ‘classical’ form, without really taxing the players’ thought processes. They receive a formula but not a

reason. It is important to let players know why they are made to perform in a certain way. For too long coaches have advocated rigid coaching points: ‘Put the

left foot eighteen inches in front of the right foot; right toe points towards the tree; turn this; turn that; then run over there’. Players learn the skills of tennis in a

rote fashion, with very little understanding. If a situation occurs that is different from the one drilled reli-

giously in practices, it is as though the racket has no

strings. Players are lost. Players should be encouraged to think about the game. The learning environment is certainly stagnant if the only question a player asks

‘after a coaching session is the whereabouts of his racket

cover.

It is essential to each an understanding of basic

' physical principles, since this enables the player to vis-

ualise what the basic tennis skills involve. It assists his

ability to self-currect, and gradually improves his own

skill level. With large classes, a given player cannot

hope to reach a high level of skill in the allotted practice time. An understanding of the basic physical principles of the skill is important for his own practice

sessions later. If a player has the knowledge and understanding of a particular stroke, his chances of mastering

it have increased. For example, too high a backswing on the backhand, especially on a grass court, will find the player continually chopping down on the ball,

particularly if forced to hit the ball late. Teach a lower arc on the backswing and tell the player why. 2. A successful flat service involves a large number of interwoven physical principles. Some of these are more important for a successful serve than others. Help the players acquire a basic understanding of the most important principles involved. When emphasising the most

important aspects of a stroke the coach should attempt

to make players feel the difference between a desired performance and their own. The player can then tell by the resulting feedback on a shot, whether it was the

desired result, an improvement, or nowhere near the

desired performance! 3. Explicitness of instruction is important. A coach’s teaching points must have meaning. I have heard some coaches come out with advice such as ‘Bad luck!’

‘Watch your footwork’; these comments mean less than nothing to a young tennis player who, minutes earlier, was told to ‘Watch the ball’.

Perhaps more appropriately, contribute such information as ‘Let your left hand help place the racket into

a hitting position on the backhand. This will ensure that you curl into a sideways position and allows for maximum rotation of your body segments.’ This,

supplemented with a suitable demonstration, would be

quite valuable.

Suitable cue words, such as ‘curl’ would then help

to generate a feeling for the movement. 4. If a player is hitting the ball quite forcefully on his first service, is it worth changing his technique to compensate for a slight weakness? For example, the ball

push into the hitting zone for the first serve takes place

at shoulder level; should the coach adjust the ball push

so that the ball leaves the fingers slightly above head height in order to help attain a little more control when

projecting the ball?

In other words, is it our aim, as coaches, to turn out

robots with all players identical in stroke production?

Because of individual differences in natural body

movement patterns, strength, body proportions and so

on, we must allow for variations in style. We can point

out basic mechanical principles and emphasise that

these must be upheld to enable players to have a fair

chance of success. There is no single stereotyped techni-

que. Each player will use the sum of his personal char-

acteristics to fit into the basic principles of movement

—there is a certain range of correctness. Coaches must,

however, work hard to eliminate gross weaknesses or un-

necessary movements, as they simply add to the work

load. For example, some players go through ritual knee

bends prior to serving. Although there are a few radical

performers, the majority of top tennis players adhere

to correct basic mechanical principles.

6. Top tennis players, especially during the warm-up

period prior to a match, often disregard correct physical principles by taking short cuts when stroking the ball.

These players have over-learned the skills of tennis and

have consequently eliminated many of the fringe neces-

sities when performing tennis skills. For example, when

volleying at the net, both feet are firmly planted on the

ground with only the racket flashing through air to block

back volleys.

This can often be a trap for aspiring young tennis

players, who copy the champion’s technique, not aware

that many of the essential elements of stroke production

have been left out, such as a step towards the net on

volleying to help facilitate the transfer of body-weight.

While working at the Modesto Swim and Racquet Club, I drove a group of juniors to Oakland, California,

to watch the professionals in action. For weeks I had religiously drilled strokes with these youngsters. After a few games, the juniors began stating ‘Til, he doesn’t do

that’, and other remarks in a similar vein. Quite a pre-

7. The old adage that ‘practice makes perfect’ is only

true in part. It is true that repetitive practice makes for

permanency in skill habit; the danger is that the learner

may be practising and habituating (and perfecting) an

incorrect skill. A better statement, a more meaningful

description of the effect of practice, is to say that ‘cor-

rect practice makes perfect’.

PROBLEM-SOLVING METHOD OF TEACHING

The problem-solving approach is a useful means of

instilling an understanding of basic tennis concepts. The

method is based on the following proverb:

To hear is to forget, To see is to remember, To do is to understand. In this method the coach imposes a number of problem

situations which the players are forced to solve.

Here are three possible problems. Once a beginnner

can hit the ball over the net, ask him to tell you:

1. What body adjustments have to be made to hit deep drives which land within ninety cm (three feet) of the base line? In other words, how can he increase the force of his

shots? Gradually, after an experimental practice period,

the players will come up with some of the following possibilities :

(a) a sideways body stance with weight transfer

(b) opening the racket face during the forward

swing and ensuring a tight wrist on contact;

(c) projecting the ball at forty-five degrees to

attain maximum distance. If they fail to recognise the basic principles of developing force, the coach could help by asking leading ques-

tions until finally all players have four or five ideas on

how to attain more depth on their drives. What is more,

they thought up these ideas themselves!

2. What body adjustments have to be made to hit a forehand across court? After an appropriate experimental period, coupled with

questions from the coach, the following are possibilities:

(a) a more open stance than for a down the line drive;

(b) ball contact point a little earlier than usual; (c) swing the racket head through the ball and in the direction of the intended hit.

ONCOMING BALL Spin Spin is applied to a tennis ball when the racket force

is applied off-centre; if the hitting force acts through

the centre of the ball, the ball will travel through the

air without spin.

How then can a player impart spin to a ball?

(a) He may stroke through the ball with an up-

ward or downward path and a flat racket face. An

upward racket path would induce topspin, which is

characterised by the ball moving in an anti-clock-

wise direction, when viewed from the left side of

the ball (see Fig. 4a). A downward racket path

would induce backspin, which is characterised by

the ball moving in a clockwise direction when

viewed from the left side of the ball (see Fig. 4b).

(b) He may stroke through a horizintal plane with

an open or closed racket face. In this situation an

open racket face will induce backspin, (see Fig.

4c) and a closed racket face topspin (see Fig. 4d). What effect does spin have on the path of a tennis ball through the air? A tennis ball which is spinning

while travelling through the air is subjected to the effects

of two air currents. (see Fig. 5).

(a) The ball tends to drag a little aid along with

it. This boundary air layer around the ball moves in

the same direction as the ball is spinning.

(b) The force provided by air resistance acts to

oppose the forward movement of the ball.

Considering these two forces: On the side of the ball

where the air resistance to forward motion is in oppo-

What Does Happen When a Tennis Ball Hits the

Court?

Angle of rebound. When a tennis ball approaches the

court from directly above, the ball will rebound back

along the exact path of approach. This is in accordance

‘To every action there is an equal and opposite

reaction’.

The entire bottom of the tennis ball is depressed on

landing, the reaction of the court on the ball is in the exactly opposite direction and consequently the ball re-

bounds along the path of approach.

However, when a ball approaches at an angle to the

court, that section of the ball making contact with the

ground, is depressed more than the front. The rebound

therefore, will be forward and upward. With only slight

deviation, and considering a ball with no spin, the angle

of approach equals the angle of rebound.

Three factors which effect the rebound force and help

create a deviation in the angle of rebound:

(a) Elasticity of the tennis ball. This is evaluated

by the ball’s ability to return to its original shape after being flattened by the force of impact. If the

ball is soft and does not resume its original shape

instantly, some of the force is absorbed and re-

bound is slower and lower. Firm tennis balls

bounce from the court quicker and higher. A player

must recognise the fact that new balls taken mid-

way through the second set will be firmer and

therefore ‘livelier’ than the old ones.

(b) Court Surface. If the surface ‘gives’, the re-

bound is similarly affected as for soft, non-elastic

balls. A clay court absorbs much of the approach

force, therefore the rebound force will be much slower. A concrete court will have the reverse effect

and the rebound force will be greater.

(c) Ball Spin. All types of spin: topspin, back-

spin, sidespin and no spin at all have their varying

effects on the rebound of the ball.

The Effects of Spin on Landing (See Fig. 6)

Topspin. A ball with topspin pushes backwards against

the court and according to Newton’s third law, the

equal and opposite reaction of the ground against the

ball, is forward. This additional force, coupled with the forward momentum of the ball, causes it to rebound

lower, quicker and farther than normal. However, hard hit topspin shots, where the ball approaches the court with tremendous speed, rebound higher than slower

hit shots with little or no topspin. This occurs because

the ball speed and excessive topspin increases the air

pressure on top of the ball, causing the ball to drop

quickly. The ball therefore approaches the court closer

to the vertical angle, and as a result the rebound will

also be more vertical.

Backspin. A ball with backspin pushes forward against

the court and according to Newtown’s third law, the

court pushes back against the ball. This reaction being

opposite to the forward momentum of the ball, causes it

to rebound higher, slower and shorter than normal. No Spin. A ball with no spin rebounds from the court

with slight topspin since the friction of the court sur-

face on landing, stops the bottom of the bail, causing

the top of the ball to move faster. As mentioned earlier,

the angle of rebound is equal to the angle of approach. Sidespin. A ball moving anti-clockwise around a ver-

tical axis, for example a right-handed slice serve to the

forehand court, will rebound towards the left drawing

the receiver off the court. The right hand side of the

ball pushes forward against the court, in reaction the

rebound force acts against the right hand side. As a

result, the ball swerves towards the left. : For the same reasons, a ball moving clockwise around

a vertical axis, for example a left hander’s slice serve to

the backhand court, will rebound to the right drawing

the receiver off the court.

control is lost. For consistent, controlled ground shots

Combining forward motion with angular motion

Due to body weight transfer, all tennis shots combine

a forward component with a component produced by

the racket head swing.

Both these forces are integrated to obtain an efficient

result. The forward force either just precedes the

angular force or both occur simultaneously. For

example, the tennis serve consists of two forward forces:

(a) A slight body weight transfer from the rear

leg to the front leg. The body moving from a

vertical stance to a position of slight forward lean.

(b) Once the forward lean stage is taken up, the

legs extend vigorously (especially the front leg)

to propel the body upwards and forwards. This

helps initiate forward body momentum and also

helps attain height to ensure a high contact point.

As the body is being propelled upward and for-

ward the swing of the arm takes place. The racket

is virtually thrown at the ball. This force adds to

the forward momentum of the body. The net result ensures that the body is virtually

pulled and pushed smoothly into the court. There is

As mentioned earlier, owing to Newton’s third law of

action and opposite reaction, the racket must be firm at

impact if maximum force is to be applied to the ball. A

loose grip means that much of the possible forward

force is lost since the racket is pushed back. Therefore,

squeezing the grip on impact is advantageous. A tight wrist is important in all but four shots—Service, Over-

head, Drop or Dink shot, and drives which are hit where

contact point is behind the body. Much of the forward

force is also lost when the strings are too loose and

some of the force is dissipated by the ‘give’ as the ball

hits the racket. This is an advantage for beginning

players as the ball stays on the strings longer providing

more control.

Generally it applies that the heavier the racket, the

greater the possible forward momentum. However

since momentum is a product of mass and velocity, if

the racket is so heavy that it cannot be controlled, for-

ward racket speed will be lost and the resulting mo-

mentum will be decreased. Therefore, an ideal racket

weight is one which is as heavy as practicable taking

into account manoeuvrability.

2. Coaching Methodology, Class Control and Discipline

A Tennis Coach’s Responsibility to His Class

(a) The ‘coach’ is the key to a successful tennis pro-

gramme. The best programme in the world, on paper,

does not necessarily result in successful coaching. The coach/pupil relationship is a critical one. Can a coach

communicate with his class? Can he control them? Is

he keen, dynamic and inspiring?

A coach must accept the responsibility of formulating

the objectives for his course. Objectives are generally

defined as the traits or characteristics participants are

expected to have once they have completed a course.

Once formulated, the coach must realise that these

objectives are not going to simply jump off the paper

and stamp themselves onto the foreheads of his group. A great deal of social interaction, work and thought

is necessary on the coach’s part. He has the power to

make or break a programme through his leadership.

It is possible for a coach who has meagre facilities, but is keen and enthusiastic, to work in a straight-

forward programme and do better than a coach who

has an eleborate programme with superb facilities but

(c) Another of the coach’s responsibilities to his class

is to carefully consider his teaching methods. The

methods must be flexible since they will continually

depend on a variety of factors. For example:

(i) The specific student objectives attained for

each individual group from the size, age, and

ability of the class, as well as its particular needs

and interests.

(ii) Environmental influences such as: the number

of courts, the type and surface of the courts; addi-

tional teaching aids including backboards, adjoin-

ing grassed areas and ball machines; and the

general range of facilities and equipment available

or improvised.

Flexibility of method also concerns the coach’s atti-

tude towards new material. He must be willing to critically analyse new ideas and discard them if found

wanting, or accept them if valuable. It is up to each

coach to develop his own coaching philosophy. (d) A coach should not be satisfied to simply pass

on his particular tennis knowledge and skills to a class.

He should attempt to encourage a player’s thirst for

more knowledge and a greater understanding of the

game—he should develop players who will surpass him. This could be done through stimulating, thought-pro-

voking discussions.

(e) A coach must recognise the potential of his teach-

ing aids and teaching area, and use them to best advan-

tage. Adjoining grassed areas, fences, walls, chairs and

benches could all be incorporated into lessons. Chairs

for example, could be used for:

(1) Targets on the court. (ii) Obstacles in a zigzag relay run. (iii) Physical fitness exercises by stepping up and

down on them. A coach may use any equipment he can lay his hands

on, for the sake of incorporating interest and variety

into lessons. Ensuring that the use of the particular ob-

ject has a worthwhile purpose and is safe, imagination

is the only limit.

(f) A coach should ensure that his players have a

general tennis background by covering areas such as:

the history of tennis, court etiquette, fitness training,

tactics and sportsmanship. (g) A coach must arrive early for classes to re-check

the teaching area and equipment. He will still have time

then, should changes or adjustments be necessary. It is

not difficult for a well planned lesson to become a

shambles simply by arriving late.

(i) The tennis court gates may be locked requir-

(ii) The kind hearted curator may have gathered

Given ten to fifteen minutes’ grace, these difficulties

(h) Attempting to cover too much material in one

lesson, is a common mistake of programme planning.

Enough time must be allowed for skills practice. If a

coach finds himself with ten minutes of time left in

This allows flexibility since each class may respond a little differently. It is always a shame to stifle a spon-

the entire class time. It is better to prepare more than

the last five minutes. The lesson must not be allowed to

wander aimlessly and carry itself along.

(b) Player boredom is the most common cause of misbehaviour. Players must be kept interested during

4. Advanced Physical Fitness Training for Tennis

FACTORS A COACH MUST CONSIDER PRIOR

TO FORMULATING A PHYSICAL FITNESS

TRAINING PROGRAMME FOR ADVANCED TENNIS PLAYERS

A good coach has a number of factors to consider about the physical conditioning of his players.

Someone advertising to the public as a tennis coach

and expecting revenue for professional services rendered,

is deceiving his public if he has nothing more than a knowledge of basic tennis skills to offer. He must also

have a concept of a tennis piayer’s total needs during

a match in both skill and fitness areas. He must then

make his training sessions as specific and realistic as possible by relating them directly to the physical de-

mands on a tennis player. Which coach is doing more

to physically prepare his players for tennis:

@ one who simply sends his group off for a run

around the courts until tired; or

@ the one who seeks conscientiously to relate

skills and fitness to the game situation and en-

sures a realistic training session? Many people believe that running plays a valuable

part in the physical fitness training of tennis players. Following this concept further, it is generally felt that

a run of two miles would be acceptable, three even

better, and four fantastic. These people do noi realise

that fitness is specific and closely related to function,

and for this reason training must also be specific.

When is a tennis player ever required to run three

consecutive miles in the game situation? Would it not

be more specific to run dozens of short six-metre

sprints, interspersed with sharp changes of direction and

a few leaps and lunges?

Consistently running long distances will certainly

help in the conditioning of the cardiovascular system

but a gocd tennis coach must strive to be more specific

when preparing his players.

I first recognised the importance of specificity in physical fitness conditioning and its close relationship with function while visiting my cousin ‘Tortoise’. I had not seen him for almost five years and was recalling him being a rather lean fellow as I strode towards the front door of his house. As he appeared in the doorway I can remember thinking that he looked leaner than ever. I shook his hand and felt an immense pain shoot

up my forearm. It felt as though I had placed by hand

in a vice. ‘What have you got there,’ I asked, thinking

he was hiding some fancy electrical gadget. ‘Nothing’

he replied, as I began frisking his pockets for batteries

and looking closely for leads. How could a lean fellow

like ‘Tortoise’ almost put me in hospital?

I looked closely at his right hand and noticed that he

was the owner of the largest first dorsal interossei

muscle I had ever seen. (The muscle between the

thumb and index finger, when viewed from the back

of the hand.) After five minutes of jostling and un-

controlled laughter I learned that ‘Tortoise’ had com-

pleted his degree in industrial design and had pushed

a pencil ten hours a day for the past five years to earn his living. The intrinsic muscles of his right hand were

extremely well developed, particularly his first dorsal

interossei. He became particularly proud of this one

muscle.

When friends ask him to flex his muscles to show his

strength, instead of flexing his biceps, as most people

would do, “Tortoise’ would raise his hand, squeeze his

thumb and index finger together and up would pop this

famed first dorsal interossei. It was close to hen’s egg

size! Shouldn’t physical fitness training for tennis be

organised specific to the tennis players’ needs?

In the game situation, a player does not stand at the

baseline stroking leisurely, then run around feverishly

at the end to become fatigued. The performance of tennis skills and increasing physical strain are inter-

woven during a match. If a player is forced to prac-

tise under extreme pressure, with perspiration dripping

in his eyes, neuromuscular tremors in his legs, and is still expected to perform well, he experiences the clos-

ing moments of a big match. Is it a more valuable

physical fitness preparation then, for players to rally up

and down the court for an hour and then head off for

a two mile run, or to remain as close as possible to game skills while also undergoing advanced physical fitness

training? Should not physical fitness training be as

realistic to the game situation as possible? In this way it

will achieve the value of specificity.

There are a few situations of course, where advanced physical fitness training combined with skills practice

as described, should be avoided. Groups to avoid this

@ Players who are adapting to a change in court

surface.

@ Players pre-season just commencing _ their

stroke practices following a seasonal layoff.

@ Beginners who are still developing their strokes and have a low skill level. Skill practices should precede any fitness training. There is a certain degree of general physical fitness required before specific physical fitness work can be programmed for a tennis player. The story is often told of the concert violinist who has exceptional local endurance in his arm muscles. He is capable of playing for hours on end. Yet if his leg muscles do not have

sufficient strength to carry him up the steps and onto

By making a self-analysis and observing one’s own body movements, or from analysing a slow motion movie of

tennis strokes, the coach should be able to identify the

(Note.—however, the scope of this book does not allow for a detailed muscular analysis. A more compre-

abdominal muscles must therefore, be strengthened. What exercise helps to strengthen them? One beneficial

exercise is the performance of bent leg sit-ups, or sit-

ups performed with the knees slightly bent. The im-

portance of having the knees bent is to eliminate the use of hip flexor muscles in the movement. This helps to

isolate the movement to the abdominals.

(b) The forehand drive involves horizontal flexion of

the arm at the shoulder joint. The pectoralis major or

‘anterior chest’ muscle vigorously contracts during this

movement. Therefore, the pectoral muscles must be

strong. The following exercise helps to strengthen them:

performing push-ups, with arms wider than shoulder width. The arms must be greater than shoulder width

apart to eliminate, as much as possible, the use of the

triceps, or muscles at the back of the arm.

Rather than listing muscles used in the various tennis

strokes, it is more important to ascertain which muscles

contribute most to the movement. Also, which muscles

are contracting at the time of impact with the ball.

Take for example, a backhand groundstroke. About six

major muscles of the upper back and arm are involved.

Each muscle is used in varying degrees and comes into play at different times during the stroke.

An electromyographic study helps to answer some of

these questions. An electric probe helps to find the

‘motor point’ of a muscle. This is the point at which

a nerve enters a muscle. These points are relatively

consistent from one person to the next, so consequently

‘motor point charts’ have been drawn up and are avail-

able. Take the backhand again as an example. Elec-

trodes are placed on the motor points of the arm and

shoulder muscles that are used in the backhand. The

player then performs a backhand shot by hitting a

suspended ball. An electrical potential builds up when a

muscle contracts. The electrodes convey this electrical

potential to a graph making it possible to read which

muscles were contracting and how strongly. It will also

show which muscles were contracting at impact. For

electromyography equipment, see Fig. 107.

(a) Your own style of play, taking into account

(b) Your opponent’s style of play, taking into

(c) Your physical condition.

Hitting deep shots 2. Tennis, like most competitive sports, has the objective

of manoeuvering your opponent so that you have an

opening or a weakness to attack.

Hitting deep shots is an important method of forcing

your opponent to hit a lofted or short stroke off his

back foot, enabling you to capitalise on his weak re-

turn.

To hit a deep drive, a player’s shots must clear the

net by sixty to ninety cm (two to three feet) and be

forcefully stroked towards the base line. For beginners

who lack power in their stroking, distance can be a

problem, in which case, a slightly opened racket face ©

and a trajectory of forty-five degrees facilitates for

adequate distance. Deep shots keep your opponent near

the baseline with little chance of rushing the net.

A deep high ball will also give you more time to

recover if caught out of opposition. .

3. Never change the style of your play when winning. Always change a losing game. There are exceptions

however. If, for example, you just lose a set on one

service break, due to a couple of good winners from

your opponent, it is hardly worth changing your

natural style of play. If you lose the first set 6—1, then

you should re-evaluate your game approach to avoid

losing the second set just as easily. For example, a 6—1

service returns. Suppose he consistently hits the returns

short and angled, forcing you to spray these low returns

into the net or the fence. Obviously if the game contin-

ues in this vein, shower time is not far away. What can

you do to get back into the game? A possible solution

would be to occasionally stay at the baseline after serv-

ing and approach the net on your opponent’s service

return. This tactic may force your opponent to hit a

more forceful return which could lead to errors. May

as well try it, you have nothing to lose!

4. Develop a game so that you feel comfortable, both

at the net and at the baseline. This flexibility is im-

court surfaces. For this reason, players must recognise

5. Aim your shots. Do not be satisfied just to hit the ball back into play. Play your opponent’s weaknesses or hit towards court openings. Move your opponent around

him. Tennis matches are won by the player who hits

trying. Expect to win, and never give up.

DOUBLES Doubles is a much faster game than singles. It calls for

quicker reflexes and thinking as all four players often find themselyes outmanoeuvering each other while close to the net. Net play becomes all important in doubles.

It has been estimated that ninety per cent of shots in doubles are won at the net. The server moves into the net after serving. The receiver follows his return into

the net. As there is not as much court to cover per man in doubles, it is relatively easy to get to the net. Winning shots from the baseline becomes virtually imposs-

ible. With two net players covering roughly the same area as a singles court, successful passing shots from the baseline in doubles should be awarded gold medals.

The main aim in doubles then, should be to take up

a position close to the net, forcing your opponents to hit a lofted ball, which you can hit down. Once posi-

tioned at the net, many shots are wasted. Remember, you are there to force your opponents to hit the lofted yolley before resorting to it yourself. Never return a volley shoulder or hip high if you can hit the ball

down at your opponent’s feet. A volley hit in a horizontal plane needs only a racket head and a tight wrist in the path of the ball and the ball will speed back to you, often with more pace on it. A volley hit

in a downward or more vertical plane, must, if returned, follow on an upward path. This upward return by your opponent increases your chances ‘of ‘putting the

ball away’.

Some players employ the successful technique of volleying at the opponent’s right hip. It is extremely

awkward for a player to volley a ball travelling in this direction as it requires a stroke half way between a

forehand and a backhand.

Doubles is primarily a game of finesse, not power drives or blasting serves. Because passing your opponents becomes very difficult, it is wise to sacrifice a little power for the sake of accuracy and keeping the ball low.

Hitting as hard as possible encourages many errors and keeps the ball high over the net so that your

opponents achieve the chance to place a winning volley.

Doubles is a game of sharply angled shots or short slow slices. In fact. good doubles players play strokes with shorter backswings and often hit the ball on the

rise as they attempt to storm the net. Every shot must

be hit with the idea of taking control at the net. It is not wise to try for clean winners unless you have an

opening. Manoeuvering for openings or rising balls

takes special touch and great control.

Mixed doubles is often regarded as ‘the fun event’ of a

tournament. It is a time when the rigorous pressures

of singles and doubles can be forgotten and the game of

tennis enjoyed in a more relaxed atmosphere.

Potentially, mixed doubles has outstanding social

value. There are only a handful of sporting events where

both sexes participate on equal terms in friendly but

spirited competition. In tournament mixed doubles,

players play to enjoy the game and crowds enjoy watching women trade blows with top men in the hope

of seeing the so called ‘weaker sex’ outvolley or outfox

the male opponent.

However, many top players, both men and women will avoid entering the mixed doubles event when play-

ing a tough tournament schedule. They argue that the

extra hours of play may tax their physical reserves and

consequently affect their chances in the more prestigious

singles and doubles events.

Mixed doubles may be divided into two categories:

The social game. This category includes the family or

group of good friends who enjoy the exercise and in-

evitable merriment accompanying a mixed doubles set.

Under these circumstances the game is characterised by

soft serves and apologies for any clean winners hit. As

long as the foursome have had good fun and become

totally involved in the experience, it has been rewarding.

It is of little consequence to these aims, who serves first

or who takes all the smashes.

The tournament level. This category involves a basic

change from the social gare. The idea is still to com-

pete for enjoyment but with a much stronger emphasis

on winning. Therefore, tactics play a vital role at this

level. The tactical points presented in this chapter re-

lated to tournament level competition.

Winning tactics in mixed doubles are essentially the

same as those discussed under ‘Doubles Tactics’ in the

previous chapter.

Because of their more aggressive style, men usually play a more dominant role in mixed doubles. Assuming that the women are the weaker of the foursome

(this would be true in most matches) the game should

be played as if it were a game of regular doubles with

the realisation that there is a weaker player on each

team.

The man must verbally encourage his partner during

a match, so that she feels an important cog in the mixed

doubles machine. It is not reasonable for a man to com-

pletely dominate the game. He will tactically and physically run himself to a standstill if he does not allow

his partner to offer support.

To offer help effectively the woman must learn to

competently play the ‘doubles strokes’, particularly the

volley, smash and lob.

In the light of these comments the following mixed

doubles tactics are suggested to help win matches.

The male member of a mixed doubles team should

serve first (unless his partner has a stronger and more

reliable service) since the first server will have the

Opportunity to serve more times during the match. It

is also phychologically important to ‘getTMthe jump’ on

the opponents by winning the first few games.

However, it is also important for the woman to hold

her service. To do this, she must develop a deeply

placed, consistent first serve. This will allow her part-

ner to poach confidently and hopefully hit winning

volleys and smashes at the net. So often in mixed

doubles matches, the margin between victory and de-

feat is as narrow as losing the woman’s service game.

While the serving female is using all her wiles to win

her service games, the opposition must also realise that

this is the easiest chance for a service break. The maie

player should hit safe but forceful returns when the

opponent female is serving and direct them back to her. He should encourage his partner to hit wide cross court

returns or lob the opposing male net player.

If a female player is not able to hit effective serves,

her male partner should compensate by standing further

back from the net than is ordinary for a server’s part-

ner. This will position him closer to the probable de-

fensive shot, and thus he can be of greater assistance to

his partner.
